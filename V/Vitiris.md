``` tab [group1:Info]
::: magazinestyle
Vitiris is a British god whose name appears in many written forms. The spellings include Veteris, Veterus, Vitris, Vetus, Hveteris, and Hvitiris. The name is Latin in dress, yet it comes from no deity of the Roman or Greek world. More than fifty inscriptions record it, many from northern England, and more than forty altars survive. Many of those altars stand in Northumberland, and some bear carvings of a serpent and a boar. His worship ran strongest among men, above all the soldiers of the Roman occupation army. He was usually named in the plural, so he may have taken a tripartite form. Some scholars would see in him the horned god of north Britain.

One inscription at the Romano-British fort of Castra Exploratorum gives the name Mogons as an epithet of Vitiris. The fort sits near the western end of Hadrian's Wall. The same name stands alone as a separate deity at Voreda, the old fort at Penrith in Cumbria. The word seems related to a root that means great. His worshippers came from within the Celtic world and beyond it. No source states plainly what he represented, yet some images show him with a pig or a serpent. The finds cluster near the lands of the Brigantes and along Hadrian's Wall. Soldiers carried his cult along the Wall, and civilians took it up beside them. His plural invocation sets him apart from most named deities of the frontier. Students of triplism compare his plural naming with other triple cults.

Altar stones of this kind record the private hopes of common soldiers. The boar carving joins the pig and the serpent as beasts shown with him. Garrisons from the Rhine to the Tyne raised such stones through the second and third centuries. That spread marks him as a god of regiments rather than of one people. The many spellings suggest spoken forms the stones never settled. A plural god of soldiers suited a frontier where men served far from home.

:::
```
