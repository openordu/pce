``` tab [group1:Info]
::: magazinestyle
Iubdan is the fairy king of Irish tradition. He appears in the tale Echtra Fergusa maic Léti, the Adventure of Fergus mac Léti, a text known from the sixteenth century. For a king he suffers many humiliations. His court poet Eisirt sets out to deflate Iubdan's boasting. Eisirt tells the king that Ulster, the nearby land, holds giants. To prove his claim, Eisirt journeys to Ulster and brings back a dwarf named Aeda. Beside the tiny folk of the fairy court, Aeda looks huge. Eisirt then lays a demeaning geis upon Iubdan, a binding demand. The geis says Iubdan must go to Emain Macha, the seat of Fergus mac Léti, and be the first person there to eat breakfast porridge. As Eisirt foresaw, Iubdan reaches Emain Macha and topples into the porridge pot. His queen is Bebo, wife of Iubdan, named in the same thirteenth-century story. The account says the tiny king is hauled out of the pot and saved. Bebo wins the regard of King Fergus, who keeps her for a year and a day, and the fairy king buys their freedom with flying shoes. The bard Abcán also moves about Iubdan's court. Some readers find in the comic misfortunes of the boastful little king an early model for the Lilliput section of Gulliver's Travels. Other Irish tales treat Iubdan as a ruler of the fairies, a figure of wit and pleasure rather than of fear.

:::
```
