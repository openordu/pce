``` tab [group1:Info]
::: magazinestyle
Iona is a Scottish mythological site. It is now best known as the center of Celtic Christianity founded by Saint Columcille. The tiny island, 3.5 by 1.5 miles, sits southwest of Mull among the Inner Hebrides, a place of high renown in Scottish Gaelic tradition. A Celtic shrine to a goddess once stood there. The goddess Ioua gave the island its first name. She has been read as a moon divinity. Others connect the name to a word for yew, a tree of sacred places and death. The island holy well rose from the magic cauldron of the Tuatha Dé Danann. Its name, Tobar na h'oige, means fountain of youth. Those who looked into the well at dusk, at dawn, or on the night of the full moon saw visions and received healing. A Celtic site admitted both men and women. Under Christianity women, except nuns, could not live there. One of the island churches keeps a figure called a Sheela Na Gig. She is a pregnant woman shown exposing herself. Gaelic speakers call the island Eilean Ì, or simply Ì. The Sound of Iona separates it from the Island of Mull. Its position sits between Ireland and Gaelic Scotland. In 563 the Irish monk Colum Cille, also called Columba, founded a monastery there. He belonged to the Northern Uí Néill royal line and died in 597. The monastery became pivotal to the Celtic church. Iona helped Christianise the Picts in the later sixth century. It drove the second conversion of Anglo-Saxon Northumbria in 635. The monk Blathmac was martyred on Iona for refusing to reveal Colum Cille shrine. The abbey scribes kept one of the earliest written records in Britain. That record, the Iona Chronicle, spans about 686 to 740. Later Irish annals rest on it. Seventh century scholars fuelled the Easter controversy over the date of Easter. Iona lost that dispute, so its influence declined after the 664 meeting at Whitby. The Book of Kells, an illuminated gospel, probably began at Iona in the late eighth century. Vikings raided the island and took many treasures. A Benedictine monastery and nunnery began in 1203. The chapel of Saint Oran is a royal burial place. It holds many Scottish kings, four Irish kings, and eight Norwegian kings. The Iona Community rebuilt the old buildings in the mid twentieth century. The Church of Scotland consecrated the abbey in 1959.
```
