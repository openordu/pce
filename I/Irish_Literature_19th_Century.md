Irish Literature: 19th century here covers the decades from the Great Famine of the eighteen forties to the nineteen twenties. The famine years stand as the darkest point in the country's literary history, for Irish retreated sharply as a spoken tongue and its older verse lost formal energy. Even so, traditions persisted: popular song-poetry stayed alive though it grew more oral, looser in metre and given to stock phrasing. Manuscript copying continued, and writing gradually reached a popular audience, appearing in song collections and in Gaelic columns of journals such as The Nation and The Gael. The publisher John O'Daly worked from a Dublin bookshop to bring scribes, antiquarians and Young Ireland patriots together and printed collections of Munster poetry. Canon Ulick Bourke issued a grammar and helped found bodies that guarded the language. The true renewal came after 1893 with the Gaelic League, whose journal carried Fr Peadar Ó Laoghaire's Séadna, a folk tale retold in lively spoken Irish that delighted readers. The first Oireachtas festival met in 1897, and new prose and verse followed from writers such as Patrick Pearse, Pádraic Ó Conaire and Douglas Hyde.
