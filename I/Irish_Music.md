``` tab [group1:Info]
::: magazinestyle
Irish Music in its common use names the living body of practice known as Irish traditional music. Much of the repertoire is not Irish in origin, for sizable parts reached Ireland from Britain and Scotland in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries and from continental Europe during the nineteenth. The tradition passes down chiefly from ear to ear within families, stays conservative in taste, and remains largely a recreation rather than a paid art. Its oldest strata include laments or keenings, lullabies, and songs tied to ritual and to work. Ballads of the Fenian cycle, built around the hero Fionn mac Cumhaill and his son Oisin, reached their full growth in the sixteenth century. Much present-day dance music, such as jigs and marches, seems to descend from earlier pipe themes that include a piece marking a march. The first printed collection of traditional tunes was the Neal book of 1724, and Edward Bunting wrote down harp pieces heard at the 1792 meeting at Belfast. Later editors George Petrie and Captain Francis O'Neill recorded and spread the surviving dance music. The association Comhaltas Ceoltoiri Eireann, founded in 1952, has worked to foster and teach the music at home and abroad.
```
