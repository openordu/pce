::: magazinestyle
Irish Independence Movement names the campaign for national selfhood that unfolded mainly across the early decades of the twentieth century, roughly 1900 to 1923. It drew its spirit from Theobald Wolfe Tone and the Young Irelanders of the 1840s and rested upon the late-nineteenth-century parliamentary leadership of Charles Stewart Parnell. Around the turn of the century a new cultural pride took shape in caring for the Irish tongue and took shape in several bodies, including a secret Irish Republican Brotherhood, the Gaelic Athletic Association, and the language society Conradh na Gaeilge, along with the literary revival woven by Augusta Gregory and William Butler Yeats. Sinn Fein, founded in 1905 by Arthur Griffith, gathered the varied nationalist currents. Delay over Home Rule, the 1916 Easter Rising in Dublin, and the executions of its leaders stirred far wider support for the cause. In the 1918 general election Sinn Fein won 73 seats out of 105 and set up its own parliament, the first Dail Eireann. That body brought the Irish Volunteers under its wing, renaming them the army of the Republican. The Anglo-Irish War of 1919-21, fought by means of the guerrilla tactics of Michael Collins and the IRA, closed with the 1921 Treaty that granted dominion rule to the south, brought the Irish Free State into being, and kept the island divided. A civil war broke out in 1922 and drained the Free State before the youthful state finally steadied.
