``` tab [group1:Info]
::: magazinestyle
Irish Literature: Post-Classical runs from the Flight of the Earls in 1607 to the Great Famine of the 1840s. The departure of the leading native chiefs and the planting of Ulster ended the old Gaelic order. The loss of their patrons doomed the professional poets as a class, yet writing in Irish still flourished through the 1600s and 1700s. Poets of this age made verse in accentual and in freer syllabic metres. The Dominican friar Paedraigin Haicead stands out among the Irish poets of the 1600s for the range and force of his work. Others, such as Daiibhi O Bruadair, wrote laments and satires that mirrored the ruin of their world. The political vision poem called the aisling became a mark of the 1700s, where Ireland appears as a sorrowing woman and a Stuart king is awaited. Verse also took up religious and moral themes. In prose, the period produced religious and historical writing, including translations of the Bible made after the Reformation. Poets increasingly wrote in Irish for the common people rather than for grand lords, and the singing of the local bards marks the age. The vigour of the tradition faded toward the middle of the 1800s as the Famine took hold.

:::
```
