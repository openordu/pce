``` tab [group1:Info]
::: magazinestyle
Imram is an Irish literary term. The plural is imrama. It means voyage. Voyage tales tell how a hero travels to the Otherworld and what he meets there. Some have aspects of the aisling, or dream poem. A few contain elements of satire. Famous examples include the Voyage of Bran and the Voyage of Maelduin. In one a human is drawn from home by a fairy lover. In the other the hero meets frightening Otherworld women at sea. A related form is the adventure. It gives less weight to the journey and more to the goal.

MacKill reads the shape of the form. The word shuts the act of bending to the oar, and it stands at the head of a class of the old tales of the sea. In them a company rows a boat to a land beyond the reach, where the Otherworld waits in the isles of the far western deep. The song of such a tale puts the going itself at the middle, unlike the wonder-stories that only step to the place. The ride is a free choice of the men on board, while the long, unwished way comes from the sundering of the home. A set list of old catalogues counts seven of the class, of which only three have reached us, and the same hand that sorts them sets one well-known of them apart as a wonder story. The famous of them all tells of a rich, home-bound hero drawn by a silver branch and by a love of a holy woman, and it is among the oldest of the tongue.

:::
```
