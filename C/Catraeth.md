``` tab [group1:Info]
::: magazinestyle
Catraeth is the battlefield mourned in the poem Y Gododdin. Writers compare the name with the Latin cataracto, a word for a waterfall. The battlefield matches the crossing of the River Swale at Catterick Bridge. That spot sits roughly two and a half miles from Richmond in Yorkshire, to the southeast. The Gododdin people gave their name to this verse. The poem tells the fate of a small war band. The warriors rode to Catraeth to face a stronger foe. The text is a large work of early Welsh poetry. It holds more than one thousand lines of verse. Aneirin is the bard credited with the poem. The work records men and events from a poorly documented age. England and Wales were forming after Rome left. The Gododdin frames a relentless heroic vision. A hero pays for his mead with his own life. The phrase talu medd marks that same debt. A warrior repaid his lord for a life of ease. In return the bards keep his fame alive. The theme shapes all the later elegies in the work.

:::
```
