``` tab [group1:Info]
::: magazinestyle
A cosmogony is a mythological story that explains how the universe came into being and how it changed over time, often called a creation myth. A handful of ancient writers, among them Julius Caesar, attest that the Celts possessed such an account. Apart from a single sentence preserved by Strabo in the first century AD, which credits the Celts with a belief that the world can never be destroyed, almost nothing of that pagan scheme remains. Flood stories from Ireland and Wales nevertheless hint at a second making of the world after a great deluge. Fragments of an account can also be heard in the Gaelic speech of Cape Breton. One such piece is a story about the Milky Way, told by John N. MacNeil and edited with a translation by J. Shaw in Cape Breton's Magazine, issue 19, dated June 1978. A more doubtful witness is the body of lore set down by Llewelyn Siôn in the late sixteenth-century Welsh collection Barddas. Siôn's vision blends Christian elements with borrowings from gnostic thought and what look like Oriental sources. Scholars have dismissed Barddas as the private fantasy of a single eccentric writer. J. A. Williams ap Ithel nevertheless prepared the Barddas text and saw it through the press under the imprint of the Welsh Manuscripts Society as volume 7.

:::
```
