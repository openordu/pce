``` tab [group1:Info]
::: magazinestyle
Cormac mac Airt stands as the most famous king in early Irish tradition, and the first whose court is placed at Tara. The annals give him a reign of forty years with interruptions, from ad 227 to 266. He also carries the names Cormac Ulfhada, 'long beard', and Cormac Ua Cuinn, a form that honours his grandfather Conn Cétchathach.

Tradition marks his reign with wonders. Calves dropped after three months, every ridge yielded a sack of wheat, the rivers ran thick with salmon, and the cows gave more milk than the vessels of the land could hold. Most episodes of the Fenian Cycle fall within his years, and Fionn Mac Cumhaill sometimes serves in his soldiery.

His line runs from Conn Cétchathach through the son Art mac Cuinn. One tradition names the mother Achtan, a smith's daughter. Another names Étain, a smith's daughter as well, with whom Art begot Cormac before the battle of Mag Mucrama, the fight that killed Art. On Art's word the mother travelled toward Connacht so that Art's friend there could foster the child. She gave birth in a brushwood during a thunderstorm, and a wolf carried the infant away and suckled him in its lair. Cormac later passed into the household of Lugaid mac Con, and he displaced that king through the strength of his better judgement.

The sovereignty figure Medb Lethderg, half-red and no relation to Medb of Connacht, appears as the queen whose favour confirms a king of Tara. One tale holds that Cormac remains a lad until he has shared her bed. She is one shape of the Sovereignty of Ireland, and the feasts of Tara and the marriage of kingship stories circle the same theme. The tale Esnada Tige Buchet tells how Cormac begot Cairbre Lifechair upon Eithne Tháebfhota, daughter of Cathaír Mór.

The best-known tale about him is Echtrae Cormaic, the journey to the Land of Promise. A warrior offers the young king a magical bough that brings sleep, and the stranger later proves to be Manannán mac Lir. In payment the warrior demands Cormac's wife among other things, a price the king refuses. Cormac follows him into an otherworld castle, where a wondrous golden cup stands. The cup splits apart at every lie and mends itself again at each truth. Manannán at last reveals himself, and Cormac returns home with the cup.

His dealings with the Fianna stay warm for most of his years. Fionn feasts at his board, and the two stand close. In several versions he is Fionn's father-in-law through Ailbe Gruadbrecc or Gráinne. Gráinne's betrothal to Fionn carries his approval, yet when she flees with Diarmait he shows the lovers restrained sympathy. The bond cools between his son and heir Cairbre Lifechair and the Fenians.

Cormac's power sets his half-brother Nia over Connacht as king. Yet it cannot hold his own kingship once blemish touches him. His nephew or son Cellach violates a maiden of the Déisi. Angus, the Déisi chieftain, avenges her, spears Cellach, and strikes out Cormac's eye with a spear-butt. A maimed man cannot bear the high kingship, so Cormac yields the throne to Cairbre. Among the Dál nAraide of the north a different tale ran, in which their eponym Fiachu Araide drove Cormac from Tara, and Fiachu Muillethan of Munster helped to restore him. The lost-parents motif at Cormac's birth recurs in his Munster contemporary Fiachu Muillethan.

Later tales, coloured by Christian hands but alive in oral tradition, make Cormac oddly foreseeing the new faith. In one story he will not lie in pagan ground. Uí Néill families of medieval Ireland claimed descent from him. T. F. O'Rahilly argued in 1946 that Cormac was an idealized figure rather than the first Goidelic king at Tara. James Macpherson built a character named Cormac into Ossian in 1760. Tomás Ó Cathasaigh published the standard study of his heroic biography in 1977, and Andrew J. Offutt turned the persona into eight volumes of adventure fiction in the Cormac Mac Art run of books, 1979 to 1986.
```
