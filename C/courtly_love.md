``` tab [group1:Info]
::: magazinestyle
Courtly love was a late medieval literary pattern and a code of conduct across western Europe. A knight bound himself to the service of a noblewoman who was married, and whose husband could well be his own lord. He vowed himself to the lady and strove through brave deeds to find favour in her eyes. The couple were forbidden intimacy. The custom lived chiefly in song, sung by the troubadours and later by the trouvres of the north. Where the fashion began is much argued. Some writers trace it to Arabic love poetry, carried to the west through Spain or in the wake of the crusades. Others place its home in Provence, weaving it tight to the reverence for the Blessed Virgin and to the laws of chivalry. A few hold that it came from the continental Celts, founded on a legend of the Gaulish horse deity Epona. The fashion reached England via the wedding of Eleanor of Aquitaine to Henry II. Court poets in the service of the English crown then spread the style. Its signs appear among the Welsh court poets of the twelfth century, chiefly in the verse of Hywel ab Owain Gwynnedd and of his fellows. Later the far greater poet Dafydd ap Gil brought it to its height in the fourteenth century. In Ireland the love theme followed the coming of the Normans. A class of Irish love poems follows the Provencal model, though most that survive were written in later times.

:::
```
