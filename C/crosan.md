``` tab [group1:Info]
::: magazinestyle
Crosán was a title used in Irish for an entertainer. The word names a buffoon or a jester. The term appears in the Old, Middle, and Early Modern stages of Irish. Such a player delighted the crowd and also vexed it. The term springs from the Irish word cros. That word means cross. The root came into Irish from Latin crux. The cross stands as the sign of the Christian faith. The speaker gave a small ending to the root. The shape gave the idea of a little cross. Over time the sense turned toward the buffoon. Welsh speech formed a like word for a jester. Legal texts used the title for certain persons. A gloss in the tract Bretha Étiti places the crosán with the scholars. It sets the jester beside the shorn maidens. That grouping may mark the low rank of the monastery. A lexicon written in the year 1660 tied the word to the teachers. The forcetlaid held the third of seven teacher grades. A later scholar found the word on his own too. He gave the title the plain sense of criticism. Old song laid harsh light on the crosán. Two verse jests mock such a man. One write shows a stray of coppers and aged man. A second rhymes the errant and thieving jester. The man dances beside a fool and an aged cleric. The figure versed in verse and minstrelsy. His art praised music and honest women. It honoured the gift ring of nobles.

:::
```
