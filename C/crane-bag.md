``` tab [group1:Info]
::: magazinestyle
The crane bag, called corrbolg in Irish, ranks among the great treasures of Irish story. Its maker was Manannán mac Lir. The bag's origin lies in a jealous quarrel: a rival named Iuchra turned a woman called Aife into a crane, and Aife then lived for two hundred years under Manannán's roof. After her death Manannán kept the crane-skin and used it to store his dearest possessions. Among those objects were his own blade and tunic. To those he added the shears belonging to the Scottish king, a helmet that belonged to the king of Lochlainn, the bones of swine once owned by Assal, and a belt taken from the back of a great whale. When the sea rises, the bag's contents can be seen beneath the waves. When the tide falls, they vanish from sight. Modern scholars have guessed that the letters of the ogham alphabet, used for writing before Christian missionaries arrived, may once have rested inside this very bag. The same scholars note that the shape of the ogham marks may have been borrowed from the legs of a crane in flight. The crane bag changed hands many times. Among its keepers were Lug Lámfhota and Liath Luachra. Fionn mac Cumhaill won it from Liath Luachra and kept it thereafter.

:::
```
