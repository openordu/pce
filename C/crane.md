``` tab [group1:Info]
::: magazinestyle
The Celts saw birds as symbolic animals. They taught that birds reached into two worlds at once, sharing this trait with trees. The crane is a tall wading bird of the family Gruidae with a wide place in Celtic tradition. It lived in air, on land, and in the water, and that range made the bird especially magical. Cranes usually stood for the feminine force, most of all when they appeared in a group of three. The bird also struck people as a transformed human, most often a woman. A crane stands upright, so people imagined it had once been a person. In folklore the crane stands for bad will and miserliness. Cranes that stayed birds seemed mean and unpleasant. To see one spelled bad luck for a warrior headed into battle. Some images blur the crane with the egret, a bird of different meanings. Fionnuala and her brothers, the children of Lir, became swans through Aife's sorcery. Aife herself fell under a curse and took the shape of a crane. Manannan mac Lir appears in some tales as her husband. He used the skin of that crane form to make his crane bag. Midir of Bri Leith kept three cranes of his own. A lone crane dwells on Inishkea, an isle near Erris in Co. Mayo, until the end of time. Oral tradition also ties Fionn mac Cumhaill to cranes in several stories. Cursing called for a crane-like stance. The pose set a person on a single leg, with the other arm stretched out. The goddess Badb took this stance at the hostel of Da Derga. From that stance she laid a curse upon king Conaire, who had broken his sacred promises. An old Irish taboo forbade eating the meat of a crane. Julius Caesar wrote in the first century BC. He reported that the ancient Britons refused crane flesh. They feared the bird had once lived as a human being. Giraldus Cambrensis found the same refusal in twelfth-century Ireland. Carved cranes appear on Gaulish monuments raised to the god Esus. Celtic images of the bird reach back to the Urnfield period near 800 BC. Healing stood among the meanings the bird held for the Continental Celts.
```
