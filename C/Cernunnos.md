``` tab [group1:Info]
::: magazinestyle
Cernunnos was a Celtic god, one of the most ancient of the Celts. His name, in Latin, means the horned one. He ranked among the chief gods of the Continental Celts, perhaps the principal one. He stood as lord of nature, its animals, fruit, grain, and prosperity. His name and image appear among both continental and insular Celtic peoples. Both go back as far as the fourth century BCE. His name survives on a single inscription in France, and part of it is effaced there. The meaning, the horned one, comes from images rather than from language. Art gives him a man's body bearing the horns of a stag. This links him to the powers of the wildwood. He often squats. He bears the sacred torc, whether worn or held, a symbol of high status. The image of the horned god appears on the Gundestrup Cauldron. Over thirty further likenesses stretch from Romania to Ireland. Welsh and Irish literary tradition still carries traces of him. Deer sprout antlers in spring and shed them in autumn. This ties Cernunnos to the seasonal cycle. In nearly every image he appears with animals, both wild and tame. People called him the Master of Animals. He may stand for abundance and prosperity. He is usually shown with symbols of plenty, such as the cornucopia and the moneybag. No myths of his survive in writing. His image remains common today. In later illuminated manuscripts, horned figures call up devilish and anti-Christian forces. Many think the horned devil image came from Cernunnos. Korneli, a Breton pseudo-saint who guards horned creatures, keeps traces of him. Gaulish art pairs him with a ram-headed servant. Julius Caesar equated him with Dis Pater. Later writers connect him with Conall Cernach and with the Hindu Pashupati, lord of beasts. Some compare his squat with that of Buddha. It may only show how Celts sat on the floor, without chairs.

:::
```
