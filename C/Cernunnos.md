``` tab [group1:Info]
::: magazinestyle
Cernunnos was one of the most ancient gods of the Celts. His representative features are antlers or horns on the head, torcs around the neck, stags beside him, and ram-horned snakes in his company. Modern writers often call him the lord of the animals. The name Cernunnos comes down in only one ancient attestation. The one ancient text that names him is the Nautae Parisiaci monument, raised by the Gaulish sailors of the Parisi people. No other text names him. The famous image on the Gundestrup Cauldron shows him seated cross-legged. Some writers have called the pose a Buddha position or a lotus position. Koch rejects both terms as inaccurate, since they imply a link to south and east Asian traditions. The same cross-legged god appears on the rock carving of Val Camonica. Antlered goddesses are known from Clermont-Ferrand and Besançon. In Britain his image appears on a carving from Corinium, the Romano-British town now called Cirencester. A single coin from Petersfield in Hampshire also shows him. Cernunnos wears stag antlers, not the horns of a farm animal, which links him to the wildwood. Deer shed antlers each autumn and grow them again in spring, so the god follows the seasonal cycle. He often carries a torc, a mark of rank, and symbols of plenty such as the cornucopia. His myths do not survive. Many believe the horned devil figure of later art grew from his image.

:::
```
