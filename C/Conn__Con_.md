``` tab [group1:Info]
::: magazinestyle
Conn (Con) is a name that dozens of early Irish figures carry, most of them from powerful families, perhaps chosen in tribute to Conn Cétchathach. The name may grow out of a word for wisdom, sense, or reason. Welsh Pwyll offers a comparison. It may also stand as a pet form of cú or con, words for wolf or hound. One early bearer is Conn, a child of Lir and the twin of Fiachra in the sad tale of the Children of Lir. Another is Conn son of Febal, who ranks among the Fianna of Fionn mac Cumhaill. The record here turns to the fairy faith of the Celts and to the naming of Celtic figures. The fairy belief shows religious features. Legend says the fairies were once gods. The leprechaun, for one, carries the name of the god Lugh. Some scholars reject the link between folk figures and the old gods. Others find resonances between the two. The hero Ass of the tales appears in folklore as the giant Finn McCool. Celtic religion did not start in writing. It started in oral story. Transcribers spelled names in many ways, and later authors anglicised them for the English reader. That habit of respelling explains why this short name survives in so many shapes.

:::
```
