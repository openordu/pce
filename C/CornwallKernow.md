``` tab [group1:Info]
::: magazinestyle
Cornwall/Kernow, Kernow in its own tongue, sits as the southwesternmost part of Britain. It has long been a home of the Brythonic Celtic language Cornish. Around 1100 the tongue ran from the river Tamar to Land's End. Close to twenty thousand of the county's roughly twenty-one thousand people spoke it at that time. The centuries after saw Cornish give ground. It retreated westwards until only the far west kept it alive into the early modern period. MacKillop's Dictionary records that the last native speaker of Cornish died during the 1700s. By about 1800 the language was almost gone. Revivalists then worked to bring it back. Modern Cornish still tends to reflect English phonetics. Users urge a stress on the penultimate syllable, as in Welsh. Today the tongue is once more taught and spoken. Scholars such as Henry Jenner, R. Morton Nance, and others led the late nineteenth-century revival. A new institutional chapter came in 1927. That year saw the founding of the Gorseth Kernow, the Cornish gorsedd. The gathering was modelled on the Welsh institution that celebrates poetry, music, and culture. Its appearance in that year placed Cornwall in a family of revivals that also produced a Breton gorsedd in 1901. The Cornish also keep a strong emblem, the chough. The bird ties itself to the county's identity and carries the definite article as a local mark. It represents a pride in the culture that endured through the long decline of the language. Within the wider Celtic vocabulary MacKillop logs the headword kern. The form kerne sits beside it. Both are anglicizations of the underlying Celtic term ceithem.

:::
```
