``` tab [group1:Info]
::: magazinestyle
Connla (Conla, Conlaí, Conle) gathers a wide family of spellings. The full set also runs Conláech, Conlae, Conlai, Conlaoch, Conlán, Condla, and Cúnla. The name may mean great lord or great chief. Dozens of figures in Irish legend and history carry it, from characters of heroic narrative to churchmen. One Connla stands as the hero of a bawdy modern Irish ballad. The name looks kin to Colla. People sometimes mix it up with Conall, yet the two names are unrelated. The record gathers the Adventures, tales of men of this world pulled into the Otherworld. Three bearers of the name stand out. The first Connla is the son of the Amazon Aife in Scotland, fathered without her knowledge by Cúchulainn. His death at his father's hands is the tale Aided Óenfhir Aife. Gaelic tradition has him raised at Dunsgáthaich on the Isle of Skye. James Macpherson retells the story as Carthon in his Ossian of 1760. In W. B. Yeats's poem Cuchulain's Fight with the Sea of 1892 he carries the name Finmole. The second Connla is the son of Conn Cétchathach. A fairy woman seduces him in Echtrae Conli, the Adventure of Connla. Some titles add the epithets the Bold, the Fair, or the Red. He quits the land of the living because the fairy woman promises him a life outside the reach of age and death. Even his father's crown cannot win him back. James Cousins retold the tale in the play The Sleep of the King, staged in Dublin in 1902 and in Chicago in 1973. In the Adventure of Connla, a fairy woman draws the youth across. In the Adventure of Cormac, the king is gripped by enchantment music that sends him off in pursuit of a lover in that realm. In the Adventure of Laoghaire, the king works to free the spouse of an Otherworldly friend. A third Connla gives his name to Connla's Well.
```
