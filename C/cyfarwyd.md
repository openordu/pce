``` tab [group1:Info]
::: magazinestyle
Cyfarwyd is the Welsh word for a storyteller. The term links etymologically to knowledge, guidance, and perception. It descends from a Proto-Celtic root that means to know and to see. An early Welsh plural form gives the sense of guides. Those guides once traced the old boundary lines of a landholding. Later use of the word means tale or narrative. The word also carried a sense of spell or magic. MacKillop treats the word as the name for storytellers of medieval Wales. Every storyteller of the tradition remained anonymous except Bledri ap Cydifor. His name appears in Latin as Bledericus Walensis and as Bleherus. Giraldus Cambrensis names Bleddri among the famous storytellers of Wales. The profession had vanished before the Mabinogi reached its final oral form in the twelfth century. The Mabinogi stands as the culmination of this tradition. Its tales show the storyteller offering pleasing narratives to a patron. The storyteller belonged to the bardic order yet held a lower rank within it. He could take materials from other poets or from written sources. He did not need to depend on his own invention. Audiences prized above all his gift for dramatic presentation. Cognate Welsh terms point to wider duties. Cyfarwyddyd means guidance, instruction, and information. Cyfarwyddai means magician. Such kinship hints at other roles for the storyteller. Welsh bardic triads tie the poets to this body of lore. Sources say a cyfarwyd could entertain a royal household with his narratives after a meal. His stock of tales and his methods live on in the Mabinogi. The law texts do not list the storyteller among the officers of the royal court.

:::
```
