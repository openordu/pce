``` tab [group1:Info]
::: magazinestyle
Cnoc Sídhe Úna is a prominent hill in north County Tipperary, Ireland. The hill rises 699 feet and stands about two miles east of Ballingarry. Tradition makes the hill a home of the fairies and holds it sacred to Úna, their queen. Her name stays attached to the summit in the English forms Knockshegouna and Knockshigowna. The hill also carried older ritual life, and people once held Lughnasa activities on its slopes. A sídh in the broader Celtic sense is a fairy mound. Such mounds show across the Irish countryside as round and flat-capped earthen mounds of old. They were raised in the deep past to mark the grave or memory of a dead ruler. In long oral tradition the sídhe marked the spots. At those spots the host of the Tuatha Dé Danann retreated beneath the soil. The human Milesians had defeated them just before. The chief god the Dagda then portioned out one sídh to each warrior of that host, men and women alike. In older Irish manuscripts the sídh often looks less like another world than a lordly hall on earth. Named examples such as Brí Léith, Clettig, Femen, and Úamain read almost as earthly noble seats. Many hundreds of such mounds appear in the old literature. Each commonly carries the name of the strongest dweller within. Sidh Nechtain, for one, sits under the rule of the god Nechtan. Once defeated by the Milesians, the old gods took on the name áes side, the people of the fairy mound. They live unseen by most mortals for most of the year. The two chief nights when any mortal could see them were Samain and Midsummers Eve. Humans blessed with second sight could see them at other times as well. At times folk of the hidden world stepped over into the world of mortals. The woman of the sídh, called bean sidhe in Irish and banshee in English, wails by night. Her cry foretells a death. Cattle must not graze upon a sídh, and most country folk kept clear of both the mound and its paths. In Modern Irish the spelling sidh fuses into dozens of compounds, from fairy mist to fairy music. Cnoc Sídhe Úna stands as one named Irish example of this wider fairy-mound class.
```
