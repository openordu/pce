``` tab [group1:Info]
::: magazinestyle
The Cailleach is a goddess and a hag of Scotland and Ireland. Her name means the veiled one or the hooded one, and modern Gaelic still uses it for an old woman. Folk-tales make her older than the Celtic gods. She shaped the mountains, and the rocks fell from her apron. One tale has her dragging the land along on a straw rope. The rope parted, and the islands of Scariff and Deenish were left behind. A strike of her staff turned a bull into a stone island. She had one eye and a grey-blue skin. She guarded the wildwood and punished hunters who killed a beast with young. She granted the rule of the land to a chosen king. When the king held the hag in his arms she became a young girl, a sign of the land renewed. In Munster she was the Hag of Beare, and her second name, the cow, spoke of plenty. Winter obeyed her, and she gave way to Bride at the turn of spring. The poem Cailleach Bhéirre, or the old woman of Beare, is one of the finest works of early Irish verse. It probably dates from the late ninth century and holds thirty-four quatrains plus a later addition. An old woman contrasts the loneliness and privations of age with the joys of her youth. No one knows who composed it, a woman or a professional poet, and its female voice gives it deep power in the Irish tradition. The best manuscript copy opens with a prose introduction that was once separate. B. K. Martin read the poem as contempt for the world and found Latin influence in it. Proinsias Mac Cana saw its true subject as the conflict between Christianity and the world of pagan belief, a clash that left the pagan side conquered and poor. John Carey held that the old woman is Christian and looks toward death, not rebirth. The poet sets the decay of the human body against the endless renewal of nature, the sea flooding after ebb and the land bearing a new crop. The word brat, a cloak, appears in a few images, including the green cloak of the King spread over Drumain.
```
