``` tab [group1:Info]
::: magazinestyle
The Cailleach is a goddess and a hag from the tales of Scotland and Ireland. Her name means the veiled one and the hooded one. In modern Irish and Scottish Gaelic the word still names an old woman, often in a harsh sense. Old Irish caillech could also name a nun or a widow. She comes from a people who lived before the Celts. The Celts did not count her among their own gods. She shaped the high mountains. Folk say the rocks fell from her apron. Other tales have the rocks hurled at enemies. One tale has her hauling land behind a straw rope. The rope broke and left the islands of Scariff and Deenish. One strike of her staff turned a bull into a stone island. She had one eye, and her skin held a grey and blue hue. She guarded the wildwood and its deer. She punished a hunter who killed a beast with young. She gave the rule of the land to a chosen king. The king took the hag into his arms, and she became a young girl. The change showed the land renewed under a just king. The Irish name Cailleach Bhéirre fits this sovereignty figure better than any English tag. The English tags Nun, Hag, and Old Woman of Beare fall short. Her power reached into the harvest as well. Ireland and Gaelic Scotland both attach the name cailleach to the final sheaf of the harvest. Irish farmers race at harvest time. The fastest farmer claims the name corn maiden for his final sheaf. The laggard takes the cailleach as a reproach. Families kept the sheaf through the year. They gave part to the cattle and scattered part on the fields for a fertile year. Farm girls refused to tie the sheaf for fear of a life without a husband. Scottish workers tied the sheaf with a ribbon and kept it on a nail through winter. On the first ploughing day they gave the sheaf to the horse for luck. People on the Isle of Lewis dressed the cailleach as a woman. Her apron held bread, a sickle, and cheese. Wales and other European countries hold like customs for an old figure of the fields. In Munster they knew her as the Hag of Beare. Her second name, the cow, spoke of plenty. She held the year in her hands. Winter obeyed her, and the storms ran at her command. At the turning of spring she gave way to Bride. She kept her watch over the wilds and the beasts of the hunt.
```
