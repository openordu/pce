``` tab [group1:Info]
::: magazinestyle
Coligny is a village of the Ain department in Burgundy, eastern France. It lies about fourteen miles to the north-east of Bourg-en-Bresse. In 1897, near the settlement of the same name, came to light a large bronze calendar. Its fragments now lie in the Museum of Gallo-Roman Civilization in Lyon. The find is the best picture we have of the Celtic year. Craftsmen cut the calendar on a great bronze plate. It covers a span of five years and runs by the moon, not the sun. The months count twenty-nine or thirty days. The five-year plan adds two extra months, and this brings the moon back in step with the sun. Each day holds a small hole for a peg, so that the reader could mark the passing days. Much of the writing is in Gaulish, set out in Roman letters. The bronze plates rank among the longest texts that survive in any Continental Celtic tongue. They give rare proof of how the early Celts felt and split their time. The month names are the heart of it. One month takes its spring sense from the Irish Samhain and from the word for summer. Another month holds the seed of the word for winter. Both the year and its months fall into halves of fifteen days. The marks for luck and little luck, for the good and the bad, follow the days. The calendar is our only Celtic calendar, and the one calm look into how the old Celts counted time. Its year speaks of a people who divided their time by the moon. It also marks the great turning points of the sun. These fall at midwinter, at the start of the warmer months, and at the fall of the leaves.

:::
```
