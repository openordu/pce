::: magazinestyle
Cath Gabhra is a narrative from the Fenian Cycle. English readers call it the Battle of Gabhair or of Gowra. The tale sets the warrior band in a poor light. It also shows their strength collapsing with the death of Oscar. The high king Cairbre Lifechair sat on the Irish throne. This monarch, whose name recalls the river Liffey, refused to pay the tribute the Fianna demanded. At that time his daughter Sgiamh Sholais, whose name means beauty of light, was betrothed. Cairbre chose to die while ridding the country of the warband. He would not rule a land blighted by them. He set the closing fight in motion by killing Ferdia, a servant of Fionn mac Cumhaill. The deed pushed the Fianna into open war. Blood flowed at Gabhair. That ground matches the modern Garristown in north west Dublin. Some tellings place the clash near Skreen in County Meath. The pitched battle drew in every Fenian. The family of Sgiamh Sholais's suitor, a prince of the Déisi of Waterford, also fought. The bitter end came when Cairbre, in mortal pain, drove his javelin into Oscar's breast. Fionn wept then, the only time he grieved for any Fenian. A man named Aichlech slew the stricken leader. Oisin alone escaped the field. Some chronicles fix the battle in the year AD 284. A rival story of Cairbre's death names the fight Cnámross. The text found early print in the Transactions of the Ossianic Society. Translator Nicholas O'Kearney handled the opening volume of that series. He worked in Dublin in 1853. A French version followed, set down by H. d'Arbois de Jubainville, at Louvain in 1884.

:::
