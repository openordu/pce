``` tab [group1:Info]
::: magazinestyle
Cad Goddeu is a short and obscure Welsh poem of great antiquity. The verse survives in the Book of Taliesin, a thirteenth-century manuscript. English readers call it The Battle of the Trees. Some know it as The Army of the Trees. It tells of a war between Arawn, king of Annwfn, and Amaethon the ploughman. Their conflict sprang from Amaethon grabbing a white roebuck. He also took a young whelp and a lapwing. The magician Gwydion stands at the center of the poem. He wields a staff of enchantment to change trees into fighting men. Scholars still cannot explain the full purpose of the poem. Robert Graves rearranged the text to support his thesis. That reasoning appears in The White Goddess of 1948. Learned commentators scorn his views on Welsh verse. Celtic tradition itself holds no formal pantheon of deities. The very use of English god or goddess misreads what survives. The label implies a superhuman immortal who receives worship and rules human affairs. Surviving records do not match that picture. Scholars still call the Dagda the Good God and Cernunnos the Horned God by convention. Escaping death alone does not make a being divine. The Tuatha De Danann appear in the pseudo-history Lebor Gabala as immortals rather than gods. Continental Celts honoured figures such as Borvo or Glanis at healing springs. Unnamed powers behind Gaulish Apollo or Mercury likely received cult. Modern scholars more often look for a shadowed divine figure behind kings and warriors of heroic narrative. Earlier writers preferred to seek a historical original.

:::
```
