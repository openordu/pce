``` tab [group1:Info]
::: magazinestyle
Cauldrons rank among the most treasured objects in Irish and Welsh story. People used them widely across the Celtic nations from ancient into medieval times, and both dig sites and texts confirm it. Late Bronze Age examples from about 1000 to 500 BC survive across Ireland and Britain. Some were left as votive gifts, others buried at Hallstatt sites, and one rested in a princely grave at Hochdorf. The Gundestrup cauldron is the best-known survivor from ancient times. Craftsmen possibly made that ornate silver vessel in Gaul, and workers found it at Gundestrup in Denmark, the find that named it. MacKillop calls the vessel a possible key for reading both myth and literature. Cauldrons served domestic, sacrificial, and other ritual purposes. Ownership marked rank, because the vessel counted as a prestige possession held mostly by rich households. In story the cauldron stands for plenty and for rebirth, and it may imply powers over fertility. The Otherworld's ruler typically keeps one as dispenser of feasts. The bruidne, the feasting halls of Ireland, held inexhaustible cauldrons. Many commentators connect the Celtic cauldron with the Arthurian Grail. Famous vessels crowd the narratives. The Tuatha De Danann counted the Dagda's cauldron among their treasures. The cauldron of Gwigawd ranked among the treasures of Britain. The Partholonians of Brea owned the first cauldron in Ireland. Cuchulainn twice carried magic cauldrons home from Scotland. Branwen, the Mabinogi's second branch, features a cauldron of regeneration prominently. Bendigeidfran secures the vessel in Ireland and hands it to Matholwch, compensation for Efnisien's mischief. Ceridwen's cauldron grants wisdom, and its potion gives Gwion the knowledge that makes him Taliesin. In a related tale a cauldron of rebirth revives dead warriors. The chief words for the vessel in the Celtic tongues descend from one old root, the coire of Ireland and Scotland and the peir of medieval Welsh. In the tale of the raid on Tara, Cormac mac Airt is made to pass beneath the wooden fork that held a cauldron, a sign of submission. Some cauldrons read the truth of a matter by the boiling of their meat.
```
