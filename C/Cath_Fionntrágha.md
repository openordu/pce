::: magazinestyle
Cath Fionntrágha is an Irish title for a long narrative of the Fenian Cycle. English readers know the tale as the Battle of Ventry or the Battle of the White Strand. The work descends from a fifteenth century recension, yet references reach back to the twelfth century. The story tells how Dáire Donn, the king of the world, invaded Ireland. Fionn mac Cumhaill and the Fianna drove his host back to the sea. The fighting unfolded at Ventry Harbour in County Kerry. That site lies four miles west of Dingle. Commentators read Dáire Donn in many ways. Some take him for a late Roman emperor. Others make him master of the Holy Roman Empire. A few even name him the Emperor Charlemagne. The tale offers several reasons for the attack. Fionn had disgraced the French throne by seizing its queen and daughter. The king also wanted lands beyond those he already ruled. He had heard of Fionn's glory and wished to test the hero. The war never truly ends, and scholars call it the never-ending fight. Folklorists schedule it under motif Z12 and tale type 2300. These arrangements gather every Fenian skill into one great sweep. Oral retellings shift the details sharply. Some Irish versions make the invader king of the lands of the men of Lochlainn. A Scots version of 1524 calls him king of Norway. The work may spring from an earlier tale, Cath Trága Rudraigi. In that text a huge body of Norsemen lands at Dundrum Bay in Ireland. A fanciful word-lore links Rudraigi with the Irish term ruad for red. The echo makes a neat pairing with the white strand. The text has seen three editions. Boston issued one anonymously in 1856. Kuno Meyer brought out another at Oxford in 1885. Cecile O'Rahilly edited a third in Dublin, on the Rawlinson B487 pages, in 1962.
