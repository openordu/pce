``` tab [group1:Info]
::: magazinestyle
Cú Roí grounds the account of the captive Bláthnat. The poet Fercheirtne, who served Cú Roí, pushed Bláthnat over a cliff. Fercheirtne died in the very same act. Bláthnat had been held as a slave by Cú Roí. Later retellings often call her a traitor. They blame her for plotting his death. A Christian legend offers a softer end. In that version she turns to the faith and joins a convent instead of being killed. MacKillop opens with a swarm of spellings around the core name Cú Roí. Curroi, Cú Ruf, Cú Raoi, and Córroí all stand for the same figure. The name may mean hound of the plain, or else hound of god. Munster counts him among its heroes, yet no figure of early Irish narrative keeps readers guessing as he does. He may be divinity, king, chieftain, wizard, sorcerer, or traveller. His patronymic mac Dáiri hints at divine birth, because Dáire is another name for Bolg. By seven he already fought as a warrior. One hand carries an immense rock and the other an axe. Haughtiness made Ireland too small to hold him. Fir fer, the truth of men, is the honour code he follows. This makes him the severest judge of heroism. Quiggin once placed him at the centre of a lost Munster mythology. On a Scottish raid into the Otherworld, Cúchulainn gains his help while he wears the shape of an uncouth stranger. The pair carry off three cows of wonder, a cauldron, and the lady Bláithíne. When Cúchulainn will not split the spoils, Cú Roí takes the lot. He plants the Ulster hero in the ground to his armpits and shaves his hair off with a sword. In the beheading contest of Briccriu's Feast he appears as a bachlach, an ugly churl. Conall and Lóegaire land their blows, yet neither allows the return stroke. Cúchulainn alone bows his head for the reply, so the churl reveals himself and proclaims him champion. Medb dissuades him from a battle with Cúchulainn during the Táin bó Cuailnge. His death comes when his own wife Bláithíne betrays him. His poet Ferchertne avenges him by clasping Bláithíne and leaping from a sheer cliff on the Beare peninsula. Each night his fortress turns upon its axis, so no enemy finds its entrance after sunset. A spell turns it even while he travels in distant lands. Cúchulainn once helped to defend it by beating nine monstrous intruders. Opinion usually seats the fortress at the Iron Age ruin on Cahirconree. That crag rises in the Sliab Mis mountains near Tralee in Kerry. That place-name preserves his own. Early narrative calls it Cathair Chon-Raoi of Sliab Mis in the land of the Corcu Duibne. Some tales seat him at Temair Luachra, also in Kerry. His best-known son is Lugaid mac Con Roi, also called mac na Tri Con. The Clann Dedad follow him. Conganchnes mac Dedad and the warrior Lóch Mór may stand as his doubles. A Welsh figure in Culhwch ac Olwen appears as his counterpart. Renderings of that name run from Cubert son of Daere to Chubert map Dare to Corroi map Dayry. In Brittany Esdados le Roux keeps a fountain in Brocéliande and may stand as another counterpart. Maria Tymoczko's Dublin translation of 1981 carries Aided Chon Roi, the tragic death tale, into print.
```
