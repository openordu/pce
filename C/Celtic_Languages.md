``` tab [group1:Info]
::: magazinestyle
The Celtic languages are a subgroup of the Indo-European family. A shared set of sound changes defines the group. Proto-Celtic stands between Indo-European and the later Celtic tongues. Scholars split the family into Continental and Insular branches. Continental Celtic includes Gaulish, Lepontic, and Celtiberian. These ancient languages of the mainland have all died. Latin, Greek, and Germanic replaced them. Gaulish survives in near six hundred inscriptions. Insular Celtic holds the living languages today. It splits into Goidelic and Brythonic groups. Goidelic covers Irish and Scottish Gaelic. Brythonic covers Welsh, Breton, and Cornish. A known division is the P-Celtic and Q-Celtic split. The old sound kw became p in the P-Celtic group. Celtiberian kept the q for most of its history. The modern name came into use in the late 1500s. In antiquity speakers ranged from Turkey, home of the Galatians of St Paul's letters, and the Balkans across central and western Europe. They spread from the Danube basin to the British Isles and held lands in northern Italy and in the Iberian peninsula. The living Goidelic or Q-Celtic branch held Irish, Scottish Gaelic, and the now-extinct Manx. The Brythonic, Cymric, or P-Celtic branch held Welsh, the now-extinct Cornish, and Breton. The speakers came in many physical types. Some were short and dark, others tall and fair, whatever the stereotype says. The word Celt seems to come from the Greek keltoi, the name for a people who lived north of what today is Marseilles. Julius Caesar recorded that the Gauls named themselves Celtae. Old Irish and Old Welsh had no matching word for Celt. English gave 'Celtic language' its present meaning only in the early 18th century. Paul-Yves Pezron popularized that usage in 1706, and Edward Lhuyd established it in 1707.

:::
```
