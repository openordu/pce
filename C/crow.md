``` tab [group1:Info]
::: magazinestyle
The crow is a large and raucous bird dressed in glossy black plumage. Its genus is Corvus, and Celtic tales tie its kinds to war and death. The grey or hooded crow, the carrion crow, the rook, and the raven are not always told apart. Several figures take the crow's shape. Badb, Mórrígan, and Macha of Ireland were among them. Badb was a crow goddess, often seen as a carrion bird. She screamed over the battlefield, urging the fighters to bring her more fresh flesh. Sometimes she blended with the sorrowful banshee. She wept over battlefields and might predict death by wailing before a fight or washing the clothes of the dying. That task recalls the washer at the ford. Badb worked mainly as a prophet, and the tidings she gave were grim. At Mag Tuired, the last great battle for Ireland, she spoke of the bad years ahead rather than calm times. Mórrígan came as a hooded crow to perch on the dying Cúchulainn. Farmers in Ireland, up to recent times, dropped their work whenever they heard a crow cry, for the sound promised ruin. Cathubodua was a crow-goddess and an ancient patroness of battle on the Continent. Tribute stones to her stand in Haute-Savoie. The tribal name Boduogenos, meaning people of Bodua, points to her in Gaul. A great dark bird called Cornu, which may not even have been a crow, entered St Patrick's Purgatory. Names give the bird wide reach. The Welsh name Brân means crow. Fuinche is Irish for the scald-crow, and Morfran means the great crow. A crow killed in winter spills blood on the snow. The scene opens the black-white-red motif. It sends questing lovers such as Peredur and Conchobar on the road. Black-robed women and druids may have imitated Badb in battle. When the druids' school at Anglesey was destroyed, they stood aside and cried out to drive the fighters on. Whether Badb was Celtic or pre-Celtic in origin is debated. Scholars link her bird form to an unnamed Neolithic goddess shown in that form.
```
