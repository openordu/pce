``` tab [group1:Info]
::: magazinestyle
Ceridwen was a Welsh goddess. She made her home on an island within Lake Bala, in north Wales, on the water called Llyn Tegid. A shape-shifting keeper of wisdom, she owned a cauldron called Amen. It lay at the bottom of the lake. Her other names are Caridwen, Cerridwen, Cariadwen, Keridwen, and Kyrridwen. The second element, Welsh gwen, means white or blessed. Folk deemed her a witch, and many still see an unpleasant hag in her. Her mate, Tegid Voel or Tegid Foel, meaning the bald, lived there with her. They had two children, Creirwy and Morfran. Creirwy, as her name says, was lovely. Morfran, also called Afagddu, was odiously ugly, the plainest child in the world. To offset his looks, Ceridwen meant to give her son the gift of far sight. She boiled herbs in a secret brew meant to bestow wisdom. The mixture had to sit in her cauldron a year and a day. A lad named Gwion, or Gwion Bach, tended the pot. Three magical drops meant for Morfran fell instead on Gwion Bach, and he licked them away. He thus took the wisdom meant for Afagddu, gaining rare insight. Ceridwen gave chase in fury. Both changed shapes. Gwion shifted through hare, fish, and bird, and she answered as greyhound, otter, and hawk. Gwion at last became a kernel of wheat, and a hen form of Ceridwen swallowed it. In time Ceridwen bore a child and set him afloat on the sea. Nine months had passed since the grain went down. That child grew into the bard Taliesin. The Breton tale of Koadalan may echo this story.

:::
```
