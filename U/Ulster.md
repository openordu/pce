``` tab [group1:Info]
::: magazinestyle
Ulster is the northern province of Ireland, its name from the Irish Ulaid and Old Norse elements. The Norse gave the second element, staðir, meaning steadings. Medieval Latin coined Ultonia on the same base. The province fills much of the island's north-east. At 6,486 square miles it is the third largest of the four, beside Connacht, Leinster, and Munster. Its present borders were drawn in the seventeenth century. Pre-conquest Ireland knew it as Cóiced Ulad. In the older five-province count it stood among the five, with Meath counted separately or Munster counted as two. Its nine counties are Antrim, Armagh, and Cavan, then Donegal, Down, and Fermanagh, then Derry, Monaghan, and Tyrone. Partition in 1922 shrank the common use of the name to the six counties that stayed in the United Kingdom. That mistaken reading covers just 52.3 per cent of the province, or 3,393 square miles. Writers also use the name as an English equivalent of the Irish Ulaid. The ancient people of that name usually held only the eastern portions of the later province. The geographical focus of the Ulster Cycle is Emain Macha, an 18-acre hill-fort in Co. Armagh. Tradition seats King Conchobar mac Nessa there. Big stretches of the Táin Bó Cuailnge fall inside the province. The Ulstermen's fortunes ride on Cúchulainn, helped by Conall Cernach and Lóegaire Búadach. The Debility of the Ulstermen and the tale of their intoxication carry Ulster in their titles. The Red Hand of Ulster belongs with the Uí Néill record.

:::
```
