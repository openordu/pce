``` tab [group1:Info]
::: magazinestyle
Uisnech is a hill of 602 feet in Co. Westmeath, twelve miles west of Mullingar. Printed variants include Uisneach, Uisneagh, Ushnagh, Usna, Usnech, and the genitive Uisnig. The hill holds a great place in the Irish imagination. Generations read the hill as the island's centre or navel, so it carries the idea of the omphalos. A stone called Ail na Mirenn, the stone of divisions, stands on it. Marked lines on that stone show where the five provincial borders met. The five are Connacht, Leinster, Ulster, and Munster counted as two. Tradition placed most of the hill inside the Connacht share. Ceremonially, Uisnech ranked second only to Emain Macha. The summit is far from lofty, yet it shows from great distances. That visibility partly explains the continued burning of ritual fires there. The Lebor Gabála credits the Nemedian druid Mide with lighting the first fire. Mide gives his name to Meath. Excavation early in the twentieth century uncovered huge beds of ash. Literary and oral tradition alike favour the hill for Beltaine fires and for the rites of druids. The driving of cattle was one such rite. Tradition credits the legendary Tuathal Techtmar with founding its annual fair. That óenach lasted into early modern times, and the goddess Ériu also drew the credit. At its peak the fair ranked with Tailtiu and Tara among the three great festivals of Ireland. Lug Lámfhota met his death at Uisnech. The killers were the three sons of Cermat: Mac Cuill, Mac Cécht, and Mac Gréine. The hill is always a place, never a person. Even so, the Irish title of the Deirdre story names the sons of Uisnech. Those sons are Noísiu with his brothers Ainnle and Ardan. Macpherson built his character Usnoth on this hill.

:::
```
