``` tab [group1:Info]
::: magazinestyle
Uí Néill is the powerful dynasty named for Niall Noígiallach, the taker of the Nine Hostages. Its many divisions and members ruled Ireland for six centuries. That rule ran from the mid-fifth century until Clontarf in 1014. Niall may have fathered as many as fifteen sons. Eight of them established small kingdoms. Four lay in the Northern Uí Néill and four in the Southern Uí Néill. The northern branch gained ground at the cost of the Ulaid, who were driven east. O'Rahilly argued in 1946 that three sons were the Three Collas of tradition. The three were Eógan, Conall Gulban, and Énna. The Collas reportedly destroyed the Ulidian capital at Emain Macha. Tír Eógain and Tír Chonaill were the two great northern kingdoms. Their names mean the land of Eógan and the land of Conall. They deeply affected the course of Irish history. Diarmait mac Cerbaill established the southern branch. That branch held the Irish midlands nearer Tara, in today's counties of Meath, Westmeath, and Longford. The two branches kept up their hostility to the Eóganacht power further south. The two branches passed the high kingship between themselves for most of six centuries. The dynasty shaped historical writing and the growth of Christian institutions. Colum Cille belonged to the kindred and counted as a descendant of Niall. The Uí Néill grew identified with the province of Ulster. The province was in fact named for the much-diminished Ulaid. The kindred's heraldic emblem is a severed right hand, raised and red. Local knowledge of the emblem reaches back to De Burgo, Earl of Ulster. The red hand became a symbol for all Ulster in the early seventeenth century. King James created the Order of Baronets to fund the plantation of Ulster. Each baronetcy sold for one thousand pounds. The O'Neill crest was attached to the Order, then to the province, then to its Unionist population. Heraldry calls the design a dexter hand appaumé gules. Variations of the emblem appear in old Assyria, Egypt, and Rome. It also shows up in Scottish Gaelic family arms. The tales that identify the man who lost the hand are later fictions. They date from after the seventeenth century. In one tale a Viking leader severs his own hand and throws it to the strand. In the other a MacDonnell chief does the same to claim Irish land for his clan. The Red Hand serves also as the MacDonnell crest in Antrim. The name O'Neill does not anglicize Uí Néill. Many families descend from the kindred, among them the O'Dohertys, the O'Donnells, and the O'Hagans. The O'Neill family is one branch of Cenél Eógain inside Tír Eógain. It honours Niall Glúndub, who died in 919, as its name-father.
```
