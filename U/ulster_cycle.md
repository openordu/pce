``` tab [group1:Info]
::: magazinestyle
The Ulster Cycle is the dominant body of early Irish saga tale. It ranks as one of the four great groups of the old literature, beside the Mythological, Fenian, and Kings cycles. The cycle centres on the royal seat of Emain Machae and the heroes of the Ulaid in eastern Ulster. Conchobar mac Nessa reigns as king, and Cú Chulainn stands as the supreme champion. Deirdre appears as the peerless tragic lover. The great cattle raid Táin Bó Cuailnge anchors the whole complex and ranks as the only Irish prose epic. The stories show a proud, haughty folk, often at war with Connacht. Commentators set their world a hundred years before the birth of Christ. That age is older than any other European vernacular records.

The group was formerly called the Red Branch Cycle. Nineteenth-century translators and romantic nationalists favoured that English name. It renders the Irish Craebruad, one of Conchobar's three houses. That hall took its name from a great red roof beam. Scholars later showed that those commentators alone were confused. Early Ireland never divided literature into cycles. Scribes classed tales by type, such as the Aided, a sorrowful death, or the Tochmarc, a wooing. Not every Ulster story belongs to the group. The frenzy of Suibhne, for example, sits in the Cycle of Kings.

Scribes transcribed the material as early as the eighth century, and it stayed living literature into the eighteenth. The tradition spread as far as Scotland and Man. Learned pens, more than oral memory, carried the transmission. Although Christians wrote every surviving text, the setting stays pre-Christian. Magic fills the tales, and old divinities meddle in mortal affairs. The society holds slaves without censure and fights brutal wars, often over cattle. The ruins of Emain Machae stood visible to the redactors, as they stand today.

The cycle's fame does not rest on wealth or headcount. Ulster lacked the riches of the Boyne and Liffey regions to the south. Fenian tales reached a wider public. Literary Ulster is pastoral yet never idyllic. Its grandeur lies in extremes of bravery and love. Thurneysen listed near eighty items for the group, and later pieces push the total close to one hundred. Many tales survive only in heavily rewritten versions, and anonymous writers reshaped the material for centuries. Keating retold several stories in the seventeenth century. Nineteenth-century nationalists carried the heroes into English, and Thomas Moore sang of the Red Branch knights. Standish James O'Grady enlarged the history, and Lady Gregory, Yeats, and Synge adapted the tales. Studies by Bruford, Jackson, and Thurneysen remain standard.
```
