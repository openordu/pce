``` tab [group1:Info]
::: magazinestyle
Esus was a Gaulish god, also called Hesus. His name may mean strength, rapid motion, or anger. The Roman writer Lucan called him uncouth Esus of the barbarous altars. Druids read the future from the blood of victims hung in trees. Other authors tied Esus to Mercury and Mars. He served merchants and warriors. Altars show Esus with bulls and cranes or as a woodcutter. The Breton town of Essé and the people Esuvii carry his name. Personal names include Esugenos, the son of Esus, and Esunertus, strong as Esus. His victims hung in trees were hung and he cut wood. Koch's Paris stone in the carved wood of the bull ruled beside a tree.

MacKill casts him as a chief of old Gaul, read from the Latin writers and from the carved stone of the cult. The old list sets him with the Gaul gods Taranir and Touturt. The poet who first names him is doubted by some as a hand against the Gauls, yet his line cannot be set aside. He shows an untamed god of the rude altars. In his worship, men are held up in the trees and given a wound, and the priests who hold no names read the fall the blood. The later readers of the poem set him beside Mercury and beside the war-god. A stone of him as a woodcutter has drawn much guess, and one temple framed the signs of the bird, the wading life, and the crane. His faith was thought to sit on one coast, but a body found in a moss bed in the north in 1984 speaks to many who study him of a gift to this god far from his name. He is the leading god of the eastern sea people of Gaul, and the ground of a coast that keeps him held.

:::
```
