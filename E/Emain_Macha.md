``` tab [group1:Info]
::: magazinestyle
Emain Macha, today called Navan Fort, is a royal hill and the old capital of Ulster. The name keeps the goddess Macha. She was a goddess of rule and birth. A king forced her to run a great race. She gave birth at the end of it and died. Her parting words warned that the men of Ulster would be weak when they needed strength. The hill carries a long record of settlement. Near it stand the ring site of Haughey's Fort and the King's Stables. Both come from about one thousand years before the common era. Haughey's Fort served as a leading tribal centre in the Late Bronze Age. The King's Stables held a ritual pool, hollowed out in the same age. Its mud kept an animal body and the front of a young man's skull. The two formed one linked group of settlement and rite. By about nine hundred BC the centre of use moved away to Navan. By the year four hundred BC the people raised timber houses on the hilltop. They built figure-of-eight huts, with a small round house joined to a larger ring. A fenced walk led into the enclosure. A skull of a Barbary ape points to a gift from north Africa. It may have come along the ocean trade lanes of the Atlantic. About one hundred BC the people built a large outer bank and a ditch. They cleared the old huts and raised a single tall hall. Some two hundred and sixty-nine oak posts stood in a ring. The circle ran forty metres across, so it is named the Forty-Metre Structure. A tree-ring study dates the main post to the year ninety-five BC. The roof question stays open, but it seems a ritual building. No finds sat on the floor of the hall. Then the people buried it all on purpose. They packed the ring with stone and burnt the outer timbers. They capped the pile with an earth mound some five metres high. The dig later showed three more ringed walls and a triple ring. A ditched cut held an early medieval brooch. Down the hill the lake Loughnashade once spread over five acres. Now it covers just one, giving four bronze horns. These horns carry the La Tène style. Offering horns to water was a wide, old custom. The name of Macha still marks the land as the old home of the kings of Ulster. One use of the mound was to bury a great hall on the hill after its rite had run its course. The tale of Macha's grief binds the hill to the royal line. The seat of Armagh lies near, and its story stays yoked to the fort.

:::
```
