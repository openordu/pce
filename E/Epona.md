``` tab [group1:Info]
::: magazinestyle
Epona is a Celtic goddess of the horse. Her Gallo-Brittonic name, a P-Celtic form, means horse goddess. Koch 2006 notes that more inscriptions and images survive for her than for any other Celtic deity of the Roman Empire. Her worship stood strongest in central and eastern Gaul, along the Rhine and Danube frontier lands, and in northern Roman Britain. Soldiers honoured her, above all cavalry and units recruited in Gaul. Juvenal mentions her, yet the cult is known mainly from altars cut with a focus for offerings, and from images. Inscriptions call her dea, goddess, or regina, queen. An altar from Pförring in Bavaria couples her with the deities of the exercise ground, set up by the first ala of singulares. An altar found at Auchendavy on the Antonine Wall honours Mars, Minerva, the parade-ground goddesses, Hercules, Epona, and Victory. A bronze plaque from a donkey cart at Alesia bears the dedication of Satigenus, a Celtic name. Reliefs show her riding side-saddle, or astride among the Treveri, sometimes with a foal in the country of the Aedui. Enthroned images were common in the Rhine-Danube frontier zone. The white horse carved into the chalk at Uffington may reflect a related pre-Roman cult in Britain. The root of her name appears in Old Irish ech, horse, and in the Gaulish month EQVOS on the Coligny calendar, both from an Indo-European word for horse. Scholars set her beside Macha and Rhiannon, who share her horse ties, and beside the Welsh Mari Lwyd and the sovereignty myth.

:::
```
