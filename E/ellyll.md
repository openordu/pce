``` tab [group1:Info]
::: magazinestyle
ellyll, plural eltyllon, names a Welsh creature that can mean fiend, ghost, goblin, elf, or other-self. Folklorists picture it as a tiny diaphanous being from the Welsh fairy world. Among Celtic fairies it stands nearest to the Teutonic elf. It ranks below the tylwyth teg in stature. Its favoured fare is the toadstool, also called fairy butter. That fairy butter is a fungoid crust. It lines limestone crevices and clings beneath roots of rotting trees. Queen Mab holds dominion over the ellyllon. A telling example of magic-working appears in the Rowli Pugh story. Wirt Sikes recorded it near Cardiff in the mid-19th century. Rowli Pugh is a struggling farmer who believes a curse dogs his fields. His neighbours prosper while his own harvest fails. His cattle waste away. His spouse Catti suffers poor health. One small ellyll steps forward with aid. The ellyll asks only one thing of Rowli: leave a candle burning through the night. It asks one thing of Catti: keep the hearth swept clean. Rowli agrees, Catti agrees, and the ellyllon honour the bargain. Night after night the couple hear busy sounds below. By dawn every room sits tidy. Three winters pass this way. Crops swell, herds plump up, and the two farmers gain strength and colour. On a single evening curiosity overcomes Catti. She pads down to the kitchen and watches the unseen labour. Laughter overtakes her, and she extinguishes the candle. The ellyllon flee at once and never come back. Rowli and Catti, however, hold onto their recovered wellbeing through steady toil. Scholars usually treat the ellyll as a solitary fairy. The Rowli Pugh account appears in Wirt Sikes, British Goblins, London, 1880, between pages 13 and 17.

:::
```
