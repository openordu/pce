``` tab [group1:Info]
::: magazinestyle
each uisce is the wicked water-horse of Irish tradition. Its Scottish Gaelic counterpart is each uisge, and earlier English writers sometimes wrote aughisky for that name. Related beings appear in other Celtic traditions: the cabyll-ushtey and the glashtin of Manx lore, and the cefiyl dwfr of Welsh material. This creature dwells in the sea or in sizable calm lakes, never in rushing streams, and that habit separates it from the kelpie of quick rivers. Whatever shape it wears, the each uisce poses grave danger to people and gladly tricks them. Most often it shows itself as a glossy horse whose smooth coat invites the passer-by to climb on. The Irish form rises from the waves chiefly in November, the season of samáin, and races across sand or charges over open ground. A rider who takes the bridle gets a willing mount, provided the rider keeps the animal well clear of any sea. Once salt water comes in sight, the each uisce plunges in with the rider clinging to its back, and later eats the rider, leaving only the human liver behind, which drifts upward. A wild each uisce may also kill and eat ordinary cattle. Local memory credits Saint Féchíne of Fore, who died around 665, with forcing a specimen to drag his carriage after his own riding horse had perished. The Scottish variety strikes even greater fear, and may take the shape of an enormous bird or of a comely young man. Old stories from Tayside recount how a water-horse took the form of a dainty pony for a group of young girls near Aberfeldy, which lay in Perthshire before 1974. As the children climbed up, the back of the beast stretched out so that it could carry them all. The creature galloped back and forth over the stony ground, but none of the girls could fall off. By the following day their livers rose to the top of a small nearby lake. A blacksmith on the island of Raasay swore vengeance after one of these creatures killed his lovely daughter. With his son he drew the beast to him by offering a roasted sheep, and pinned it down with iron hooks until daylight. The each uisce is linked in some degree to the Welsh afanc, yet the reader must keep that being apart from the splendid lake horses that the hero Cúchulainn once caught and broke in. Cúchulainn sent those animals back to their mountain tarn by his own choice after they suffered mortal hurts. The Shetland version of the story bears the name shoopiltee. Folklorists catalogue this material under motif codes B184.1.3, F234.1.8, F420.5.2.1, and G303.3.3.1.3.
```
