``` tab [group1:Info]
::: magazinestyle
The Érainn hold a place in the record of Ireland. In the last centuries before the common era, a Celtic people reached the southwest of Ireland. The geographer Ptolemy called them the Iverni. Scholars call this people the Érainn. They were not the first settlers of the island. Those who built the Megalithic Civilization came first, and their names are unknown. They were not the last migrants either. The Lagin and Féni peoples came after them. Some scholars trace the Fomorians and the Fir Bolg to these early people.

MacKillop dates the first Érainn arrival at perhaps the 5th century BC. He centers them in what is now County Cork. He identifies them with the Iverni, listed by Ptolemy in the 2nd century AD. Ptolemy drew that name from an Érainn branch called the Corcu Loigde. Modern scholarship no longer treats the Érainn as the aboriginal people of Ireland. The current view holds that the Cruithni (the Picts) preceded them. The Lagin and Féni followed.

T. F. O'Rahilly set out contested theses about the Érainn in his 1946 work Early Irish History and Mythology. He classed the Érainn among the P-Celtic peoples. Their arrival, on his reading, came before the Q-Celtic Goidels who came to dominate the island. He identified the Érainn with the Belgae. The Belgae, on his account, left the Low Countries, crossed Britain, and reached Ireland. In his reading, the mythical Nemedians and Fir Bolg of pseudo-history rest on Érainn memory. The Lebor Gabala, the Book of Invasions, places them there. He further read Togail Bruidne Da Derga as preserving the recollection of a defeat dealt by the Lagin. The title means the Destruction of Da Derga Hostel. Many scholars still doubt the P-Celtic link, and so the British link, yet O'Rahilly's views stand.

MacKillop lists smaller groups as actual subdivisions of the Érainn. They include the Corcu Loigde already named. They include the Corcu Duibne of Kerry. They include the Déisi of Waterford. They include the Múscraige (Muskerry). They include the Osraige (Ossory), who allied with the Q-Celtic Eóganacht. In the north, the Dál Riada, who colonized Gaelic Scotland, also counted as Érainn. So did the Ulaid, who gave Ulster its name. Dáire mac Dedad stood as the principal ancestor-deity of the Érainn. Mór Muman served as the territorial goddess. She was, by appearance, the Great One of Munster. She was, by origin, an Érainn goddess before all else.
```
