``` tab [group1:Info]
::: magazinestyle
Eadwine, also known as Edwin, was king of Northumbria in the early seventh century, a ruler of the Deirian line. He had been driven out of the north by his rival Æthelfrith, yet he returned to become a powerful king. According to Beda in his Historia Ecclesiastica, at the height of his power he exacted tribute from the Isle of Anglesey (Môn) and from the Manx island (Ellan Vannin), and he made himself overlord of the kings of Gwynedd, Cadfan and his son Cadwallon. Eadwine holds an important place in the history of Celtic Britain, because his rule reached into Welsh lands and his downfall came at the hands of Welsh forces. The Welsh poem Moliant Cadwallon ('In Praise of Cadwallon') calls him Tad rwy tuylluras, 'a father of excessively great deception,' and notes that, as a Deiran, he lacked a legitimate hereditary claim to Brynaich in the eyes of the Gwynedd court. He lost his life on 12 October 633 at the battle of Meicen (Old English Haethfelth, Hatfield Chase), where Cadwallon of Gwynedd defeated him and killed his sons Osfrid and Eadfird as well; his line died in that battle. A Welsh poem, Gofara Braint, claims that his head was carried to Cadwallon's court at Aberffraw.
```
