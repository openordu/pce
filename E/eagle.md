``` tab [group1:Info]
::: magazinestyle
The eagle is a symbolic bird. MacKillop notes that this large diurnal bird of prey plays a surprisingly small part in Celtic myth and art beside other European traditions. Gaulish examples appear copied from Roman models. Yet Irish and Welsh tradition counts the eagle among the oldest of animals. A Scottish Gaelic phrase calls it the veritable bird. The one-eyed salmon of wisdom could appear as Fintan mac Bóchra. That ancient invader shifted into many forms, the eagle among them. In British legend a magic pig named Henwen bore eaglets and also made bees and wolves. Only the hero Lleu Llaw Gyffes took the eagle for himself. He changed into it after his wife murdered him. MacKillop adds Taliesin and Tuan mac Cairill as heroes who wore the same shape. These changes often ran in a series, and a shift into a salmon usually followed the eagle stage. Culhwch consulted the ancient eagle of Gwernabwy during his search for Mabon. In Irish folklore the hawk of Achill tricked an eagle. It sent the bird on a fruitless errand and ate the eagle's chicks while it was away. The bird sometimes marked oracles or came as an omen of things to come. A shape-shifting magician or bard could also wear the eagle's form. In Wales the eagles of Snowdonia acted as oracles of peace and war. Flying high signalled victory. Flying low and crying without pause implied defeat for the Welsh. People who ate eagle flesh gave their heirs strong psychic powers. These lasted to the ninth generation. Later tradition hands the bird several roles: emblem of Saint John the Evangelist, zodiac Scorpio, or the water element among the classical four. Irish and Scottish Gaelic folklore holds that Adam and Eve live on as eagles.

:::
```
