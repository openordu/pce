``` tab [group1:Info]
::: magazinestyle
Éremón is an Irish hero. His other names are Heremon and Eremon. The Book of Invasions calls the Milesians the last wave of settlers. They were the men of Mil. Éremón led the attack with his brother Eber Finn. The two fought the magical host of the old gods. They won control of the island. The myth may hold a memory of a real Celtic arrival. Those settlers took rule from the older tribes. After the win, the brothers argued about the rule. The poet Amairgin settled the land in two. He drew a line on the low glacial hills. The line runs from present-day Dublin to Galway. This strip is the Eiscir Riada. Éremón took the north. Eber Finn held the south. Eber Finn stayed angry. He opened a long war. Éremón had to end his brother's life. Éremón then ruled a united Ireland. He founded the kingship at Tara. He gave the sacred hill the name of his wife Tea.

MacKillop ties Éremón to the close of his rule. From the victory he raises his seat at the hill of the kingship. He gives that place his wife's own name. In one old reading, the man who joins the island sits next to David of the Bible. A medieval gloss placed him there. A newer student of the old gods, Georges Dumézil, reads the same record with a cooler eye. The north man takes the whole land and the whole crown. The map of power bends to his side. The old name, under many spellings, was then worn by later rulers. They set their lines back to this first of the line. So the one hero opens nearly all the centuries of Irish high story.

MacKillop lists Éremón also as Erimón, Éireamhóin, and Erem. He marks him as the first Milesian (that is, Goidelic) king of Ireland. He ties his capital at Tara to the Lebor Gabala. The book names him one of the two chief sons of Míl Espáine, set beside Eber Finn. In the Milesian conquest he stands out at a battle. The battle, fought at Tailtiu, broke the power of the host. The fight is told in Altrom Tige Dá Medar. After the battle he falls into dispute with Eber Finn, his brother and ally. The poet Amairgin settles the partition. He draws the border along the Eiscir Riada, a long ridge of mounds from Galway Bay up to Dublin. Six chiefs stand with Eber in the south. Seven stand with Éremón in the north. Eber, displeased with the share, attacks Éremón and dies by Éremón hand. Éremón rules the whole island alone. The feud runs on through their descendants. He sets his capital on the hill of Temair (Tara). The hill takes the name of his wife Téa. The name, in different spellings, sat on later petty kings and noblemen who traced their lines back to him. So many pedigrees and genealogies cited him.

Medieval commentators of an exegetical bent compared Éremón to King David of Judea. They did so on the strength of his union of the island. Modern scholars, above all those shaped by Georges Dumézil (1898–1986), place weight elsewhere. They note that Éremón, as the north man, holds both the entire island and the kingship. The argument runs in Dumézil's Le Troisième Souverain (Paris, 1949), pages 167–86.
```
