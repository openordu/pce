``` tab [group1:Info]
::: magazinestyle
Nudd, also called Lludd, is a British god. In the Roman period a large shrine stood for a god named Nodens. Scholars believe Nodens is the same being as the Welsh Nudd or Lludd. Koch records Nodens from inscriptions in England, one at Cockersand Moss in Lancashire and a further three at Lydney Park in Gloucestershire. At Lydney the god was equated with the Roman Mars. The temple sat on a hilltop in a pre-Roman hill fort above the Severn estuary. It was a rich Romano-Celtic shrine and bath complex built in the later fourth century, a time when Christianity was already the state religion. Bronze plaques of fishermen, sea monsters, and other marine beings mark Nodens as a water god. The Irish counterpart in name is Nuadu, genitive Nuadat. In the early Middle Ages Nuadu was a common Irish personal name. In Irish legend Nuadu is a leader of the Tuath De. In the tale Cath Maige Tuired he lost an arm in battle with the Fir Bolg, and this loss cost him the kingship. The physician Dian Cecht made him a new arm of silver, so he gained the name Argatlam, 'silver hand'. Irish genealogies name Nuadu as a forefather of Finn mac Cumaill. The Welsh forms Nudd and Lludd both descend from the old name Nodens. Lludd Llaw Ereint, 'Lludd of the Silver Hand', is the Welsh match for Nuadu of the Silver Hand. In the Welsh prose story of Lludd and brother Llefelys, Lludd is king of Britain, and his brother Llefelys helps him beat three supernatural oppressions. Gwyn ap Nudd appears in Welsh lore as a king of the otherworld Annwfn, and in the Life of Saint Collen he holds a wondrous castle near Glastonbury. Nudd also reads as a god of the river mouth and estuary.
```
