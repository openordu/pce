``` tab [group1:Info]
::: magazinestyle
Nudd, also called Lludd, is a British god. Welsh tradition ranks him among Wales' three most generous men, beside Rhydderch Hael and Mordaf Hael. The Triads carry that ranking. Genealogies of the early period treat him as a man of history. Yet the hero appears to descend from a god. MacKillop derives the legendary figure from Nodons, a god worshipped at Lydney Park. He reads Nudd as cognate of Nuadu Airgetlám, the divine king of Ireland. MacKillop names Edem and Gwyn ap Nudd as sons of Nudd.

In the Roman period a large shrine stood for a god named Nodens. Scholars believe Nodens is the same being as the Welsh Nudd or Lludd. Koch records Nodens from inscriptions in England. One site is Cockersand Moss in Lancashire. A further three stand at Lydney Park in Gloucestershire. At Lydney the god was equated with the Roman Mars. The temple sat on a hilltop in a pre-Roman hill fort above the Severn estuary. It was a rich Romano-Celtic shrine and bath complex. The building dates to the later fourth century. At that time Christianity was already the state religion. Bronze plaques of fishermen, ichthyocentaurs, sea monsters, and other marine beings mark Nodens as a water god.

The Irish counterpart in name is Nuadu, genitive Nuadat. A late ogam inscription attests this name. In the early medieval period, Nuadu was a name carried by many ordinary Irishmen. It also served as the name of the head copyist of the Cambridge Juvencus manuscript. That man was an Irish scholar at work in Wales around the year 900. In Irish legend Nuadu is a leader of the Tuath De. In the tale Cath Maige Tuired he lost an arm in battle with the Fir Bolg. This loss cost him the kingship. The physician Dian Cecht made him a new arm of silver, so he gained the name Argatlam, 'silver hand'. Early tradition equates Nuadu with Nechtan, an aquatic god, and the genealogical poem Nuadu Necht joins the two names. Irish genealogies name Nuadu as a forefather of Finn mac Cumaill.

The Welsh forms Nudd and Lludd both descend from the old name Nodens. Lludd Llaw Ereint, 'Lludd of the Silver Hand', is the Welsh match for Nuadu of the Silver Hand. In the Welsh prose story of Lludd and brother Llefelys, Lludd is king of Britain. His brother Llefelys helps him beat three supernatural oppressions. Gwyn ap Nudd appears in Welsh lore as a king of the otherworld Annwfn. In the Life of Saint Collen he holds a wondrous castle near Glastonbury. Nudd also reads as a god of the river mouth and estuary.

The double name puzzles students. The form Lludd appears to grow from alliterative pull between Nudd and the epithet Llaw Ereint. That pull slides the name from Nudd toward Lludd. Because of it, readers struggle to divide this Nudd from other men named Lludd. The best known rival is Beli Mawr's son in the story Cyfranc Lludd a Llefelys.
```
