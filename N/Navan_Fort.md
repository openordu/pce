``` tab [group1:Info]
::: magazinestyle
Navan Fort is the name of a great ancient hill-fort in Ulster. The old Irish world knew the site more fully as Emain Macha. The mound stands at Navan, two miles west of Armagh in Northern Ireland. The place covers an area of about eighteen acres at its heart. This was the royal seat of the king Conchobar mac Nessa. Emain Macha served as the capital of the Ulaid in their age. It ranks among the most cited place names in early Irish writing. In the Ulster Cycle it stands as the mythical capital of Ulster. An immense circular bank and a ditch enclose many earthworks. At the top rises a univallate mound of earth and stone. That summit once held a dwelling and later served ritual ends. Excavation from 1963 to 1971 laid the story of the site open. Workers found that the round house began around 700 before our era. That building was rebuilt nine times down to about one hundred years bce. The surrounding stockade likewise changed shape six different times. Scholars link the fort with the Isamnion in Ptolemy's old maps. That link ties the site to the wider Roman world of its day. The discovery of a Barbary ape skull points to long-distance trade. It hints that news of the fort reached far beyond the Irish coast. The residence came to a violent end in the stories of the Collas. Three brothers from rival Ulster kin are said to have ravaged it. That ruin came before Christian missionaries reached the island. The last king to rule there was Fergus Foga in the older accounts. Even after its fall the old hill-fort kept a public role. It remained the ground for an annual feast through medieval times. Today the site stands as the chief example of an Irish royal capital. Its long story joins the real earthwork and the mythic palace. Irish scholars identify the two names without any modern doubt. The archaeology matches every older claim about the royal seat. A visitor today sees the grass ring that once held a king's hall. The mound still draws those who study early Irish kingship.

:::
```
