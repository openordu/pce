::: magazinestyle
Neo-Druidism is the modern revival of the ancient druidic tradition. Many current movements trace their roots to the druidic revival of the 18th century. The Ancient Order of Druids spread widely in the 19th century. This order reached America, Canada, Australia, and Europe. The Golden Dawn movement also left an influence. It shaped some branches of modern druidry. The later 20th century saw great growth of new druid groups. These groups appeared in Britain, Ireland, Europe, America, Australia, and New Zealand. This growth went with the rise of Paganism and alternative spirituality. Practitioners often consult Greek accounts for guidance. They respect learning and the oak, mistletoe, and grove in their ritual. Human sacrifice is a controversial topic. Some modern druids reject ancient accounts of sacrifice as slurs by outsiders. Others argue that a willing victim received a great honour. The old druid is sometimes compared with the Brahmin of Hinduism. The ritual year follows the Celtic eightfold calendar. Summer and winter solstice are treated as especially important. The link between druidry and Stonehenge goes back to the 17th century. Aubrey first made the link, and Stukeley reinforced it later. Around 14,500 people came to Stonehenge for the summer solstice in 2001. Some believers hold that druids built the stone circle. Others say the build predates the historical druids. New circles are still built for ritual use. Druidry takes much from the bardic orders of Wales and Cornwall. Groups gather in stone circles and wear long white robes. Some recite the Gorsedd Prayer written by Edward Williams. Modern druidry is a diverse, eclectic, and flourishing movement.

:::
