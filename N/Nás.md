``` tab [group1:Info]
::: magazinestyle
Nás is an Irish goddess remembered as a wife of the radiant god Lug Lámfhota. Her name also appears in the spellings Naas and Nass. Tradition gives that god four wives, and Nás stands among them. Received lore credits Lug with every art. It sets him over the Tuatha Dé Danann as their king. A goddess joined to such a figure carried weight in the tales of medieval Ireland. Irish tales often pair gods with more than one consort, and the lists of partners vary between sources.

Folk invention later claimed the town of Naas as the scene of her birth or burial. The town lies west of Dublin in County Kildare, within the old kingdom of Leinster. Its Irish title, Nos na Riogh, marks it as the place where kings held assembly. The title still points to that role and pictures a town built for gathering rather than for war. The name thus reads as a memorial, as if the whole town kept the goddess in view. MacKillop flags those birth and burial claims as invention by storytellers rather than settled doctrine. Irish tradition often frames a place as the grave of the figure who lent it a name. Naas follows that pattern. Place-name lore of this kind gave communities a way to anchor their gods in the landscape they farmed.

The record adds firmer ground beneath the legend. Naas itself stood as a royal seat of the Leinster kings until the tenth century. It served them as a home and as a gathering ground for their great assemblies. At such gatherings the men of the province heard judgments, settled disputes, and renewed their loyalty to their rulers. A visitor to Naas therefore walked through a working centre of kingship, not only a stage for legend. The folk tale and the political record meet in one site, and each explains a different layer of Naas.

A closing note: the earlier base text for this entry recounted the settlement of Ireland by the Fir Bolg. That material arose from a homograph slip and belongs to another subject, so this entry leaves it aside.
```
