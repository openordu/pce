``` tab [group1:Info]
::: magazinestyle
Nuadu Airgetlám, of the silver hand or arm, is the king of the mythical Tuatha De Danann who brings his people into Ireland and then forfeits the kingship over the blemish of his severed arm, as MacKillop 2008 narrates. Dispute over the succession fills the opening action of Cath Maige Tuired, the tale of that second great fight on the plain of towers. The conception is older than the Mythological and Fenian Cycles in which he appears, and he takes markedly different aspects across the tradition. His early British cognate is Nodons of Lydney Park, and his likeness to the Welsh Nudd, who carries the epithet Llaw Ereint, silver hand, is unmistakable. In the oldest Irish material Nuadu stands beside the Boyne as consort of the river's eponym Boand under his pseudonym Nechtan (1), while his affair with her goes on under yet another pseudonym, Elcmar. Southern Ireland bound him so tightly to the Eoganacht dynasty that its leading member, Eogan Mor, carries the alternative name Mug Nuadat, servant of Nuadu. As a Tuatha De king he carries the stone of Fal into Ireland, and the sword among his people's four treasures belongs to him. His kingship breaks twice. First, in the opening battle of the same saga, the Fir Bolg warrior Sreng severs his arm; the Tuatha De carry him from the field, and when Nuadu next challenges Sreng to bind his own right arm for a fair fight, Sreng refuses. To spare Nuadu fatal injury, the Tuatha De cede to Sreng's people the whole province of Connacht, and the Fir Bolg take the offer. The ill-starred Bres then becomes leader, but seven years on, Dian Cecht crafts the silver arm for Nuadu, and a further twenty years of rule follow his restoration. During that second stretch of rule, the dreadful might of Balor daunts him, and Lug Lamfhota replaces him and brings victory. The dictionary adds a Fenian-cycle role: perhaps confused with the Leinster ancestor-deity Nuadu Necht, Nuadu is thought to have married Eithne (i), elsewhere the mother of Lug, to found the line that gives Fionn mac Cumhaill his maternal ancestry. The fortress on the Hill of Allen was thought to have been his at the start, also attributed to Tadg mac Nuadat, whom some take for the father under another name. Tradition counts Nuadu among three prophets of the pre-Christian age, beside Goibniu and the shadowy Mathu, another attributed consort is the war-goddess Fea, and oral tradition buries him at Ailech in Co. Donegal.
```
