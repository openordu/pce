``` tab [group1:Info]
::: magazinestyle
Niall is known as Niall of the Nine Hostages. MacKillop gives the long form Niall Noigiallach, rendered Nine Hostager. He founded the line of the Ui Neill. All that house counted him as its founding father. His house ruled Ireland for six centuries. Tradition credits him with foreign conquests. It also names him a possible captor of St Patrick. Some writers call Niall the first provably historical high king. His story carries heavy mythological freight. The usual reign dates, 379 to 405, stay disputed. He ought to have lived a generation before Patrick arrived, traditionally 432. Niall's son Loegaire was said to have met the saint. James Carney argued against that chronology. He put Niall's death as late as about 452. The Ui Neill branches fought each other often. They still named Niall their common ancestor. Many hands padded his persona over time. Two tales explain the epithet. The older tale ties him to the nine petty kingdoms of the Airgialla. He carried off one leading man apiece from those peoples. Seizures of that kind marked his earliest wins. Medieval scribes probably made up the better-known tale. There he holds one pledge from every Irish province. Pledges from Scots, Saxons, Britons, and Franks stand beside those. The existing entry, built on Monaghan and O'Rahilly, keeps a shorter record. It has Niall succeeding at Tara after twenty-three years in Connacht. It carries the claim that the historical Dathi was no king at all. MacKillop preserves the fuller tradition instead. Niall was the son of the captured British slave Cairenn. His father was King Eochaid Mugmedon, a man of Connacht origins. Eochaid had already raised four sons with his wife Mongfhind. The tale Echtra Mac nEchach Muigmedoin tells how Niall took the kingship ahead of his half-brothers. He lay with Dame Sovereignty, a loathsome hag who turned beautiful. The druid Sithchenn tested the boys. He foretold that Niall and his line would hold Ireland. One variant makes Mongfhind reach for poison. Her sons had agreed to split the island. Both tellings end with her dying at Samain. That death explains why Munster women once said Halloween prayers to her. MacKillop weighs the conquest claims with care. Irish raiders did plunder Britain's coast in late Roman times. He rejects Ridgeway's 1924 claim that Niall was the Scot whom General Stilicho resisted in 399. Slave-raids on parts of Great Britain remain believable. The unproven extra is that young St Patrick numbered among his captives. Four death-accounts survive, all from texts after the eleventh century. The pursuer is Eochaid. His father Enna Cennselach rules Leinster as its king. The feud began when poet Laidcenn refused Eochaid food. Eochaid burned the poet's house and killed his son. Laidcenn answered with a satire that stripped Leinster of leaves for a year. Niall invaded the province in reply. The Leinstermen later handed Eochaid over, but he killed Laidcenn with a stone. Niall banished Eochaid for as long as the king drew breath. While Niall stayed abroad, Eochaid killed him. The four tellings put that killing in Scotland, in the Alps, in the English Channel, or beside the River Loire. Every version sends his body home for burial at Ochann. The place is now Faughan Hill. It sits southwest of Kells, south of the assembly at Tailtiu. Eight of his sons, maybe fifteen in all, went on to found the Ui Neill. Four set up the northern branch in Tir Chonaill, today's Donegal. They displaced the Ulaid of Ulster. Four more founded kingdoms in Tir Eogain, today's Tyrone. Four others, with Diarmait mac Cerbaill among them, raised the southern branch in the midlands. The branches traded the kingship at Tara back and forth. They shaped how history got written and how Christian institutions grew.
```
