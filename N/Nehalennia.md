``` tab [group1:Info]
::: magazinestyle
Nehalennia is a Continental goddess who watches over seafarers. Writers also give her the Latin form Nehalannia. Two cult sites along the North Sea honoured her worship. Rising waters later covered and destroyed both of those shrines. Depictions show her as a young woman in a distinctive local dress. A small round cap rests on her head. A short shoulder-cape wraps around her shoulders. She usually sits with fruit-laden baskets set in her lap and at her side. A dog nearly always accompanies her. The animal is best read not as a servant but as the goddess in a shape-shifted form. Other divinities share this same bond in the sources. Cuchulainn appears with a dog. Sequana appears with a duck. Cernunnos appears with a stag. Moccu appears with a pig. Epona appears with a horse. In Romanized Gaul the names and figures survive but the myths behind them are not told. The poet claimed the power to take animal shapes as well. Witches made the same claim old writers note. Surviving evidence shows that wealthy patrons supported her cult. These clients were Roman citizens of Celtic stock. Some of those patrons may also have been Germans. A whole study by A Hondius-Crone examined the temple at Domburg. Another work by J van Aartsen looked at the goddess her cult. She ranks among the best-documented goddesses of the Roman north-west. Both sanctuaries stood along the coast of the Low Countries. Domburg lies on the island of Walcheren in Zeeland. Rising sea and shifting sand eventually swallowed the shrines. Seafaring merchants raised altars there before setting out. They prayed to the steerswoman for a safe voyage and a happy return. The fruit baskets in her lap carried the promise of cargo that arrived unharmed. Her little round cap and short cape mark her as a local woman. So Nehalennia kept the North Sea trade under her blessing. Every recovered altar fills one more gap in her forgotten story. The drowned temples bury her rites, yet her name endures in stone.

:::
```
