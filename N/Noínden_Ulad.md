``` tab [group1:Info]
::: magazinestyle
Noínden Ulad is an Irish term bound to the Ulstermen and to their famous debility. The full form is Ces Noinden Ulad, 'the nine days' debility', also glossed 'the pangs of the Ulstermen'. Older spellings such as ceisnoidhe record the same idea of a nine-day affliction. Tradition places the origin of this debility in a cruel wager. The wager fell on Crunniuc mac Agnomain's household. His wife Macha was made to race a horse. She carried a child at the time. She won the race, and at the finish she bore her children, so tradition says. In her pain she laid a curse on the watching Ulstermen. Whenever danger pressed hardest, the warriors would feel the pangs of childbirth. The weakness would grip their race for nine times nine generations. After that, no Ulster champion could fight at full strength when the seizure came. Received lore holds that the young Cúchulainn alone escaped the curse. This is why he must defend Cooley single-handed when the great cattle raid begins. Tradition elsewhere honours Macha as a goddess, and Emain Macha is said to carry her name. MacKillop notes that the story opens the Táin Bó Cuailnge itself. He reads Noínden Ulad as an Irish example of couvade. In couvade, husbands in some pre-technological societies take to their beds during labour. They act as though they themselves were bearing the child. The parallel is close, because the Ulstermen suffer birth-pangs without ever bearing children. Because the sickness strikes only in crisis, storytellers use it to isolate a single defender. MacKillop also lists studies of the puzzling word noinden by Tomás Ó Broin. Vernam Hull edited Irish texts of the debility and of the Ulstermen's affliction. Scholars still debate the exact sense and length of the sickness. The term names a malady rather than a people. This short notice therefore points readers to the fuller account of the Debility of the Ulstermen.
```
