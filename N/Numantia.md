``` tab [group1:Info]
::: magazinestyle
Numantia rose as a Celtiberian town to the north of Soria, beside the upper Duero in eastern Castile and Leon, Spain. Scipio Africanus the Younger captured it in 133 bc, closing the Celtiberian wars and laying the central Iberian Peninsula open to Rome. The town started as a large castro, or hill-fort, covering some 32 hectares on a plateau 70 m high, above the point where the three rivers Merdancho, Tera, and Duero come together. It ranks as a major archaeological site, studied for over two centuries. The Museo Numantino at Soria opened in 1919 to house the finds and direct research. Occupation reaches back to the early Bronze Age at the close of the 3rd millennium bc, and the Celtiberian town was set above an earlier Iron Age hill-fort. A regular street plan served blocks of ten to twenty houses. Each house ran about 12 m long and 3 to 6 m wide on a one-room 'shotgun' plan, with three or four rooms strung back to a yard and store shed. A city wall stood roughly 6 m high. Numantia took up arms in 143 bc and the fighting dragged on until 134 bc, when Scipio, fresh from destroying Carthage in 146 bc, became consul and could raise an army. He ringed the town with seven camps joined by a 9 km wall and starved the defenders out. The Numantians fielded only 8,000 fighters against a far larger Roman force, and their terms were refused. Starved into surrender, many killed themselves rather than submit. The survivors were sold into slavery. Yet life at Numantia did not end. The rebuilt town kept the old street grid, straightening and widening the roads and adding cisterns and drains. It grew into a trading centre, and an important ceramic industry developed on the local clays. The east-west road from Caesaraugusta to Asturica crossed the Duero just below the town. In the 1st century ad Numantia became a municipium, granting its people Roman citizenship. It remained a modest settlement with no public bath, theatre, or forum, and was abandoned in the later 4th century.
```
