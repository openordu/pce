``` tab [group1:Info]
::: magazinestyle
Nemed is a legendary leader of early Irish settlers. His full name is Nemed mac Agnomain. Writers also spell it Nemhedh, Neimheadh, or Nemedius. The sense of the name rests on the Old Irish word nam ed, which carries ideas of the holy, the sacred, privilege, and sanctity. He stands as the eponymous leader of the Nemedians, the third mythic invaders of early Ireland. That ranking comes from the pseudo-history known as the Lebor Gabala, often called the Book of Invasions. The text presents Nemed as a Scythian whose descent ran from the biblical Magog and from Japheth. He reached Ireland with only his wife Macha, four sons with their wives, and twenty more followers. Macha passed away after twelve years and was interred at Ard Macha, a place this text names in her honour. That place is now known as Armagh. Another of his wives is named Cera. Three of his sons stand out in the record: Fergus Lethderg, the soothsayer Iarbonél, and Stam. After building his settlement he faced the Fomorians. Some scholars read such invasion myths as faint memory of history that is now lost. The pattern of a small band landing and then multiplying speaks to how the pseudo-history built the story of the taking of Ireland. His people are said to have cleared the twelve plains of the island. They likewise dug four great lakes and raised two royal forts. The first of those forts crowned the sacred hill at Armagh. A plague carried off Nemed himself, as the old record tells. After his death the Fomorians returned to grind down his folk. These sea raiders demanded heavy tribute from the settlers. The survivors rose at last and made war upon their masters. Those deeds grew into the long struggle that marks the whole cycle. His name drawn from the old word for the holy links him to the sacred. That title set Nemed apart from the earlier invaders. In the Lebor Gabala he stands as the founder of a line. The plains and lakes he shaped still carry his people's mark in myth. So the tale remembers him as a bringer of good order.
```
