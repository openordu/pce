``` tab [group1:Info]
::: magazinestyle
Lamadelaine, a burial ground in Luxembourg, dates from the 1st century bc. For Celtic studies it matters because it reveals elaborate ritual practices and many further marks of daily life in a district that the Treveri held at the time, a Celtic speaking tribe of the Belgae of north east Gaul. The finds show the close bond between funeral custom and formal feasting among the pre Christian Celts. One of the cemeteries linked with the Titelberg oppidum, Lamadelaine holds seventy graves and eighteen offering ditches, set in three distinct groups. Animal bones preserved to an unusual degree have illuminated animal sacrifice among the Iron Age Celts. Several signs suggest that animal bodies spent time in the open air before cremation. In pig sacrifice the offering was shared out: a part went on the pyre, another lay in the grave, and the choicest piece went to the living for a meal. When the tomb was closed, the cremated parts went in first, then offerings of unburnt meat, now and then laid out with scattered pieces and jars so as to recreate the shape of a pig. This exact rite marked the first stage of the cemetery, then slowly faded, until finally every offering went on the pyre and the graves grew uniform, showing how old native customs were evened out by incoming Romanizing conformity.

:::
```
