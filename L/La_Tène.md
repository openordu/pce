``` tab [group1:Info]
::: magazinestyle
La Tène is a place and the name of a stage in Celtic culture. The site lies in the shallows of Lake Neuchâtel in Switzerland. In 1858 an amateur archaeologist found a great hoard there. MacKillop calls the spot a glory of the barbarian world, and it marks the chief outpost for Celtic craftspersons and artisans. The settlement flourished from shortly after 500 B.C.E. until the Roman conquest. The rich trove runs to several hundred brooches, nearly three hundred spears, a score of wooden shields, and well over a hundred swords. It also holds gold collars, iron cauldrons, and silver pins. Votive offerings added dogs, pigs, cattle, chariots, and even human remains. Because of this wealth of material, the name La Tène now describes the third stage of Celtic culture, after the Urn field and the Hallstatt layer. That culture ran from about 500 B.C.E. to about 100 C.E. and divides into three phases, I, II, and III. The La Tène style grew out of the older geometric art of Hallstatt, and Etruscan and Greek forms crossed from the Mediterranean to shape it. Its telltale marks are S-shapes, spirals, and round swirling patterns placed in even order. Known across all of Celtic Europe, the style shows most vividly in the pre-Roman British Isles. It weakened under Roman power yet clung to life in Ireland, passing through the early Christian centuries and fading only at the Anglo-Norman invasion of 1169. Scholars like Paul Vouga and the Megaws helped fix its place in later archaeology.

:::
```
