``` tab [group1:Info]
::: magazinestyle
Leinster is one of the four great provinces of modern Ireland, set beside the eastern coast. Its name reaches back to the Laigin, the early people who ruled that part of the island, and the province keeps their memory alive. Before the English conquest it was one of five parts of Ireland, since the middle region of Meath was counted by itself or Munster was taken as two. The present line of its borders was drawn only in the seventeenth century, so the province is a newer shape laid over an older map. Many counties now stand within it, including Dublin, Kildare, Kilkenny, Laois, Longford, Louth, Meath, Offaly, Westmeath, Wexford and Wicklow. Large stretches of the northern part of the county, chiefly the old graves, the passage tombs and the pagan holy places along the Boyne in Meath and Westmeath, were held in early times by Meath and are not counted as part of it. The old kings of Leinster kept two great seats of power. One was the massive hill fort of Dun Alinne, which covered more than thirty acres near Kilcullen and is the largest such fort in the land. The other, Dind Rig, rose on the bank of the River Barrow in Carlow and served the kings of the southern district. A tale of the early cycle, Orgain Denna Rig, tells how the royal seat of Dind Rig was burned to the ground by the warrior Labraid Loingsech. The abbeys of Clonmacnoise and Glendalough, two of the most famous early Christian foundations, also stood inside the region now called Leinster.

:::
```
