::: magazinestyle
Lugaid mac Con is a mythical king of Munster and leader of the Érainn. His patronymic means son of hound. Storytellers set him at the heart of the Cycle of Kings. That medieval body of tales follows the high kings. He must not be confused with Lugaid mac Con Roí, the killer of Cúchulainn. Annalists date his reign to the 2nd century AD. Most tales about him were written down centuries later. Many carry the mark of the Uí Néill, dynasties that opposed his line. Their writers blamed drought and suffering on his usurpation. Lugaid gained the byname mac Con as a fosterling under Ailill Aulomm's roof. A dog suckled the boy. Ailill's own son Eógan grew up in the same household, and the two became constant adversaries. At Cenn Abrat the Eóganacht, led by his foster-brother Bogan, broke his power. Lugaid escaped because his fool Do Dera, a near double, wore a crown and died in his place. Eógan then spotted Lugaid's white legs and chased him from the field. The fugitive reached Scotland with twenty-seven companions. The band swore silence about their leader. A Scottish king gave them a year of hospitality and marvelled at such order and skill in a leaderless band. Two events broke the anonymity. A poet landing from Ireland brought news of how Eógan misruled the land. Lugaid's grief at that report gave him away. The king then served his guest several dead mice. Lugaid ate like a regal guest, and his men followed suit. Asked outright, Lugaid admitted his identity. The impressed king then gathered a host drawn from Scots and Britons alike, stretching from the Scottish coasts to Ireland. This allied army swept aside every opponent on the road to Mag Mucrama. On the eve of that battle Art mac Cuinn conceived Cormac mac Airt. Art would die in the fighting the next day. Díl, a blind druid, foretold defeat for Bogan. The druid judged Eógan's cause unjust. Art fell, Eógan fell, and the seven sons of Ailill fell with them. Tara then answered to Lugaid for seven years, with the young Cormac fostered at his side. A false judgment over an old woman's sheep cost him the crown. The men of Ireland expelled him and made Cormac king. He returned to Ailill's household. Ailill Aulomm greeted him with a kiss of false welcome. Ailill's poisonous tooth burned his cheek. Within three days the venom had eaten away half his face. Soon after, Ailill's retainers killed him beside a waterfall on the Bandon River. Later storytellers added an inventive etymology for the byname. While bathing, his mother was impregnated by an otter, the water-hound of the wordplay. When sleeplessness tormented the grown king, his otter-father carried him beneath the waves and cured him. Medieval tradition also spun off near-namesakes. Lugaid Conmac, Lugaid Lága, and Lugaid mac Ítha all overlap with his legend in genealogy and story.
