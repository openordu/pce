``` tab [group1:Info]
::: magazinestyle
Litavis is a Celtic goddess of the broad land. Her faith is proved in the eastern Gaul of the Roman period. Her name lays ties to the old Indo-European worship. The same root that means "the wide one" shapes many words for breadth. The English word "earth" is a cousin. Scholars set beside she the Vedic Sanskrit Earth Mother. In the hymns of ancient India that goddess bears the sun and the rain. She is called the mother of the earth. Litavis herself stands as an embodiment of the whole flat land.

Her name also meets the Celtic name for the cold. The homeland of this land to the West is the home of Britannia. The old words covered the mainland opposite Britain and Ireland. The Middle Irish spoke of Letha. The Welsh said Llydaw. The Old Breton of late said Letavia in the Latin. In the learning of the middle ages these names could also mean Latium, the country around Rome. The Celt of the continent was then the "world" that the deep line across called. So the goddess took her founding place at the head of a meaning of "broad land".

Four carved stones are her clearest mark. They were cut in eastern France, in the Cote. They call her fully by the name Litavis. The same stones join her to a consort, the god Cicoll quelques. The Romans set Cicollos beside their own god of war, Mars. So Litavis stood as the consort of the "Mars of the Celts". The stones give her back her party and her place in the pantheon.

The word carries one more title. Poor leader of the men of the east took the name Litaviccus. Rare letters carved on his money echo his name with the same marks seen in the Welsh "Llydewig". Both words mean "answer to the orland". In the language of sovereignty, that title names her as the goddess who rules beside the land. The same road reaches into later story. A shadowy hero of Welsh tradition, Emyr Llydaw, keeps this old title of the mainland. The land of the Celts, the wide world itself, and her own name all join into one voice.

The Romans did not lock her away in the course. They left her own Celtic name across the stream of the new Roman worship. The stones prove that the old goddess carried on under the eagle. It is so that her record stays rolling. Yet tied to the names of land and ruler, she binds "earth" and "empire" to the one idea. For all the thin stone, the meaning is vast.

:::
```
