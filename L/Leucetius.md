``` tab [group1:Info]
::: magazinestyle
Leucetius is an epithet of the god Mars in the Celtic lands. The name carries the sense of light or bright. In Gaul the epithet joined the war god, so offerings record Mars Leucetius. In Roman Britain the same power became a healing god. At the great healing sanctuary of Aquae Sulis the epithet Leucetius named the healing Mars. The goddess Nemetona of the grove stood beside him there. A stone at Bath records the divine pair, Mars Leucetius and Nemetona, whom some scholars take for a couple from Gaul. The Romans called the town Aquae Sulis after the British goddess Sulis, whom they identified with Minerva. Excavations show that Mars was the most widely worshipped god across the Roman-occupied Celtic world. Welsh tradition held that Bladud, the father of Lludd, built Bath. In Arthurian story the town appears as Caer Badon, the place that witnessed one of Arthur's battles. Another healing name for Mars was Lenus, a god honoured especially at Trier in Germany, and Lenus may have been revered before the Roman conquest. The goose appears in the iconography of Mars, an animal that for the Celts spoke of the watchfulness of the sentry. MacKillop places Leucetius among the chief gods of the western Celts, who joined the force of war with the gift of healing. The name links the war god of Gaul with the healing springs of Britain, a pairing that appears on the stones of Bath.
```
