``` tab [group1:Info]
::: magazinestyle
Lailoken is a hermit of the medieval Scottish tradition who lives in great hardship in the wild and is said to have the power of visions. The figure closely matches the Suibne Geilt of Ireland and the Myrddin of Wales, whom many writers know as Merlin, and all three are thought to owe a shared north British origin. The Scottish texts that first name Lailoken are the Lives of Saint Kentigern, written soon after the Anglo-Norman overhaul of the Scottish Church. Two tales, named 'Kentigern's Encounter with Lailoken' and 'Lailoken in King Meldred's Court', survive in a late manuscript and were headed the Vita Merlini Silvestris. The wild man story likely began as a popular tale of Cumbric Strathclyde, joined to the battle of Arfderydd. Lailoken is a Brythonic name that links to the Middle Welsh word llallog, meaning friend, brother, or lord.

MacKillop paints Lailoken as a naked, hairy wild man of the woods. He shows prophetic gifts at the court of Rhydderch Hael in Strathclyde, a Welsh tongue realm of the Old North, that is the Scottish Lowlands, in the 6th century. By the same line of argument, much of the Lailoken story feeds Geoffrey of Monmouth's picture of Merlin in the Vita Merlini from about 1149. In the 15th-century Scottish tale called 'Lailoken and Kentigern', the hairy wild man tells Saint Kentigern that he brought to an end the fighters who died at the battle of Arfderydd. That meeting is dated 573 or 575. The text says the figure named Myrddin, the man behind the Merlin of legend, went mad after that defeat.

:::
```
