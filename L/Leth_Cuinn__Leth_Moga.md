``` tab [group1:Info]
::: magazinestyle
Leth Cuinn, Leth Moga are the two halves of Ireland in early tradition. The surrounding bank between them is the ridge called the Eiscir Riada. The ridge runs from Galway Bay in the west to the Dublin district in the east. The northern half, Leth Cuinn, is the portion held by Conn of the Hundred Battles. The southern half, Leth Moga, is the portion held by the champion Mug Nuadat, who is also named Eogun Mugain. The Book of Invasions, the Lebor Gabala, cuts the island along this same ridge. In that book Eber Finn takes the land north of the line. Eremon takes the south. So the old story works twice, once with two humans and once with two gods. The cut along the ridge is older than the later five provinces. Ulster and Connacht lie mostly north of the line. Leinster and Munster lie south of it. Munster grew so wide that scholars counted it as two parts, east and west. The ridge itself is a chain of low sand hills left by the last ice age. It is not a wall or a river but a line of faint higher ground. Poets and genealogists kept the memory of the two halves long after the kings were gone. Each half carried its own list of kings and its own claim to the high seat. The two names thus fix the oldest map of power in Ireland. That map still shaped the border of the chief kingdoms down the centuries.

:::
```
