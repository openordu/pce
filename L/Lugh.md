``` tab [group1:Info]
::: magazinestyle
Lugh, also called Lug or Luga, was a great god of the old Irish tales and a chief among the Tuatha De Danann. He stands with Fionn mac Cumhaill and beside Cúchulainn as a leading hero of the Irish mythic cycle. His title Lámfhota means long-armed, and Samildánach means one who is skilled in many arts. A further name, Maicnia, marks him as a boy warrior. He may have started out as a sun god or a god of light. The Luigni folk of Meath and Sligo once traced their line to him. His great tale is told in Cath Maige Tuired, the account of the second fight at Mag Tuired. He was the grandson of Balor, the one-eyed Fomorian king. A seer told Balor that his grandson would slay him, and so Balor shut his daughter Eithne away. The hero Cian, son of the healer Dian Cécht, entered the tower with the druidess Birog and thus fathered the child Lugh. Triplets were born, but his two brothers were lost soon after or changed into seals. Balor looked at the young one and praised his long hands, words that shaped the boy's name. The ocean god Manannán mac Lir guarded the young Lugh. The smith god Goibniu and the ruler Eochaid raised him as well, while Tailtiu and Duach cared for him as mothers. Lugh grew into a man both fair and strong. When the Tuatha De Danann faced the Fomorian armies, their wounded king Nuadu could not lead. Lugh came to the assembly for the war at Mag Tuired. At the gate of the fort he was refused, for he could claim no single craft, and the men already kept a smith, a harper, a fighter, and a bard. He asked whether any one man could master all their skills together, the Samildánach, and then they received him. He cast a great stone over the high wall and played the harp, and old Tara marvelled. Nuadu surrendered the crown, and Lugh took the Tuatha against the Fomorian host. Lugh drove his spear through the magical eye of his grandfather and so won the day. Tales say he invented the game of fidchell and first raised the races of horses. He sat as king for forty years. In one account the sons of Tuireann slew him near the hill of Uisneach, and he drowned in the lake called Lugborta. His name yet stands in many place names across the land, and he is often set beside the god Lugos and the Welsh figure Lleu Llaw Gyffes.
```
