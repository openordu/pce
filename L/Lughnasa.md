``` tab [group1:Info]
::: magazinestyle
Lughnasa was one of the four great feasts of the old Celtic year. It was the harvest festival held on the first of August, and it closed the year of festivals. The next cycle, begun by Samhuain, came after it. The feast took its name from the god Lugh. The word keeps the name of Lug, who held a fair at the start of harvest each year. That fair grew into the great Oenach Tailten, the Fair of Tailtiu. It took place at Telltown, County Meath. The man who made his claim to the kingship kept the fair each year. It was among the most noted gatherings of early Ireland. Lug himself set the feast to honour his foster mother Tailtiu in the region of Brega. He also led the horse riding and the martial contests at that gathering. Soon the celebration spread, and Emain Macha kept it for Ulster while Tara kept it for the whole island. At first the Christian Church allowed the feast, since it marked the harvest's start and the moment when calves and lambs were weaned. Over time the festival left its fixed date of the first of August. It might then run across many days, often on Sundays, from the middle of July to the middle of August. The celebrations gave time for racing horses, for swimming them, and for games of hurling. People climbed hills to pray and pick bilberries. Others gathered at lakes and holy wells. The fairs also permitted trade in goods at places such as Killorglin and Ennistymon. Máire MacNeill traced customs of Lughnasa across the British Isles. Smaller fairs not tied to Lug were kept for Calan Awst in Wales and at Morvah in Cornwall. The English and Scottish loaf mass, Lammas, may be Celtic in origin. The Romans kept a feast for the emperor Augustus at Lugudunum, the town of Lyon. That feast may rest on an older festival of the god Lugus. Among the continental Celts it was the time when the great Council of the Gauls met. In the Manx tongue the day was Laa Lhuanys, while in Wales it bore the name Calan Awst.
```
