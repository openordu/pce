``` tab [group1:Info]
::: magazinestyle
Jacobite Poetry is the Gaelic verse born of the Jacobite risings in Scotland. It runs from the flight of James the Seventh in 1688 to the death of Charles Edward Stuart in 1788. Most surviving poems deal with the rising of 1745 and its defeat. Alasdair Mac Mhaighstir Alasdair stands as the leading poet of that campaign. He also served as a captain in the prince army. He mocked sympathizers of the House of Hanover and urged the clans to take arms. Iain Lom MacDomhnaill wrote against William and Mary. He also praised the 1689 victory at Killicreeankie. That win cost the Stuarts their general, John Graham of Claverhouse. Iain Lom later mocked the unpopular Union of 1707 in song. The rising of 1715 brought many roll calls of clans and sharp calls to fight called brosnachadh. Women also raised their voices. Sìleas na Ceapaich wrote a tense address to King James's army. She set the Sheriffmuir standoff in three poems. The Disclothing Act of 1747 stripped tartan from loyalist and rebel alike. Poets answered in protest. Donnchadh Bàn mocked the new trousers in verse. Rob Donn MacKay put Jacobite feeling in a song against the act. After the defeat of 1746 the poets read it as a pause, not a final defeat. Uilleam Ross wrote the last note. His elegy for Charles Edward Stuart arrived nearly forty years after the rising. The verse proves the political debate of the day ran live across the Highlands, and song was its public voice.

:::
```
