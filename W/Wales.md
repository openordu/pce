``` tab [group1:Info]
::: magazinestyle
Wales is a principality of the United Kingdom, west of England. Its Welsh name Cymru stands for the nation, and Cymry for the Welsh people. The word Cymry comes from the Celtic combrogos, meaning compatriot. An old claim traced it to an eponymous founder named Camber, but that etymology is clearly spurious. The Welsh name for themselves was long Y Gwir Frythoniaid, the true Britons. The English name Wales comes from OE wealh, a foreigner, meaning a native Briton rather than a Saxon. French still names the Welsh les Gallois, the Gauls. The Welsh people descend from the P-Celtic British conquered by Rome in the first century BC. Wales drew apart from ancient Britain, and chroniclers date that parting to the Saxon victory at Chester around 615. In the poem Y Gododdin, Welsh warriors ride from the Scottish lowlands toward what is now Yorkshire. The poem commemorates their heroic deaths. The phrase Gwyr y Gogledd, men of the north, gathers the old kingdoms Rheged, Gododdin, and Strathclyde. Many a gwlad, or petty kingdom, rose inside the principality and then merged with a neighbour or faded away. The most lasting were Gwynedd in the north and Dyfed and Deheubarth in the south. The south-east of the country often went by the name Morgannwg, later become Glamorgan. Four bishoprics divided the medieval principality, and their centres met at Pumlumon. That five-peaked massif also sources the Wye and the Severn. Anglo-Norman and English designs ended in conquest under Edward I and the death of Llywelyn ap Gruffudd in 1282. In 1301 Edward gave his own son the title Prince of Wales. Ever since, each male heir to the British throne has borne that style. Literary tradition in Wales opens with the sixth-century Cynfeirdd poets Aneirin and Taliesin. Surviving manuscripts include the Black Book of Carmarthen, the White Book of Rhydderch, and the Red Book of Hergest. Through these codices run the four branches of the Mabinogi, the most regarded cycle of medieval Welsh prose. Lady Charlotte Guest gathered the Mabinogi with seven more tales and gave them to English readers as her Mabinogion. The Acts of Union of 1536 and 1542 barred Welsh from official use, yet the language thrived at home. By the twentieth century's end nearly nineteen per cent of the population claimed some Welsh. No other Celtic culture can match that share or that total.
```
