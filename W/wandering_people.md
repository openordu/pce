For centuries the roads of Ireland carried a large and varied company of travelling folk, and Kevin Danaher fills a whole chapter of his book on Irish customs with the many sorts of people who made their living by moving. He opens with a witty yarn about an impatient Corkman who vowed to keep walking until the world ran out, and who scrambled up a sky-scraping wall only to find a Kerryman already perched at the summit, taking his ease with a pipe and staring across the endless blue distance. The jest gives the chapter its heart: the Irish of old were movers, not stayers. A scholar named Walafrid Strabo, writing over a thousand years ago, marvelled that wandering had grown second nature to his Irish contemporaries, and he watched whole boatloads of them arrive abroad, monks, builders and learned men whom kings and bishops received gladly and whose labour shaped the faith and knowledge of the West. Poets, acrobats and travelling players travelled too, loved by common people rather than by the great. Some dodged the comfortable routes through France and Germany and pushed their boats the other way, past Iceland and perhaps even that new land beyond the western sea, bringing home reports of floating ice islands, of the long-toothed walrus, and of the bewitching isles hedged round with wonders.Irish feet were almost certainly abroad well before those saintly golden years, Danaher notes. A much-mythologised king called Dáithí was said to have died by a lightning stroke beside the Alps, and the raiders who seized Patrick for a slave remade the country's future without any such plan. Once St Colmcille left his dear Derry for a life of exile, an unbroken river of Irishmen flowed out in every direction, holy men and fighters, the celebrated figures of Columbanus, Fiachra, Killian, Sarsfield, Lally and O'Mahony the least of the catalogue, so that no continent lacked some travelling son of Ireland.The journeying of ordinary folk on the home island was just as constant, which plainly overturns the respectful myth of the past as a land of isolated parishes. Danaher argues that more persons moved along the roads of Ireland in old times than in the age of the motorway. Work far more than pleasure sent them out, and among the most feared of these mounted poets whose upkeep could ruin a patron, who demanded fruits out of season and soft beds in their own strange ritual, and who answered a refusal with a scathing lampoon of the kind that a kitchen hands learned and spread, a satire carried like a brand until it blackened the man it named. The bands that troubled Guaire, the generous king of Connaught, numbered six hundred singers with their people and servants and several score hounds, and only the patience and counsel of his brother the holy Marbhán kept the king from being eaten out of everything. In the end all Ireland rose and meant to expel them, and it was the gentle plea of Colmcille that won them the right to remain on a pledge of order.The pilgrim scholars wandered for very different reasons than poets, in search rather than escape of learning. Under the Penal laws, higher study was scarce, and a young man of promise had to walk to whatever priest or hedge-master was said to teach Latin and Greek well, and there he laboured on a farm or schooled the house children for his keep, and such a one might climb to eminence in the church or abroad. Music too drove men along the roads, the ballad-singers who could count on a hearth every night, leading figures being a harper of fame called Turlough Carolan, the last of his line; these players were made welcome at both the old gentry houses and those around by Cromwell settlers and partisans of King William, as the man who could not appear to love music was shrugged off as a clown. Story-tellers similarly always found a fire, and the anecdote runs of a boy who owned no tale and so was turned from every door, until a house at night that put him in a room of terrors, and in the morning the curious man of that range said he had now a splendid, long story to be kept.Hard work had its own travelling castes. The smith, the cooper, the cobbler, the saddler, the carpenter and the stone-cutter all sought work around the wrack. The tailor camped in a farmhouse until the whole family's cloth was sewn. Migrant labourers called the spailpíní crossed from the west to the rich farms of Leinster and the east, spade their tools spring and scythe and hook in them harvest, and many of them made the annual sea crossing to England and Scotland, returning with the same master and the same acres as their fathers, greatly valued for their strength and their whistling. The road had its cruelties as well, for a Dublin newspaper of 1790 recorded how a band of twenty-seven reapers, their boat already waiting, were snatched up by a press-gang and compelled to serve in the king's navy, the officer observing that men who could cut meadow could equally well be made to cut the rulers' foes.Evicted families, a handful each time, drifted to the western and southern ports to take the emigrant salt ship, and though they approached a farmer's door with deepest shame, the alms asked for in God's name were never declined, and a committee into Irish poverty in the 1830s estimated that the farmers' yearly charity food was worth near a million pounds. And roaming at all times were the wandering people, the tinkers that prefer the name travellers, who mapped their own circuits, each family loyal to its season and its place, who guarded the private language of their trade called the Tinkers' Cant, whose tinsmiths ranked above the peddler, and whose names had their shape for the region, as given the Joyces of Connaught, the O'Briens of Munster, the Doyles and Delaneys of Leinster and the Dohertys of Ulster, and among the travellers of former days the gifted executants, families bearing the names Delaney, Doran and Cash, were held in especially warm remembrance. Danaher closed his chapter with gentle sorrow that these final wanderers, in the manner of the travelling craftsmen, the spailpíní and the questing pupils who came before them, were slowly disappearing, having no room on the new highways, and that with them would fade much of the colour and hardy thrift of the old Irish countryside.
