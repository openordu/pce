::: magazinestyle
William of Orange acted as the leading statesman of the Netherlands, and in the year sixteen eighty-nine he ascended to the crown of England as King William the Third, once his wife's father, King James the Second, had been stripped of the throne. In the folk memory of Ireland his standing rests almost entirely upon the overwhelming victory he won against James beside the River Boyne in sixteen ninety. By the old calendar that battle took place on the first of July, yet according to the corrected reckoning it is observed on the eleventh, and it marks the defining moment of his campaign in Ireland. Seventeen days or so before that encounter William set foot on land at Carrickfergus. He came at the head of an enormous armada of close on seven hundred ships, carrying a large and finely equipped force made up of contingents drawn from Denmark, the Dutch lands, France and Prussia. A few choice legends cling to that landing. One holds that his boot left a lasting mark pressed into the paving of the wharf. Another tells that, to set down his first warrant in writing, he had recourse to his own blood for the ink together with a small sprig of hyssop for the pen, insisting that the very first Orange warrant must never be written with anything produced by human craft. In this way the event was wrapped in wonder from the very outset, as befits a figure who was already turning into a legend on Irish soil. Making his way south with the army toward the Boyne, he is supposed to have snapped off a flowering spray of lily and worn it as his mark, and from that gesture comes the tradition of the Orange Lily. Every twelfth of July Irish loyalists call these matters to mind, honouring the triumph at the Boyne through processions, melodies and colourful display. Their banners depict the king in dazzling military uniform, sword raised on high, astride a noble white charger. Though William truly did go into the struggle decked out in his full honours, a practice that perturbed his own men because it turned him into an obvious target for enemy fire, this iconic rendering has grown out of far later imagination rather than out of that day itself. That familiar likeness traces back to a painting finished by Benjamin West in seventeen seventy-one. In reality the portrait of William so well known today belongs to the closing years of the eighteenth century far more than to the seventeen hundreds, because it was only upon the establishment of the Orange Order in seventeen ninety-five that he became the steadfast champion of every Protestant grouping and the unrelenting adversary of what was then called Popery. Curiously, many of the Catholic powers on the continent had openly delighted in his success, angry as they were with Louis the Fourteenth of France, the patron and backer of James. Looked at in purely religious terms, however, his win brought hardship to the Presbyterians no less than to the Roman Catholics, so the consequences spread far beyond the battlefield and left their mark on the whole confessional life of the island. In the thick of the action at the Boyne William gave proof of striking personal courage, and memory sets this in sharp contrast to the manner of James. One ball lodged in his shoulder; another glanced off the clasp of his pistol; and a third knocked the heel from off his boot. Though an attack of asthma was upon him, still he rode unceasingly in the fiercest part of the fighting, tending personally to each unit of his force and refusing to withdraw anywhere for safety. So steadied was his conduct that even his Jacobite adversaries held him in regard, and Patrick Sarsfield is reported English officers that if only the two sovereigns could be exchanged between the sides, his own men would cheerfully begin the battle afresh with them. Such testimony from a defeated enemy shows how deeply that display of grit impressed itself upon the age. What lingers at last is a blended memory of hard soldiering, curious wonder and later ceremony, and it is that mixture which still colours the place of William in the Irish tradition.
