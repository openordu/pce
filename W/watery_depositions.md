``` tab [group1:Info]
::: magazinestyle
Watery depositions are offerings placed into water. People deposited goods in bogs, lakes, springs, and streams. The famous Lindow Moss body comes from Cheshire. The Dowris hoard lies in County Offaly. Ritual deposits occurred at the thermal Bath site. Those rites spanned the pre Roman Iron Age. They outlived the Romano British cult. The water connection drove the act of giving. It did not matter where the site sat. Bronze Age people made many wet offerings. The Dowris hoard dates about 800 to 650 BC. Iron Age groups kept this custom too. The Llyn Fawr deposit dates near 650 BC. Later hoards held at Duchcov and Agen. The Llyn Cerrig Bach site thrived into Roman times. Tacitus records its active use until AD 60. Aqueous stores follow a narrow array of types. Weapons and soldier gear lead the count. Horse gear, chariot fittings, and trumpets fill the lists. The Loughnashade finds match that pattern. Sheet metal vessels complete the main group. Ornamented items made from precious metal appear. Gaulish wells hold the remains of meals. Food sat in ceramic jars with Italian amphorae. Those amphorae possibly carried wine. The Seine source yielded votive body parts. Similar wooden, stone, and bronze figures persist today. Human bones occasionally appear in these waters. Lindow Man is the best known case. Workers pulled his body from Cheshire peat in 1984. He died a threefold death. Someone garrotted him and cut his throat. He also drowned in a bog pool. Early Irish tales tell of that same end. Diarmait died drowned in a vat of ale. His death happened while his house burned. Hints of the practice appear elsewhere. Skulls from the La Tène site show weapon marks. Those skeletons confirm a brutal, recurring rite. The wet deposits thus reveal real sacrifices.

:::
```
