::: magazinestyle
 Suibhne (Sweeney, Mad Sweeney, Suibne, Suibne mac Colmain, Suibhne geilt) was a king of a small region of Ulster. He went to war to help the king of the province, but a curse hung over him. He had treated a monk, Saint Ronan, with contempt, so the saint cursed him. When the fighting began, Suibhne lost his wits and fled his post. He thought he was a bird, so he hid in a yew tree. He moved from tree to tree as his people searched for him. At last he came to a monastery. A relative, dressed as an old woman, earned his trust and brought him home. Then she showed him how high he used to leap. He tried it again and lost his senses. He wandered to a monastery in County Carlow and spoke with Saint Molling. As he told his story, a jealous husband killed him with a spear. Suibhne is not on any king list, so scholars read him as a form of the wild seer Merlin. Koch names Suibne Geilt, the madman or wild man, a central figure of a set of Middle Irish texts. His madness is a spiritually ecstatic state. It works as an inspiration to poetry, born of the outcast life of the saintly madman. As a prophet and poet deranged by war and cut off in the wastes, he comes near to Myrddin and Merlin in Welsh tradition and Lailoken of Strathclyde. The three figures share attributes, stories, and a northern British geography. Scholars think they spring from a common sixth- or seventh-century Strathclyde tradition of the wild man as mad prophet. A legal triad in Bretha Etgid already notes Suibne's wildness at the battle of Mag Roth, saying it gave benefit through the stories and poems which followed. A gloss in the Codex Sancti Pauli puts in his mouth riddling verse on a treetop perch described as a hermit's oratory. Something like the saga Buile Shuibhne must have been known by the ninth century. The word geilt, meaning roughly wild man or mad man, most likely comes from the Brythonic gwyllt, the stock epithet of Myrddin. It entered Gaelic along with the legend from Strathclyde. The speech of the Gaels handled geilt as a noun and later changed to a feminine stem. Near homonyms such as Welsh gwellt and Irish gelt, meaning grazing, may also play a part. Like the biblical Nebuchadnezzar, the Celtic wild man is sometimes shown living on grass. The saga looks back to the fight at Mag Roth in 637, on ground now called Moira in County Down. The horror of that battle turns Suibhne into a geilt. Domnall Brecc of Scottish Dal Riata and Congal Caech of Ulaid meet defeat at the hands of the high king of the Northern Ui Neill. Suibne drifts into a frenzy of fear, grief, and guilt. His wild roaming leads him to woodland above Dumbarton on the Clyde, where he comes upon the wild man Alladhan. He wins spiritual perfection through long suffering, through hunger, cold, and the loss of company. In the saga and the related poetry, he dies and is buried at Saint Mo-Ling's monastery, there to await the resurrection. Mo-Ling, not Ronan, seems to be the older saintly companion of the legend. Heaney's full verse rendering, Sweeney Astray, brought the figure a recent revival. Flann O'Brien made Suibne familiar to readers of At Swim-Two-Birds. Modern writers continue to work with his madness and his verse.
