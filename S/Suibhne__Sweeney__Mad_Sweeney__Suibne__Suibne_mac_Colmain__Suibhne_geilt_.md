``` tab [group1:Info]
::: magazinestyle
Suibhne (Sweeney, Mad Sweeney, Suibne, Suibne mac Colmain, Suibhne geilt), was a king of a small region of Ulster. He went to war to help the king of the province, but a curse hung over him. He had treated a monk, Saint Ronan, with contempt, so the saint cursed him. When the fighting began, Suibhne lost his wits and fled his post. He thought he was a bird, so he hid in a yew tree. He moved from tree to tree as his people searched for him. At last he came to a monastery. A relative, dressed as an old woman, earned his trust and brought him home. Then she showed him how high he used to leap. He tried it again and lost his senses. He wandered to a monastery in County Carlow and spoke with Saint Molling. As he told his story, a jealous husband killed him with a spear. Suibhne is not on any king list, so scholars read him as a form of the wild seer Merlin. Koch names Suibne Geilt, the madman or wild man, a central figure of a set of Middle Irish texts. His madness is a spiritually ecstatic state. It works as an inspiration to poetry, born of the outcast life of the saintly madman. As a prophet and poet deranged by war and cut off in the wastes, he comes near to Myrddin and Merlin in Welsh tradition and Lailoken of Strathclyde. The three figures share attributes, stories, and a northern British geography. Scholars think they spring from a common sixth- or seventh-century Strathclyde tradition of the wild man as mad prophet. A legal triad in Bretha Etgid already notes Suibhne's wildness at the battle of Mag Roth, saying it gave benefit through the stories and poems which followed. A gloss in the Codex Sancti Pauli puts in his mouth riddling verse on a treetop perch described as a hermit's oratory. Something like the saga Buile Shuibhne must have been known by the ninth century. MacKillop defines the headword geilt as an Old Irish word meaning one who goes mad from terror. The word most often describes Suibhne, and the tale that carries the meaning is Buile Shuibhne, The Madness of Suibne.
```
