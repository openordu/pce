``` tab [group1:Info]
::: magazinestyle
Swords were the clear offensive weapon most often linked to the Celts. Yet their use was not universal. Chiefs and the upper ranks mainly carried them. The Celtic tongues keep five old native words for the blade. The oldest root is Proto-Celtic kolgo and kalgo. This shows in Calgacus, the name Romans gave the swordsman who led the north Britons at the Battle of Mons Graupius. Latin took the cut blade root and gave it to the gladius. In time the Brythonic and Goidelic tongues shaped their own forms, from Welsh cleddyf and Cornish kledha to Old Irish claideb and Scottish Gaelic claidheamh. Other roots, kledo, slakk, and fugo, also show behind later words. Shape decided use. A pointed kolgo was a thrusting weapon and a cledruy was a cutting edge. The long eighty-centimetre slashing blades of the La Tene style were unknown in Iron Age Ireland and north Britain. The classic sword carried a leaf-shaped bronze blade. Such carp-tongue and antenna-hilted swords of about 1100 bc rank as the earliest claimed Celtic blades. Iron and bronze Gündridgen and Mindelheim swords later served the first Celts who appear in history. The La Tene fashion brought the sword back at dawn of the Iron Age. A blade of near sixty centimetres grew past eighty. A soldier wore it in a scabbard at the right hip. The Irish swords stood short, from 37 to 46 centimetres in the 30 known finds. Roman, Saxon, and Viking weapons then came into Ireland and the Celtic lands of Britain. The claymore, a long double-handed Scottish blade, closed the line. Swords remained in use in the Celtic world well into the later Middle Ages.

:::
```
