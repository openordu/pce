``` tab [group1:Info]
::: magazinestyle
In the early Celtic world, salt stood as a vital resource. It is the compound NaCl, and it came from three sources. Seawater held it in solution, salt springs carried it, and large mineral beds stored it underground. People made salt two ways. They evaporated salty water in shallow pans, and they mined the hard mineral. Both crafts date from the Late Bronze Age, roughly 1400 to 1200 BC. The broken clay pans, called briquetage, survive along the European coasts. Tregastell and Sene in Brittany have yielded good finds. Salt springs fed the works at Bad Reichenhall in Germany. The richest mines lay in the eastern Alps, at Hallstatt in Upper Austria and at Dürrnberg bei Hallein in Salzburg. The Hallstatt folk grew wealthy on this trade, and their name still marks the Iron Age culture.

The word for salt runs through the Celtic tongues. The Welsh word is halen. Breton gives holenn. Cornish has holen, and Old Irish salann. The roots differ because of a sound shift from s to h that swept parts of the south. Latin pushed this change, for Rome ruled the lands where it happened. Ireland stayed outside the empire, so its speech kept the s. Old Irish law names the stuff murlúaith, sea-ash, and uses it to salt cut meat. Because no Irish mines are known, scholars think people burnt seaweed and took salt from the ashes. A salt tax, called salanngabál, is recorded on the Aran Islands. Ships carried salt to Ireland, and one wild tale speaks of English salt, salann Saxanach.

:::
```
