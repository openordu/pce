::: magazinestyle
Serglige Con Culainn, with agus Óenét Emire completing the title, is an Irish narrative of the tenth and eleventh centuries. English readers know it as The Wasting Sickness of Cúchulainn, The Sickbed of Cúchulainn, or The Only Jealousy of Emer. Most references shorten the title to Serglige Con Culainn. The copy in the twelfth-century Book of the Dun Cow joins two earlier versions of the tale. That joining leaves some incoherence in the surviving story. The full title hints that two older stories stand behind the text we hold. Cúchulainn's wife appears first as Eithne Ingubai and later as Emer. The men of Ulster gather at Mag Muirtheimne at Samain. A flock of beautiful birds settles on a nearby lake. Every woman desires a pair, one bird for each shoulder. Cúchulainn hunts them down and gives two birds to each noble woman. None remains for his own wife. He vows her two other birds in their place. Those birds fly bound by a golden chain, and their song brings sleep. Cúchulainn only grazes them with his spear, yet falls into a deep sleep against a pillar-stone. In a dream two women appear to him, one in green and one in red. They laugh and whip him, at first in play and then so hard that his strength drains away. That wasting becomes his sickness. His people carry him to the twinkling hoard at Emain Macha. He lies there close to a year. Shortly before the next Samain an otherworldly man appears beside his bed and sings a riddling offer of cure. He gives his name as Lugaid of the red stripes, son of Áed Abrat. He asks Cúchulainn to come to Mag Cruachna in County Roscommon. There he will be healed, for Angus's sister Fand longs for him. The woman in green then meets Cúchulainn at the pillar-stone. She is Li Ban, paragon of women, one of his dream tormentors. She seeks his friendship. She brings word from her husband Labraid, the swift hand on sword, who rules Mag Mell. Labraid offers Fand, now parted from her husband Manannán mac Lir, for a single day of service against his enemies. Cúchulainn is drawn to the offer and remembers Fand's famous beauty. Still weak, he sends his charioteer Láeg to scout for him. Láeg rides the bronze boat beside Li Ban out to an island. Fand greets him with her company of women, and Labraid laments that Cúchulainn stayed home. Back at the sickbed, Láeg paints Mag Mell in glowing words. Emer comes to her husband and shames him for lying weakened by woman-love. She calls on him to throw the sickness off. He rises, and his weariness leaves him. A year later the same meetings repeat at the pillar-stone. Again Cúchulainn sends Láeg in his place, this time over land. Láeg returns with fresh wonders and tells his master a fool would stay behind. Cúchulainn then takes his chariot to Mag Mell with Li Ban and Láeg. He gets a warm welcome. At Labraid's request he scouts the great enemy camp alone. He asks Labraid and his men to fall back so he can fight without them. He holds the ground through the dark hours. At daybreak his battle fury takes him, and his face turns to a vision of terror. The rage burns so high that attendants must cool him. They plunge him three times over into a cold vat of water. Cúchulainn then cuts down Senach Siaborthe and Eochaid Inber and spears Eochaid Iúil as the man bathes. All Mag Mell rejoices, Fand most of all. He stays with her a month of continual love. He vows a second meeting at the yew tree of Ibor Cind Tráchta, hard by modern Newry in County Down. Emer hears of the assignation though the men try to hide it. In her fury she brings fifty women armed with whetted knives to the trysting place at the set hour. What follows is unexpected. Emer speaks on how men lust for what they lack and reject what they hold. Cúchulainn answers that his wish is to dwell with her for all his days. Fand, seeing their love, asks to be let go. Emer, seeing the unselfishness of Fand, offers to step aside herself. Each woman would hand her lover to the other. The deadlock breaks when Manannán mac Lir arrives unlooked for and carries Fand away. Cúchulainn wanders the mountains of Munster in grief and will not eat or drink. The druids finally give him a drink of forgetfulness. Emer drinks too and forgets her jealousy. Manannán shakes his cloak between the hero and Fand so they can never meet again. Myles Dillon gave the standard modern text to the Mediaeval and Modern Irish Series in 1953. His translation ran in Scottish Gaelic Studies in 1951. W. B. Yeats built on the same story. His drama of 1919 bore the title The Only Jealousy of Emer. He recast it in prose as Fighting the Waves. The drama is still no true adaptation of the tale.
