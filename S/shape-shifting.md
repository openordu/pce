``` tab [group1:Info]
::: magazinestyle
Shape-shifting is the Celtic power to move between bodies. In the myths of Ireland and Wales it belongs most often to bards and to druids. A shifter could leave one body and take another while the inner essence stayed the same. The poet Amairgin, first poet of the Milesians, marked his entry into Ireland with a long verse. He named himself a wave of the sea and a salmon in a pool. Traditional lists of shifters live on through the poems of Tuan Mac Cairill and the Welsh poet Taliesin. Enchantment could force a change as well. The poet Sadb, mother of the poet Oisín, was turned into a deer. A spiteful fairy made the beauty Aige a doe. The cruel Aífe cursed her own kin to live as swans. The curse lasted nine hundred years. She herself was changed into a crane. Some shifters chose their own form. Étain and Midir left the royal hill of Tara as swans. Cian took the shape of a pig to hide from the Sons of Tuireann. The Welsh enchantress Ceridwen shifted many times to catch her thieving servant Gwion. Witches took the form of hares to steal milk and butter. A witch who struck as a hare would limp the next day.

:::
```
