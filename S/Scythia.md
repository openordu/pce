``` tab [group1:Info]
::: magazinestyle
Scythia named an ancient land that lay north of the Black Sea. It held a strange pull on the early Irish imagination. Nobody could mark its borders. It ran between the Danube and the Caucasus. It covered ground close to modern Ukraine and Crimea. No Celtic remains have ever been found on Scythian soil. Even so, writers have traced Scythian motifs inside La Tène art. The real Scythians were nomads of eastern Europe, known for horses and lavish goldwork. Several early Irish texts, most notably the Book of Invasions, claimed the Irish came from Scythia. Their compilers took the Scythi of that land to be kin of the Scotti, the Irish people themselves. Fénius Farsaid, credited as the fabled maker of the Irish language, was said to have reigned there. The same tradition made Scythia the homeland of the Nemedians. No ancient country of that exact name ever existed. The poet Edmund Spenser bent the link to harsh use. He claimed the Irish were Scythian barbarians. English settlers could then take their land without moral restraint.

:::
```
