``` tab [group1:Info]
::: magazinestyle
Shamanism is a religion of the far north. The English word comes from a Tungus term recorded in southern Siberia. Scholars have compared it with the Sanskrit word for an ascetic. Anthropologists first described the practice in the late nineteenth century. They worked among the Tungus and other peoples of western Siberia. The same pattern later turned up among North American Indians and elsewhere. Many readers have seen hints of the same ritual among the early Celts. The poet of early Ireland sometimes practised divination while wrapped in a cloak of bird-feathers. The Celtic poet did so much as Siberian shamans did. The tale called Fis Adamnáin, the Vision of Adamnán, sets the saint's soul loose from his body. The soul departs on the midsummer feast of Saint John the Baptist. The date marks the summer solstice. The soul's departure mirrors what shamans claim to do beyond the body. From the Welsh Arthurian cycle, Gwrhyr Gwalstawd Ieithoedd carries a shamanistic gift. The gift is the ability to converse with beasts in their own speech. Scholars have tied the Celtic bard to the shamanic type. The bard stood as a seer who shifted shape. He drummed and danced his way into a trance. He took another body. He did so just as a Siberian shaman takes the form of a bear. The Song of Amairgin names the poet a wave of the sea. It also names him a tear of the sun. The poet crossed into another world and back. The bard kept the community in balance through satire. The Celts reached that state with ale rather than with mind-changing plants. We cannot prove an arctic hysteria among them. Scholars stay split on whether the word fits. Some want shamanism kept for Asia. Others doubt we can read an old faith that way.

:::
```
