``` tab [group1:Info]
::: magazinestyle
Scéla Mucce meic Da Thó, printed also as Scél Mucci maic Dóthó, titles a ninth-century story of the Ulster Cycle. The tale implies that enmity between Ulster and Connacht was of great antiquity. It stands among the few tales of Ulster in which Cuchulainn takes no part. Mac Da Tho, a wealthy landowner of Leinster, owns two prized animals. His hound Ailbe defends his property alone with the ferocity of ten armies. His tame boar has been reared seven years on milk to furnish a great feast. Ailill and Medb of Connacht and Conchobar mac Nessa of Ulster bid against each other for the hound. Connacht offers 160 prize cows with a chariot drawn by its two best horses. Ulster offers friendship, a standing alliance, and cattle each year without end. On his wife's advice Mac Da Thó promises Ailbe to both sides and bids them come and claim it. By the time both parties arrive he has slaughtered the pig. Sixty oxen must haul it into his hall of seven doors and fifty beds. Briccriu advises that the pig be carved by the warrior with the greatest battle victories. One claimant after another yields to a challenger with the stronger case. Cet mac Magach shames Ulster and moves to carve, until Conall Cernach bursts in and defies him. Conall boasts he has never passed a day without slaying a Connachtman. Cet answers that his brother Anluan is greater, and Conall flings Anluan's severed head down on the floor in reply. Conall carves the pig, keeps the choice portions, and leaves the forelegs to the westerners. The men of Connacht attack, and the hall fills with bodies. On departure they split the hound Ailbe in two for favouring Ulster, leaving its head on a yew tree. The Ulstermen then look for healing to the maidens of Emain Macha. The central motif stages the contest for the champion's portion. That scene echoes what Posidonius recorded of feasting among the ancient Gauls.
```
