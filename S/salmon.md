``` tab [group1:Info]
::: magazinestyle
The salmon is the large pink-fleshed fish of the species Salmo salar. It has long held a central place in the Celtic imagination. It stands chiefly as a vessel of otherworldly wisdom. That role runs especially deep in the traditions of Ireland and Wales. Material evidence carries the idea into Gaul. A Gaulish relief depicts a human head flanked by two great salmon. A Gallo-Roman altar shows a fish, probably a salmon, leaning close to whisper into a human ear. The ancient British god Nodons, lord of the river Severn, is carved hooking a salmon. Welsh and Irish lore often sets the fish in fixed water. The traditions place them at wells, pools, and waterfalls along great rivers such as the Boyne and the Severn. Two salmon of wisdom lived in Ireland. One swam at Linn Féic, a pool along the river Boyne. The other dwelt at the cataract called Assaroe on the river Erne. The boy Fionn mac Cumhaill caught both. In the better-known story, the bard Finnéces had angled for seven years at Linn Féic for the salmon. When he finally drew one in, he believed his long wait was rewarded. He set the fish over a fire. Fionn touched it with his thumb. The burning oil passed to his lip. He sucked it. The fish thus poured its otherworldly knowledge into him. That same salmon carries the personal name Fintan in the tale. When Fionn spears the second fish himself at Assaroe the fish bears a byname. That byname is Goll Essa Ruaid, meaning the one-eyed fish that swims the Assaroe falls. A Welsh salmon of wisdom swims the Severn under the name Llyn Llyw. The Welsh traditions call Llyn Llyw the oldest of living creatures and the wisest of forty animals. Llyn Llyw speaks to the hero Culhwch. The fish tells him where Mabon is held prisoner.

:::
```
