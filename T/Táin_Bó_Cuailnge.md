::: magazinestyle
Táin Bó Cuailnge, the Cattle Raid of Cooley, ranks as the supreme work of classical Irish letters. It stands as the key epic of the Ulster Cycle. Early writing in prose and verse alike dates from the seventh and eighth centuries. Complete texts survive in the Dun Cow codex, the Book of Leinster, and the Yellow Book of Lecan. The many revisions trend from lean prose and sharp humour toward bombast, florid alliteration, and sentiment. The action pits Queen Medb of Connacht and her host against the Ulster champion Cúchulainn. Behind the war stand two bulls: Finnbennach the White Bull and Donn Cuailnge the Brown Bull. Medb and her husband Ailill quarrel at Cruachain over whose possessions carry more worth. The white bull left the queen's herds because it would not serve under a woman. Medb resolves to take the brown bull of Cooley by force after its owner refuses her bargain. Seven foretales, the rémscéla, supply the background the audience needs. A ninth-century anecdote tells how the tale came back from the grave with Fergus mac Róich. He recited it to the poet Senchán Torpéist. The foretales cover Conchobar's kingship, the birth pangs laid on Ulster by Macha, and the sad story of Deirdre. They also tell of Cúchulainn's wondrous conception, his wooing of Emer, and his slaying of his own son Connla. A last foretale explains how the two rival bulls were begotten. Cúchulainn alone escapes the pangs, and he holds the army alone through single combats at the fords. The prophetess Fedelm sees the plain crimson before the host, a vision Medb challenges in vain. Medb reviews her host on the first night and scatters her eager Leinster allies among the battalions. Cúchulainn bars the road with ogham hoops cut in a single stroke and set on pillar-stones. The four-day duel with his foster-brother Ferdiad at Ath Fhirdiad forms the climax. Cúchulainn wins with the Gáe Bulga, a spear that opens one wound into thirty points. Sualtam, the hero's mortal father, falls on his own shield edge, and his severed head roars out the alarm. The Ulstermen rise, and the great battle breaks the Connacht lines at Gáirech. Medb is spared and sent home, and the brown bull goes with her. The bulls fight across Ireland for a day and a night. Donn Cuailnge then dies of a broken heart at the Cooley border. Joseph Dunn, Winifred Faraday, Cecile O'Rahilly, and Thomas Kinsella head the translations. Ernst Windisch printed the Book of Leinster text in 1905. Mallory's Aspects of the Táin and the 1993 Tübingen studies carry the modern commentary. Augusta Gregory, the band Horselips, a Moriarty ballet, and Ní Dhomhnaill's A-táin poems number among the adaptations.
