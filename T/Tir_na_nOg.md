``` tab [group1:Info]
::: magazinestyle
Tir na nOg is the Land of Youth, the ever-young realm of the early Irish Otherworld. The name comes from the Irish word og, which means youth. Gaelic spellings include Tir nan Og and Tir na n-Oc. One tradition names the place Eilean na nOg, the island of the young. It is the most famous of all the otherworldly lands of Irish story.

The old gods, the Tuatha De Danann, settled the realm after the Milesians defeated them. In later belief they faded into the fairies of older lore. No one there knows sickness, age, or decay. The people dance and sing in a land that never ends.

Its fame rests largely on a 1750 Irish poem by Michael Coimin. Its subject is Oisin's sojourn in the Land of Youth. The Fenian hero Oisin lives there three hundred years with Niamh of the Golden Hair. To him the ages pass like a single day until he returns to the world of mortals. One step home ends the spell, and all the stored years land on the hero at once. Bran also reaches the land in the Voyage of Bran. Irish tradition knows several such promised lands, and Tir na nOg is the best remembered of them.

Tradition often places the realm west of Ireland, beyond the limits of any map. One entrance runs below the water of Lough Gur, in a cave on Knockadoon Island. Another lies on Rathlin Island, north of County Antrim. Long oral tradition puts an entry at Liscannor Bay in County Clare, south of the Moher cliffs. In 1861 Bryan O'Looney described a beautiful city there, ringed by white waves that break between Liscannor and Lahinch. The Welsh poet T. Gwynn Jones reshaped the idea in a 1910 ode. Scholars record the tale type under folk motifs D1338.7, F172.1, F377, and F378.1. Related entries cover the otherworld, Ynys Afallon, the king Herla, and Roc barraidh. Studies by David Spaan and the 1861 O'Looney translation anchor the modern scholarship.

:::
```
