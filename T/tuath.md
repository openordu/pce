``` tab [group1:Info]
::: magazinestyle
The tuath is an ancient Irish concept. The word meant both a people and the land they lived on. Most people stayed within the bounds of their own tuath for their whole lives. They met friends, relations, and enemies at festivals and assemblies called oenach. Legal rights mostly held only within the tuath. The bard or poet, however, could travel beyond its borders. Each tuath chose its own king, and Ireland held many such groups. A king's power came from his tuath, his people and their land. It also came from his link with the goddess who stood for the territory. MacKillop's broader tuath entry, which he cross-indexes with the Irish word for people, opens onto a striking triadic pattern. Early Ireland groups people and powers in threes. The three Fothads are a first triad. The Dagda has three sons, a second triad. Three Finns of Emain Macha, also called Finn Emna, form a third. Three female personifications of Ireland, Eriu, Banba, and Fodla, stand together. Three gods of craft, Credne, Goibniu, and Luchta, also stand as a triad. Three sons of Simon Magus assault Tlachtga, and she gives birth to triplets. Ingcel Caech carries three pupils set in one terrible black eye. Branwen of the Mabinogi counts as one of three matriarchs. Welsh tradition knows It also knows Three Generous Men on the Isle of Britain. The Manx emblem is the only a polity but a member of a wider threes that structured early Celtic thought.
```
