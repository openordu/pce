::: magazinestyle
Tuatha Dé Danann is the name the Lebor Gabála gives the principal family of pre-Christian Irish deities. Alternate printed forms include Dé Donann and de Danaan. The name joins the Irish words for a people or tribe and for god. The last element probably carries a title of the goddess Ana. The group was described as excelling all the peoples of the earth in every art. The short form Tuatha Dé is older than the Lebor Gabála itself. It covered both the Israelites of Bible translation and the elder gods. The origin of the Danann element remains disputed. The forms Dédanann and Dedananns are common but non-standard. The group is also called the Ever-Living Ones. Writers often imply them with the phrase áes sidhe, the people of the sidh. In the invasion scheme they arrive thirty-seven years after the Fir Bolg. They displace the Fir Bolg and come 297 years before the Milesians. Their usual adversaries are the Fomorians, the other euhemerized deities. Where other invaders came in ships, these newcomers stepped out of sombre clouds before Beltaine. Their landing causes a three-day eclipse. A variant text burns their ships on the shore, and the smoke causes the eclipse instead. They descend through eleven generations from the Nemedians. Tradition says they gained magic and druidic craft in far northern places. Four cities of enchantment had housed them before: Falias, Findias, Gorias, and Murias. Four great treasures came out of those cities. Falias yielded the crowning stone that cries aloud beneath a rightful king. From Findias comes the sword of Nuadu, which lets no one escape. From Gorias comes the spear of Lug, which guarantees victory. From Murias comes the cauldron of the Dagda, which leaves everyone satisfied. Near Lough Arrow in County Sligo they crushed the Fir Bolg in the battle called the first at Mag Tuired. They then formed three lakes and introduced pigs into Irish farming. From their stronghold on Tory Island by Donegal the Fomorians harried earlier settlers. The two peoples intermarried despite their rivalry. When Nuadu lost his hand and his fitness to rule, his successor Bres had a Fomorian father. Their champion Lug was grandson of the Fomorian juggernaut Balor. Lug and his people won the day in the battle named the second at Mag Tuired. After that battle they made Tara their capital, calling it Cathair Crofuind. Tradition credits them with building many pre-Celtic ruins, including Ailech. Ireland was divided among the Dagda's three sons. Each married an eponym of Ireland: Banba, Fódla, and Ériu. The three gods of Danu appear again as Brian, Iuchair, and Iucharba, sons of Ana. Those three lead the sorrowful tale of Tuireann's children. Three more sons of Danu were Goibniu the smith, Credne the artificer, and Luchta the wright. Leading figures include the Dagda, king and master of druidical magic. Beside him stand Manannán mac Lir the sea-god and Dian Cécht the healer. Lug was champion and master of arts, while Angus Óg governed poetry and music. Brigid was fire-goddess and patron of poets, and Boand was goddess of the Boyne. The war-goddess triad numbered Badb, Macha, and the Mórrígan. Ogma was a god of eloquence, strongman and warrior, and Donn ruled the dead. In later days Bodb Derg, offspring of the Dagda, held kingship. Artists of the folk included the harper Cas Corach and the musician Féthnat. The poets Abcán and Ai mac Ollamon and the druids Esras and Figol served them. The Milesians beat them twice, at Tailtiu and Druim Ligen, but the struggle was small. Popular materials then describe the Tuatha Dé surviving as immortals in barrows and cairns. The world split in two: the surface went to mortal Ireland, the underground to them. The route between is the sidh, the fairy mound. Fighting between deathless beings and mortals drove them to hold back milk and food grain. Restitution followed. Some texts keep the Dagda alive after Mag Tuired so he can reign beneath the ground. His son Bodb Derg frequently fills that underground throne instead. Their realms mirror mortal life yet remain idyllic: Mag Mell, Emain Ablach, and Tír na nÓg. Their power of féth fiada made them invisible, so they roamed among mortals undetected. They kept quarrels, hierarchies, and intrigues beneath the beauty and agelessness. In time their realm grew indistinguishable from that of the fairies. Finnbheara, king of the fairies, was reckoned once a king among these same people. The great figures of the Lebor Gabála were never reduced to that status. Some malice credited to them may be borrowed from mix-ups with the Fomorians. Dwelling below ground, cut off from sunlight, shrank them in the telling. All across the countryside people pointed out spots where these beings made their homes.
