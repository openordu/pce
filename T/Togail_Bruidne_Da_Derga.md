``` tab [group1:Info]
::: magazinestyle
Togail Bruidne Da Derga is the Irish title behind the English rendering, the ruin of Da Derga's hostel. Composition may fall in the eighth or ninth century, with written form no later than the eleventh. Though nominally an Ulster Cycle tale, its settings and characters belong to Leinster. Manuscripts carry the tale in the Dun Cow codex and the Yellow Book of Lecan. The opening appears to continue from Tochmarc Étaíne, with a lush description of that story's princess. The action centres on the legendary king Conaire Mór, innocence itself before relentless fate. Before his just and prosperous rule at Tara, a series of gessa, or taboos, were laid on him. Birds were to hold privilege in the kingdom at all times. He might not ride sunwise or withershins round Tara and Brega, nor chase Cema's crooked beasts. A ninth night spent away from Tara was forbidden too. He might not sleep where firelight showed after dark or lodge a lone guest at night. Three red men must never go first into a red man's house. He might not allow raiders to land in his reign or decide a quarrel between any two of his people. In the course of the tale he unintentionally breaks every one. His foster-brothers, sons or grandsons of Donn Désa, turned to marauding, and he banished them. The three Ruadchoin of the Cualu went the same way. At sea the exiles met reavers under the one-eyed Briton Ingcél Cáech. With the exiled sons of Medb, each named Maine, they wasted Britain first, then Ireland. The raiders landed at Merrion Strand in County Dublin and marched inland with five thousand men. Conaire was travelling to Da Derga's hostel near Bohemabreena or Glencree, where a hideous seer prophesied the defenders' doom. The magical hostel is usually described with seven doorways, though some texts give nine. Ingcél spied on the building, Fer Rogain identified its defenders, and three fires failed to burn it down. Many died, the fool Lomna first, as he had foretold, but the defenders slew many attackers too. When every drop of water was gone, Conaire died of thirst, and two reavers decapitated him. His severed head thanked Mac Cécht for crossing Ireland in search of water to ease his thirst. Some commentators accept T. F. O'Rahilly's view that the tale reflects the historical triumph of the Lagin over the Érainn. John V. Kelleher argued that the tale is alluded to in Joyce's story The Dead from Dubliners of 1914. Whitley Stokes printed the Dun Cow codex text in Revue Celtique for 1901. Eleanor Knott edited the Yellow Book version in 1936.
```
