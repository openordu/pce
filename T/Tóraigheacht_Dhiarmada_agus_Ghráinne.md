Tóraigheacht Dhiarmada agus Ghráinne is the Irish title of the greatest prose narrative of the Fenian Cycle. English readers know the tale under the title The Pursuit of Diarmait and of Gráinne. Story elements reach back at least to the tenth century, but the surviving texts are all Modern Irish. The oldest, from the seventeenth century, carries the sediment of many accreted versions. The triangle of the ageing Fionn, the young Gráinne, and the handsome Diarmait Ua Duibne invites a comparison. The Deirdre tale offers the nearest parallel. Medieval references hint that the story first ran otherwise. In that older shape Gráinne married Fionn and was divorced by him. She was then wooed again beside Ailbe, another daughter of Cormac mac Airt. The feast ends the betrothal account. Grieving afterward for his dead wife Maigneis, Fionn complains that a wifeless man cannot sleep well. Diorruing names Gráinne, daughter of Cormac, as the worthiest woman in Ireland. Fionn sends emissaries, and Cormac grants the match if Fionn proves worthy to be his son-in-law. At the betrothal feast Gráinne fixes her gaze on Diarmait of the dark curls. In oral versions she cannot resist the love spot on his brow, a mark he keeps covered. She drugs every guest but Diarmait and orders him to run off with her. He refuses from loyalty until she lays a destroying geis on him. The lovers cross the Shannon, and Diarmait raises a seven-doored house in the woods. Fionn follows with his men, though some of the Fianna restrain their leader's blood-lust. Oisin sends the hound Bran to warn the pair, and a loud-voiced man raises a false alarm. Diarmait plants three kisses on Gráinne before the enraged king. Angus Óg then wraps Gráinne in a cloak of invisibility, while Diarmait leaps clear over the heads of the pursuers. The flight wanders through west Munster and Connacht. Folk variants carry the pair to every corner of the Gaelic world. The common folk name for a dolmen, the bed of Diarmait and Gráinne, records those wanderings. Diarmait holds back from Gráinne out of respect for Fionn. A splash of water on her leg at last draws his daring taunt. The love is consummated, and Gráinne conceives. Her craving for the rowan berries of Dubros brings the pair to the tree of the surly ogre Searbhán. That ogre is one-eyed and guards the Sligo wood. Diarmait overcomes that magic with the ogre's own iron club, and the lovers feast on the berries aloft. Fionn and the Fianna rest beneath the tree at a game of fidchell. Diarmait drops a rowan fruit upon the playing board, and Oisin's next moves turn three matches. Challenged to show himself, Diarmait presses three more kisses on Gráinne before Angus carries her to the Brug na Bóinne. Angus later brokers a peace, and the couple settles near Céis Chorainn in Sligo with their children. One night a hound cry on the scent drags Diarmait from his bed against Gráinne's warning. Fionn has laid on a boar hunt at Ben Bulben, knowing the geis that bars Diarmait from pig hunting. A half-brother was magically restored in that shape, yet the doomed hero joins the chase. The boar mortally wounds the unarmed champion. The old king stands over the dying man and gloats. Diarmait recalls the healing water that two whole hands could carry. On three tries Fionn lets the water drip away, and Diarmait perishes. The ending varies with the telling. Gráinne urges her sons to vengeance, mourns until death, or weds Fionn himself. The ballads of the seventeenth-century Duanaire Finn make her swallow her disgust and marry the old king. James Carney read the triangle through the late Roman pairing of Mars, Venus, and Adonis. Gertrude Schoepperle argued in 1913 that the tale fed the Arthurian triangle of Mark, Tristan, and Iseult. Nessa Ní Shéaghdha printed the standard edition for the Irish Texts Society in 1967. The Yeats and Moore play Diarmuid and Grania, with music by Edward Elgar, carries the story onto the stage.
