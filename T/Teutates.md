``` tab [group1:Info]
::: magazinestyle
Teutates is a god of the continental Celts. His name carries the sense of the tribe or the people. The Welsh tud and the Irish tuath share that root. The Roman poet Lucan set him with Taranis and Esus among the three great gods of Gaul. He wrote that the gods each took human sacrifice. A ninth-century commentary on Lucan adds a grim detail. Teutates favoured drowning, above all on the first of November. That date is the old Samain, the feast that opened winter. Modern students of the Gundestrup Cauldron claim his figure there. It is the warrior plunging a victim into a vat of water. The Lindow bog man of 1984 may have gone to Teutates or to Taranis. He is read as a war-god. Dedications join him with Mars, who bears the name Mars Toutates at Barway in Hertfordshire. Gaulish Mercury takes his company as well. Koch adds the prisoners of war offered near Massalia and the paired stones with Cocidius, Apollo, and Mercury. Such stones turn up from Seckau in Austria to Rome and Castor by the north road. The name stays shadowy despite many inscriptions in Gaul and Britain. MacKillop holds it most likely a title, not one god's own name. A tribal protector could carry it, and any number of divinities might receive it. The hero Tuathal Techtmar may take his name from that same stem. Paul-Marie Duval printed the study of the three gods in 1958, and his Dieux de la Gaule followed in 1976.

:::
```
