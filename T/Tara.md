::: magazinestyle
Tara is a prehistoric hill site in County Meath, the land the Irish call Contae na Mí. The Irish once called Ireland itself Ériu. The hill's own name marks a great and lasting kingship. Many call this office the high-kingship of Ireland. Tara holds the seat of the ard rí na hÉrenn. The hill sits between the towns of Navan and Dunshaughlin. It is not a grand high rise. Its plain shape hides a long and deep past. The archaeology of the hill is hard to read. It does not stop at the monuments you can see. Digging and geophysical surveys keep finding more human life. The ground holds more than the eye meets. The main monuments form a clear set. There is a Neolithic passage tomb from the New Stone Age. A long linear earthwork runs across the hill. A hill-fort stands on the high ground. A standing stone rises on the ritual mound. Legend ties this stone to Lia Fáil, the crowning stone that cried under every rightful king. MacKillop sets the hill's height at 507 feet and its place six miles south-east of Navan. He records the name forms Temair, Teamhair, and Temuir. The anglicized Tara grew from the genitive form Teamhrach. The old etymologies reach in many directions. Scholars have found the dark one inside the name. They have also read a spectacle, a high place, a hall of assembly, or a free eminence with wide prospect. The Lebor Gabála contrives a different path. It derives the name from tea mur, the wall of Tea. The Milesians gave the site the name Temair for Tea, Éremón's queen. Her name displaced the older one, Druim Cain. The hill also answered to Cathair Crofhind, to Druim Léith, and to Fordruim. Near Gorey in County Wexford rises a Tara hill of 831 feet. The same name thus travels far from the royal hill. From Ireland's earliest history Tara stood as a great centre of religious ceremony. The hill belonged to Medb in her old rank as a goddess. It belonged also to her double, Medb Lethderg of the red side. The ground had taken burials in the second millennium before Christ already. Kings held court at Tara who were also overkings of the region. The same men headed the Uí Néill federation. The central rite of each kingship joined the king with the local earth-goddess. It came in the ritual banquet called the feis temrach, the feast of Tara, held at Samain time. The Uí Néill took their name from Niall Noigiallach, Niall of the Nine Hostages. Tradition says he seized Tara from the Leinstermen in the fifth century, before the coming of Christianity. The Irish rule of descent did not keep property in order, so Niall's many sons carved up his hegemony. In later times the title king of Tara was only an honour. The ruler who held it often sat far from the hill. Many monarchs of Tara stand in the Annals, pagan and Christian alike. The list opens with Sláinge, who is said to have ruled in the twentieth century BC. Christianity's advance may have put down the feast, so pagan in its nature. The last king to celebrate the feis temrach was Diarmait mac Cerbaill. Later ecclesiastical writers invented the tale of Saint Rúadán's curse on Diarmait in a dispute of Church and State. The Annals still imply the hill was not abandoned even two centuries on. The Uí Néill kept naming their leaders kings of Tara while the site itself grew over. In time the hill drew one of the largest medieval fairs, held every third year at Samain. That fair stood beside Tailtiu, Tlachtga, and Uisnech. Credit for beginning it goes to the legendary king Óllam Fódla, many hold. Early Irish literature returns to Tara again and again. Even so, the tales view the hill across a distance in time. The tales of Conaire Mór show a magical kingdom there. Cormac mac Airt stands as the greatest mythical king of Tara. His court may echo the ambitions of the Uí Néill. The young Fionn mac Cumhaill won his first heroic honour on the hill. He slew the burner Aillén mac Midgna, who came each year to prey on the palace. The Tara king who met Saint Patrick was Lóegaire mac Néill. Many monuments of the site carry English names of modern date. Some grew from a fond reading of the Dindshenchas, and their pull stays strong even where evidence runs thin. Adamnán's Cross is an upright stone linked to the biographer of Colum Cille. Its faint outlines hold a female figure, possibly a Sheela-na-gig. The Banqueting Hall is a rectangular earthwork of 750 by 90 feet. Medieval literature describes a five-sided hall, and the earthwork does not match it. Modern scholars read it as an entrance-way through which horses and chariots passed. Cormac's House is a small earthwork inside the Fort of the Kings, and the Lia Fáil stands at its centre. The Fort of the Kings is also called the Rath of the Kings and the Royal Enclosure. An oval hill-fort of 950 by 800 feet, it nearly rings three other earthworks and the standing stone. The Fort of the Synods is a trivallate earthwork. An old tale set a meeting of Saint Patrick and Saint Brendan there, among others who never shared an age. In the late nineteenth century British Israelites mutilated the banks while they hunted the Ark of the Covenant. Digging later showed timber palisades of the first and third centuries AD. The Lia Fáil is a twelve-foot pillar-stone that rises six feet above ground. Granular limestone went into the stone, rock from no local quarry. It was raised in memory of those who died in the 1798 rising. It lay near the Mound of the Hostages until movers set it at the centre of Cormac's House. The letters R.I.P. now mark it. Claims that it is the ancient crowning stone, the mythical Fál, convince few. A small earthwork, the Mound of the Hostages, closes the Fort of the Kings on the north. Records say the stone should have stood there before 1798. The Rath of Gráinne, a burial mound, sits near the Banqueting Hall, with the Sloping Trenches beyond it. Fancy set the elopement of Gráinne and Diarmait there, though she was betrothed to Fionn. The Rath of Lóegaire is a large univallate ring-fort of the king who met Patrick. Rath Maeve, a univallate hill-fort, measures 750 feet across. It lies half a mile south of the hill's centre. Medb, though queen of Connacht, appears at Tara in Briccriu's feast and other tales. The Royal Seat, the Forradh, is a small earthwork beside Cormac's House. The Sloping Trenches are two unusual ring-earthworks in the far north-west of the site. That age saw a curse fall on the hill. A holy man spoke against its king, Diarmait mac Cerbaill, dead in the year 565. The curse is said to have left the hill unused. He was the last king to hold the older wild rite. So the great hill fell silent after a long public life.
