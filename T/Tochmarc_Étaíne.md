``` tab [group1:Info]
::: magazinestyle
Tochmarc Étaíne is the Irish title of a Mythological Cycle narrative usually called The Wooing of Étain in English. Its oldest parts may go back to the eighth or ninth century. Mutilated and disconnected fragments survive in the Yellow Book of Lecan. R. I. Best recovered long-missing pages in the 1930s, yet the whole stays disjointed. The first section opens with the Dagda winning Boand's love and tricking her husband Elcmar. Elcmar was lord of the Brug na Bóinne. Their son was Angus Óg, poetry's god and the Brug's future lord. The child went in fosterage to Midir of Brí Léith near Ardagh in County Longford. When Angus came of age and took the Brug, Midir visited and claimed compensation for a feigned injury. He asked for the fairest maiden in Ireland, Étain, daughter of Ailill. Angus won her for Midir by clearing twelve plains, making twelve rivers, and paying her weight in precious metal. Midir's first wife Fuamnach struck the new bride with a magic rowan rod, changing her into water. Heat and evaporation turned the pool into a worm, then a great purple fly of wonderful beauty. Midir recognized the fly as his wife, and she stayed beside him. Fuamnach knew it too, and raised a wind that drove her out over the sea. After seven years of misery the fly alighted on Angus's breast, and he kept her in a sunlit crystal cage. Fuamnach drove her away again, and she fell into the cup of the wife of Étar, an Ulster king. Swallowed with the drink, Étain was reborn as Étar's daughter, 1,012 years after her first birth. In the second section a thousand years have passed, and mortal Gaels now reign. Étain married the high king Eochaid Airem, whose people refused tribute until he took a queen. Eochaid's brother Ailill Anglonnach fell sick with hidden love for her. The physician Fachtna saw that only her love could cure him. While the king travelled on royal business, Ailill confessed, and Étain agreed to a tryst outside the stronghold. A magic sleep fell on Ailill, and a look-alike impostor lay with her for three nights. The impostor revealed himself as Midir, her husband from fairyland, who had filled Ailill with longing to arrange the meeting. Étain would not leave without her husband's consent, and Ailill was cured. In the third section Midir tricked Eochaid at a game of fidchell on a silver board. Eochaid won three matches and the prizes, among them fifty horses and a bog causeway in Tethba. When Midir won the fourth, he asked to hold Étain and receive a kiss. Eochaid agreed for a month later, then ringed Tara with armies when the day came. Midir seized Étain and rose through the smoke-hole, and watchers saw two swans vanish afar. Eochaid wrecked sidh after sidh in the search, and at Brí Léith Midir faced him with fifty Étain-shaped women. Eochaid chose his wife by her skill at pouring a drink, but he picked her daughter instead. Midir told him the truth: the true Étain had been pregnant when carried off. Eochaid laid waste to Brí Léith and rescued his wife. The child of that incestuous union was exposed, but a herdsman raised her. King Eterscél chose the grown girl for his queen, unnamed in the text. The narrative runs into Togail Bruidne Da Derga, where Eterscél mates with Mes Buachalla. Each of Étain and Mes Buachalla is named as mother of Conaire Mór. Bergin and Best printed the standard text in the journal Ériu for 1938. Lady Gregory, Fiona MacLeod, and Rutland Boughton's opera The Immortal Hour carry the story into modern art.
```
