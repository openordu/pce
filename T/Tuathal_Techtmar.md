::: magazinestyle
Tuathal Techtmar, printed also Teachtmhar or Teachmhar, was a shadowy high king of second-century Ireland. The name elements suggest ruler of all the people and legitimate or wealth-possessing. Genealogy, pedigree, and pseudo-history supply nearly all that is told of him. Writings giving his life and the reign dates of AD 130 to 160 appear first after the eighth century. His two great attributed achievements do reflect pre-Patrician Ireland. He is said to have led Goidelic invaders to Ireland and to have carved out the kingdom of Mide. T. F. O'Rahilly judged his central historical presence factual but much embroidered. Others see him as an invented grandfather for the heroic king Conn Cétchathach. The high-kingship institution itself dates from a later time. Historians have wrestled with his contradictory chronology since Geoffrey Keating. Late tradition has Tuathal landing in Ireland after an exile spent from his birth in Scotland. He came to avenge his father Fiachu Findholaid, murdered by Éllim mac Conrach of the Dál nAraide. God punished that wickedness with famine. O'Rahilly read the Scottish exile as a cover for a foreign origin. At age twenty, commanding a host largely foreign, Tuathal came ashore at Inber Domnann, the modern Malahide Bay. He conquered the nearby Fir Bolg, Domnainn, and Galióin, then marched on Tara and slew Éllim. A tenth-century text credits him with instituting the boráma tribute from the Leinster peoples. Each levy took five thousand head of cattle plus sheep, swine, mantles, bronze basins, and weighed silver. Gathering it went on across many centuries. The levy's stated cause was the insult of Eochaid, wedded to Tuathal's daughter Fithir. He then craved her sister Dáirine as well. Shame carried off Fithir, and sorrow carried off Dáirine. Tuathal meant to bend Connacht, Munster, and Ulster likewise to his hand and raise his own realm of Mide. Mide certainly existed, but his link to it rests on an etymological misreading. The text makes him cut Mide, the middle, from the neck of each province. Mide also takes in the country around the hill of Uisnech, the perceived centre of Ireland. There people believed he started the yearly assembly, though he likely never did. He traced his line back six generations to Eochaid Feidlech. Feradach Fechtnach stood as his grandfather, Eithne Imgel as his mother. His son Fedlimid Rechtaid fathered Conn Cétchathach, and another daughter was Báine. O'Rahilly takes up his story inside the 1946 Early Irish History and Mythology.
