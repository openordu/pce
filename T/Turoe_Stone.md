``` tab [group1:Info]
::: magazinestyle
The Turoe Stone is an Irish mythological site. It stands in a field outside Loughrea, near Galway City. The boulder is human-sized and upright, its top covered with ornate low reliefs. It first stood near a rath called Feerwore and was moved in historical times to its current place. The carving is in the La Tene style, which marks the stone as Celtic. Its purpose is unknown, though four similar pillar stones are found elsewhere in Ireland. Such stones may mark a central point, like the Stone of Divisions on the hill of Uisneach. A replica of the Turoe Stone stands in the National Museum in Dublin.

Koch calls it the finest of five non-representational carved stones dated to the pre-Christian Iron Age of Ireland. The other closely comparable stones stand at Castlestrange in Roscommon, Killycluggin in Cavan, Mullaghmast in Kildare, and Derrykeighan in Antrim. The boulder is an erratic left by a glacier, cut from fine-grained granite. It now stands 1.2 metres above the ground, with a further 0.48 metres below it. Its round shape and domed top have led some to suggest a phallic meaning. Its upper part carries finely cut La Tene ornament that sweeps across the crown. Below it sits a band of a cruder step pattern. The curved motifs fall on three different levels and follow a four-part plan. Their shapes recall the metalwork of southern England and Wales in the first century, though no similar carved stones are known from Britain. It was moved from its original place in the 1850s to serve as a lawn ornament for Turoe House. Excavations in the 1930s at the close-by ring-fort left these stones unstudied, so the original spot stays unknown.

:::
```
