``` tab [group1:Info]
::: magazinestyle
Taranis is a mainland Celtic god of thunder. His name carries the word for thunder in Welsh, Breton, and Gaulish. The poet Lucan named him in the Pharsalia. He wrote of Julius Caesar's conquest from a hundred years' distance. Lucan set Taranis with Esus and Teutates among the three great gods of Gaul and Britain. Each of the three, he wrote, took human sacrifice. Lucan judged the cult of Taranis crueller than the Scythian Diana's. Victims could be burned alive inside wooden vessels. A ninth-century commentary on Lucan calls Taranis a master of war and links him to Jupiter. MacKillop reads the god more narrowly. What we know shows only a bodying forth of thunder as a natural force. Taranis lacks the complexity of the Roman sky-god. He lacks the wide-ranging functions of that god too. Other commentators join him to the Roman Dis Pater. They connect him also with the British Ethamn and Etirun. The archaeological record does not support Lucan's story. The name survives on only seven altars. The stones run from Britain to the Balkans. Their size and implied wealth stay modest against gods like Gaulish Mercury, whose worship spread far wider. Speculation on the Lindow bog man of 1984 ties him to either Taranis or Teutates. Paul-Marie Duval printed the main study of the three gods in 1958. Miranda Green examined the Chester altar and the name Tanarus in 1982.

:::
```
