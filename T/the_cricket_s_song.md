The cricket's song was a sign of good luck in the old Irish farmhouse. Kevin Danaher set one chapter of his study of custom aside for the topic. The country people loved to hear a cricket sing from the hearth of the fireplace. They thought a house without this sound was not truly a happy one. A family that moved into a new building would catch a few crickets and bring them to the fresh fire. Nobody wished to harm the little creature; even the cat would not touch a cricket. An old saying warned that whoever killed or wounded one would lose the good fortune of the home. On that night the crickets would come out and eat holes through the clothing. The cricket knew, the folk said, the hand that had done it wrong. If a stranger caused the hurt, the crickets followed him back to his own house and roused his own crickets to punish it. But an accident that the family truly regretted was forgiven and nothing more came of it. Many people set a crust of bread or a boiled potato upon the hearth stone for the cricket's supper. When one cricket died the others held a solemn funeral for it. Four of the insects carried the body while the rest walked behind in a long line. These ways are retreating now, for the cricket can make no home in a wall of concrete. Far different was the creature they called the cockroach. The folk loathed it for its reek and its filth and killed it on sight. Even so it escaped constantly, for it shot away at the first flash of light. Boiling water was poured into the cracks where it hid, yet a few always lived on. The old people feared a lizard even more. A man who fell asleep outdoors on a warm day might open his mouth, they said, and a lizard would creep down inside him. Then nothing the man ate could ever fill his belly, for the lizard had taken hold. A clever woman could drive it out by keeping the man from food and then by hanging him over the fire. Only a few of the farms kept hives of bees, and the people feared the stings. If a member of the household died, someone went across to the hives to carry the sad news. A little black cloth was hung on a hive as the sign of their grief. When no one gave the news, the bees were sure to fly away. The brown bee stung harder than the big black bee; neither hurt as much as a wasp. The one creature that would go against the wasps was the hooded bird, which broke open their home for the honey. The ant stood to the children as the fine example of hard work. There they told the fable of the grasshopper that spent the warm days in dance and song and came to the ant in the cold to ask for help, only to be told to dance. A swarm of winged ants at dusk spoke to them of coming rain. The frog too gave a sign of the weather, for its skin turned yellow in dry air but dark when the rain drew near. The people would rather not kill a frog, for the frog was tied to the fairies. The flea could be the subject of a jest, but the louse and the bug were no jest at all. The flea drove for the deepest filth, and to name it was as bad as a horrible word. The butterfly, though it grew from a caterpillar that gnawed the cabbage leaves, was never destroyed. The children would hold it in a little cage and always set it free again. The spider was spared as well, for it caught the flies, and a spider that ran across the clothes promised new garments. A man who went abroad to a country of serpents should carry a stick grown from ash or hazel, for St Patrick had driven all the snakes out of Ireland. Thus the cricket's song brought the whole set of household creatures into the keeping of the old record.
