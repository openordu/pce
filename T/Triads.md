``` tab [group1:Info]
::: magazinestyle
The Triads are a Welsh and Irish literary form built on the mystic power of three. Poets and other bards composed them, and a triad lists three examples of a quality, a person, or a place. The gain was easy memory. Celtic learning loved triple groupings. The love met the need for memory aids. The two together produced a verse pattern for ordering traditions, precepts, and lore. Both Ireland and Wales used it. Most mentions of the Triads in the singular mean the great Trioedd Ynys Prydain. MacKillop dates the gathering of that collection to the twelfth century. Manuscripts from the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries preserve it. The Welsh triadic verse form englyn was known by the ninth century. Welsh triads therefore far outnumber the Irish ones. Collections from different manuscripts cover topics from native learning to poetry, law, and medicine. The triadic habit reaches deep into story. All four branches of the Mabinogi cite triadic lore again and again. The earliest Welsh order appears in a manuscript from the thirteenth to the fifteenth century. Later copies survive in the White Book and Red Book of Hergest. The lists name a broad body of native mythological and historical figures. Most of those stories do not survive as full narratives. The lists therefore reveal the scope of a tradition that now reaches us only in fragments. The largest Irish set is the Trecheng Breth Féne. It dates from about the ninth century. It holds 214 triads, plus a few nonads and longer entry lists. Some triads may come from oral tradition. Most seem to be the work of one author, probably a cleric. The collection opens with the Irish monasteries. The author knew the law texts, since forty-four of the sets deal with law. The work belongs to the general class of gnomic or wisdom literature. Kuno Meyer printed the Irish collection in Dublin in 1906. Rachel Bromwich edited the Welsh Triads, and her second edition appeared in Cardiff in 1978. Her commentary on the triads in Welsh literature and scholarship grew out of her 1969 study. The Welsh triads list the three splendid women of King Arthur's court. They also record the three sorrows of Ireland and many other groups. Scholars study the Triads for the myth material they hold. Each group binds its members by a single common trait. The device makes odd and ordinary things sit together. It turns scattered lore into a fixed, teachable set.
```
