``` tab [group1:Info]
::: magazinestyle
The tree stood at the heart of Celtic cosmology. Trees were far more than big woody plants. They were sacred objects of the highest rank. A tree spans many levels of reality, with roots hidden in the dark ground and reaching branches touching the sky. For this reason the tree stood for wisdom. In Irish the word for tree is fid, and the word for wise is fios. In Welsh the word is gwyb, and the wise one is gyweron. The sanctity of trees is recorded from the earliest writers. Tacitus wrote of the dense sacred groves of Anglesey, where the druid orders made their last stand. Medieval Irish Brehon law dealt with trees as it did with persons. It named seven noble trees, for which fines were paid if they were cut. The list runs from the oak and the holly. It carries on through the hazel, the apple, the birch, the alder, and the willow. The oak was the most sacred of all. The druids honored it and held the mistletoe that grows on it to be magical. Five great trees of Ireland were held especially strong. Coins are still placed in sacred trees at holy wells, though the metal can kill the tree. MacKillop's entry on tree also points readers to a Hiberno-English tale recorded by Jeremiah Curtin. Curtin published Gilla na Grakin and Fin MacCumhaill in his 1890 Myths and Folklore of Ireland. MacKillop further cites Carol T. Heffernan's 1982 Eire-Ireland study Combat at the Fountain, which traces the early Irish pursuit of the Gilla Decair. A. H. Krappe's 1932 article in Revue Celtique on the same pursuit rounds out the scholarly cross-references.

:::
```
