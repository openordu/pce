``` tab [group1:Info]
::: magazinestyle
O Donnchi Donnall na nGeimhleach held the headship of an O'Donoghue kindred in south Kerry. He is remembered chiefly as the ghost said to haunt Ross Castle at Killarney, and as a horseman who rides across the waters of the great lake on the first morning of every May. The extra title given to him, na nGeimhleach, was probably intended to mean of the fetters, although an older form of the phrase carries the sense of the slender steeds.
The person behind the stories seems to have been a chieftain of the twelfth century called Domhnall, a grandson of that Donndhach from whom the sept took its name. He was styled Domhnall ua Donndhchadha, a form that in later speech became the more familiar Donall O Donnchu. The belief that his spirit outlived his body may have grown from some family devotion that placed the O'Donoghues near the otherworld-deity Donn. Yet a great part of the legend now bound to him appears to have been carried over from the fame of the Earl of Desmond, whose rising belongs to the sixteenth century, and so the telling most likely came after that date. It is suggested that the O'Donoghue chieftain of the time joined the revolt and lost his lands and standing as a penalty.
The best-known of his stories places him at secret arts within a hidden room of Ross Castle. When one of his charms went awry, he fell under enchantment himself and today lies sleeping beneath the lake water. On each May morning he is seen riding out over the surface, his horse shod with shoes of silver, and when those shoes have worn away he is promised to come back. In the manner of the Desmond lord, he is reported from time to time to come back and shield the country people against cruel landlords. One tale gives that he handed the tenants the money to meet their pressing rents, whereupon the holder of the receipts found each coin had changed into leaves. A more wonderful story describes a later kinsman laying a wager with a lord of England on the outcome of a hurling match, and when his own side looked beaten, Donall himself entered the field and brought the victory.
These borrowed tales shaped him into the likeness of a fallen lord who rests beneath the lake and who will wake when the shoe of silver is spent. In that way he stands as a second Earl of Desmond for the southern lands, the keeper of the water whose name its people still speak on the May mornings.
```
