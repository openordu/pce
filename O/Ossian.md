Ossian is the fictional narrator of the Poems of Ossian (1760-3). James Macpherson presented this work as his translation of a lost Celtic epic. Macpherson built the character on Oisin, the poet-hero of Scottish Gaelic ballad tradition. During the late nineteenth century, popular retellings of Fenian tales preferred the name Ossian to Oisin. Macpherson makes this narrator the son of Fingal and Roscranna. He weds Everallin and fathers Oscar (3). Malvina, mistress to Oscar, kept the aged Ossian company. This Oscar (3) differs from Oscar (1), the Fenian hero of native tradition.

Macpherson worked as a Scottish 'translator' of the mid-eighteenth century. He grounded the poems partly in Gaelic oral tradition. He turned some Gaelic ballad passages into prose and invented many further 'translations'. The work came out serially in three books: Fragments of Ancient Poetry (1760), then Fingal (1762), then Temora (1763). Despite the title Poems, the text runs wholly in prose. It set itself up as a Celtic answer to Homer's Iliad. His cast plainly echoes Irish and Scottish Gaelic figures. Fingal stands for Fionn mac Cumhaill, and Darthula for Deirdre. The book electrified Europe across the late eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries. Translations appeared in every major European language. Napoleon, Goethe, Blake, Jefferson, Thoreau, and Brahms all numbered among its admirers. His work fed the Celtic Revival, a fashionably morbid and 'primitive' movement of the later eighteenth century. Thomas Gray, born in Wales, belonged to it. The vogue also spread the word 'bard' through Welsh and Irish usage.

Samuel Johnson, though he could not read Gaelic, saw through the fabrication at once. The Ossianic Controversy over authenticity began with the first publication and never wholly ended. The 'originals' he offered as evidence were forged. Even so, the fraud drew fresh attention to the storytelling of Ireland and Gaelic Scotland. The Poems also made the name Oscar famous far beyond Celtic lands.
