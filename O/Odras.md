``` tab [group1:Info]
::: magazinestyle
Odras is an Irish heroine whose legend survives in one stark scene. Her name appears in the variant forms Odhras and Odrus. Those spellings show scribes carrying the tale across generations. MacKillop 2008 keeps the entry brief. Odras is a hapless mortal woman. The Mórrígan worked a change on her. Odras became a pool of water by that goddess's power. That single fate carries the whole record, yet it ties Odras to a vivid pattern in Irish story. The base sources cite the Metrical Dindshenchas, the Royal Irish Academy's collection of placename poems. That collection preserves the landscape lore of early Ireland in verse. Such tales anchor a name to a feature of the land. Received lore treats such metamorphoses as origins: the woman passes into the water, and the water holds her memory. Tradition elsewhere records parallel endings. Received lore holds that Boand became the River Boyne and that Sinann became the Shannon. A mortal's death could found a great river. The pool of Odras belongs to that family of stories. In them, divine power turns a living body into enduring water. The wider tradition depicts the Mórrígan as a battle goddess, a figure of strife and sovereignty. Her favour and her fury both fell on mortals. Against such a being, Odras stands as the hapless human. She meets the goddess, she fails, and the encounter dissolves her into the pool. MacKillop gives her no dates and no kin. Monaghan's encyclopedia, a second base source, lists Odras among the heroines of Ireland. It preserves no further biography, so the transformation remains her only recorded deed. The brevity matters. Some figures of Irish legend carry long genealogies, battle rolls, and love stories. Odras survives as a name joined to a single act of divine power. Her story preserves an old way of seeing landscape. A pool could be a person, and a person could outlast death as water. Meanwhile a goddess remade the land with a gesture. Readers who seek her should look not to chronicle but to the water itself. There the Dindshenchas tradition located her fate.
```
