``` tab [group1:Info]
::: magazinestyle
Ogmios was a god of the continental Celts. In Gaul he ranked as a lord of eloquence and letters. One second-century text carries his story, and scattered inscriptions support it. The inscription record stays thin, so Lucian remains the main witness. Lucian of Samosata, a Greek satirist of the second century C.E., describes a painting from southern Gaul near Marseille. The picture shows a bald old man with dark skin. He leads a band of men who look glad and willing. Thin chains run from his tongue to their ears. Each follower stays attached by the ear, the channel that receives his speech. The glad faces show that eloquence, not force, holds each listener. The image stood for the hold of eloquence over its hearers. Lucian's informant equated Ogmios with Hercules, citing great strength as one reason. Hercules usually appears young and muscular, crowned with thick hair. The painting instead gave Ogmios the bow and club of Hercules. The bald head and aged body form a pointed contrast with that usual youth. For the Celts this aged figure was Trenfher, the strong man. His great age hints that eloquence ripens with years. Albrecht Dürer, a German Renaissance painter, depicted this deity. Scholars commonly pair Ogmios with the Irish god Ogma. In some tellings Ogmios carried the dead to the Otherworld, joined there with Dis Pater.
Letters fell under his care as much as spoken skill did.
The chain image drew studies of Irish and Welsh parallels.

:::
```
