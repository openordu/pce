::: magazinestyle
Owain Glyndŵr, also written Owen Glendower and known by Koch under the headword Owain Glyndßr, lived about 1354 to about 1416. He counts as the last Welsh-born prince of Wales recognised as such by his own people. He led the major armed rising of the Welsh against English rule, was seen even in his own day as a national redeemer, and grew into a banner of modern Welsh nationalism. On 16 September 1400 he was proclaimed Prince of Wales, a claim that rested on descent from the Powys royal house through his father and from the Deheubarth lineage through his mother. He read law in London at the Inns of Court, took part in Scotland's wars, and dwelt as a gentleman at Carrog and Sycharth. Both manors opened their doors to wandering bards, and Iolo Goch celebrates them in verse. His rebellion began in 1400. His war against English oppression, a struggle that nearly won Wales self-rule, and his vision of a Welsh parliament, a separate Welsh church, and a Welsh university lifted him into the central image of Welsh nationalism through much of the twentieth century. The group Meibion Glyndŵr, the Sons of Glyndŵr, took his name as their rallying cry. Since the National Assembly for Wales was created, he is celebrated less as a rebel fighter and more as a founding statesman whose project looks forward to a modern Wales within Europe.
