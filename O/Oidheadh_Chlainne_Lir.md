``` tab [group1:Info]
::: magazinestyle
Oidheadh Chlainne Lir is the Irish title for the late medieval romance, surviving in manuscripts from about 1500; English readers call it the tragic tale of Lir's children, and the tale ranks among the Three Sorrows of Storytelling. MacKillop 2008 recounts the whole arc: after defeat by the Milesians at Tailtiu, the Tuatha Dé Danann choose Bodb Derg as king, which offends Lir, whose wife dies leaving him four children. His second wife Aife (2), childless and obsessive with jealousy, transforms the children with a druidical wand into four white swans on Lough Derravaragh, dooming the four to nine hundred years in three sentences of increasing misery: three hundred years on that lake, three hundred crossing the Sea of Moyle that parts Ireland from Kintyre's Mull, and three hundred on the west coast between Erris and Inishglora. Since Finnguala herself requested the duration, her people's powers cannot lift it, though the children keep speech, song supreme among mortals, and their senses. In exile their singing draws crowds, then centuries of forgetfulness, until the disciple Mo Cháemóc of St Patrick carries Christianity to Inishglora and links the swans with a silver chain. The prophecy ends when Deoch of Munster marries Lairgnéan of Connacht, and the queen's demand for the singing swans breaks the spell: the children rise from the water as withered pillars of dust and are baptized in time to save their souls. The dictionary records a stone cross of the early period on Inishkeel and a thin folkloric afterlife, with modern fame resting on nineteenth-century anthologies such as P. W. Joyce's Old Celtic Romances.

:::
```
