``` tab [group1:Info]
::: magazinestyle
The otter held a dread place in the lore of Ireland. The Highland Scots held it too. It tied the creature to the realm of the dead. Folk tales named a king of the otters. They called him the Dorraghor or the Dobharch nigh. He led his colony through water. His pelt sold for a sum past any coin. A hunter learned that a white mark under the jaw was the only place a blade could bite. The rest of the hide turned every weapon. The man who killed that king paid the debt with his own life. The kin declared it so. A sliver of the pelt set near the door guarded the whole household. The same sign saved the soldiers at the rout of Culloden. Those men carried the hide as they marched. That same dread of the realm beyond runs through Welsh and Irish tale. In an old Welsh story, Arthur nearly loses his life. He tried to carry off the cauldron of plenty from the Welsh Otherworld of Annwfn. Irish legend keeps its own place for that dread. The name Dún Scáith means fort of shadow and fear. Tech Duinn serves as the house of Donn. Donn is the unnerving figure who welcomes the dead. The Welsh word Uffern now means hell. It probably grew from Christian hatred of pagan religion. Latin writers of the late twelfth century set down the word. Walter Map and Giraldus Cambrensis were those writers. The word comes from the Latin infernum. Folk tradition names several rulers of the Otherworld. They include Arawn, who contends with his rival Hafgan. Named lords include Cethem and Eochaid Iúil. In a separate district of the realm, Eógan Inbir appears with Bé Chuma at his side. The pair Labraid Luathlám with Li Ban rule another district. Mannanán mac Lir holds it together with Fand. Dáire stands with Rigru Rosclethan. Tethra rules in his own quarter. The craft-god Goibniu hosts a feast. The feast is known as Fled Goibnenn. Ale flows there without end.

:::
```
