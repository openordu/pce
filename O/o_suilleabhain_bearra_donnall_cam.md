O Suilleabhain Bearra Donnall Cam held the leadership of the O'Sullivan family of Beare in the west of Cork. He earned his island-wide fame through the long retreat carried out in the winter of 1602-1603, when he and his people broke clear of the Elizabethan forces. He marched out of Glengariff with above a thousand men, women and children, and for a whole fortnight this company moved toward the north under constant attack and cruel hardship. In the end Donall and the worn remainder of his band reached the stronghold of the O'Rourkes in Leitrim.A lively epic lore of west Munster keeps alive the heroic memory of Donnall. His nickname told of a 'crooked' man, for although he was tall and well made the stories maintain that he stood a shade bent, with bandy legs. They also say he was idle as a youth, and for all his parents could press him he would do no work. The brother of his mother presided over the allied O'Donovan of Carbery, and this uncle once descended upon O'Sullivan ground. When the party was drawing off the Beare peninsula, shoving a vast herd of cattle and laden with stolen goods, Donnall's mother rebuked him for lying stretched by the hearth while his own people were set upon. Infuriated, the youth jumped up and caught his father's blade. One sword snapped, a second broke likewise, but a third was strong and held fast, and with this he went out to confront the raiders in the field. The O'Donovan had kept his sworn word that he would know no ease until his palm touched the far edge of Beare, so Donnall hewed that hand off and afterwards killed him. To discharge the old promise he carried the hand to the very last projection of the shore and set it there.The ballad versions of the business meet with a little confirmation in the annals, although these describe a different raid. The records report that in the year fifteen eighty-one a son who bore the chief's own name, rather than the man himself, invaded Beare at the prompting of the English. Donnall, then twenty years of age, drew together fifty men, and falling upon the raiders folk slew many hundreds of them and their captain besides, who was his own cousin.When the Irish had been beaten at Kinsale in sixteen hundred and one the O'Sullivan held out for another year against an overwhelming enemy, and after James the First had reached the throne he went to England with other Irish leaders and sought the return of his lands. The suit came to nothing, so he slipped away unnoticed to Cork and from that port sailed to Spain with his people. Such was his skill at eluding pursuers that folk opinion gave him the secret of vanishing from sight, a power he was said to have found in a hut beside the shore. He slept there one night, the tale runs, and a voice came to him in his slumber, telling him of a betrayal that had already been carried out, that English soldiers now stood all around the cabin, yet that a herb resting on the board, once laid on his tongue, would make him unseen. He followed the advice, stole out, slew the dumbfounded troops and got away unharmed.Once he was in Spain King Philip received him with honour, and folk memory had its own grand painting of the meeting. So the story goes, he pushed past the king's own escort and entered the royal presence unbidden, and when Philip demanded an explanation Donnall replied that his sword was his passport. A great slab of brass stood in the court which no champion had ever been able to divide with a single stroke, and the king bade Donnall put it to the test. Cast a terrible glance about the company, he burst himself aloft and brought the blade down upon the block with such force that the brass split in halves. His servant then threw water over him, and when the king asked the reason Donnall told him he had driven himself into such a heat to summon all his force that, while it stayed so hot, every person in that hall was in danger of him. Feats of that kind are reported of several of the other Irish lords who could split broad slabs with a single blow, so the theme was a stock device of folk saga.Donnall Cam laboured hard to obtain Spanish help for a new campaign in Ireland; like other Irish chiefs abroad, his affairs were watched closely by English intelligence, and this unease shows up in folk tales of attempts to cut him down. The popular account blames his death on the greed of his own servant. As Donnall bent to tie his shoe his cloak rose and left a chink in his armour, through which the man pierced him. In that way he came to be spoken of as Donnall Cam struck low by treachery in Spain. The true record of his end was equally grim. Outside a Madrid church he was run through and left dead. He had earlier advanced money to an English acquaintance, John Bath, and that debt led to sharp words between him and a younger kinsman of the chief, Philip O'Sullivan Beare, who was Bath's nephew. Two of Donnall's retainers strove to part the pair, yet Bath lunged suddenly at the unsuspecting chief and stabbed him through the neck, killing him outright.
