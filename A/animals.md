``` tab [group1:Info]
::: magazinestyle
In Continental and insular Celtic myth, gods and goddesses appear with animals and birds. Certain creatures kept their links from the oldest Celtic times at Hallstatt and La Tène, in both iconography and storytelling. Nehalennia and Cúchulainn stand with the dog, Sequana with the duck. Cernunnos stands with the stag, Moccus with the pig, and Epona with the horse. Writers often call the animal the god's servant. It is better to say the god appears in the animal's form. In some tales shape-shifting is part of the character. Gods and heroes transformed themselves at will into beasts. The Cailleach appears as a hare, and Oísín's mother gave birth to him while in the shape of a deer. In Gaul, Roman rule left names and figures but no myths. So there is no story of the bond between beast and god. Poets could also change shape. The poets Amairgin and Tuan mac Cairill say they lived many lives in different bodies, including those of animals. It may show shape-shifting, or the passing of a soul from one body to the next.

Celtic Christianity carried the closeness forward. Some saints, such as Pol of Brittany, held power over the animals. Others, like Ailbe and Bairre, were said to be suckled by wolves. Witches were thought to become hares. People offered animals in sacrifice, and the bull was sacred. Some beasts, such as the hare, the hen, and the goose, were usually not eaten. Yet they could be killed as offerings. The queen Boudicca sacrificed a hare to read the future. Taboos could follow belief in descent from a divine beast. King Conaire, whose mother was a bird, could not hunt or eat fowl. Families such as the O'Flahertys and the Coneelys were said to descend from seals. Tribal names could name an animal kin, as the Bribroci, the beavers of Britain.

The boar stood at the head of the Celtic iconographic beasts from the earliest age. The Continental Celts gave their highest esteem to the horned, stag-like god Cernunnos. A whole chapter of divine beasts appears in the art of pagan Britain. Anne Ross catalogued that art at length in her study of 1967. The wide list runs from badger and bear through bull, cat, and cock. It continues through cow, dog, otter, ram, and stag to the swan. Birds and fish carry their own entries in the same lore.
```
