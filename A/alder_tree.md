``` tab [group1:Info]
::: magazinestyle
The alder tree is a sacred tree of the Celts. It belongs to the genus Alnus, a shrub or tree of the birch family. It usually grows in wet ground, and its branches carry tiny cones and dangling catkins at the same time. The Celts read that double bearing as a sign that the seasons of past and future meet. They tied the tree to divination and to foretelling, and the link lasted into early modern Scotland. The wood also served the diagnosing of disease. The alder or the yew supplied the fé. That rod measured the bodies and burial pits of Ireland before Christianity. The letter F, the third consonant of the ogham alphabet, took its name from the alder. In the old tongue the alder was fern, a word still echoed by Irish fearnóg.

Fresh alder wood turns from white to red, as a wound does. The old Celts saw the change as a living spirit. So they made the felling of an alder a kind of murder. Law once punished the cutting, and folk practice still avoids it.

The tree stands close to the god Bran. At Cad Goddeu, the tree fight, Gwydion named Bran from the alder twigs he held. An old Taliesin riddle asks why the alder is purple. The answer is that Bran wore purple. His alder may stand for resurrection. The name Gwern, the boy of Matholwch and Branwen, means alder. The Fairy folk hid their theft of cattle behind alder boughs, leaving the branches in the place of the beasts. In Wales the hero Brân the Blessed carried an alder branch, and the poet Gwydion knew him by it. Place-name lore keeps the link too. The Irish district of Farney means the plain of the alder. Tongues across the Celtic world name the tree in one family of words, from Irish fearnóg to Welsh gwemen. The tree thus stands at the meeting point of god story, folk law, and language history.

Modern readers meet the same lore in place and speech. Woods of the species still favour Irish riverbanks, as they did in the lore's own day. The old awe survives as custom more than creed. Carpenters prize the wood, and country speech keeps the old names alive. Death in the red sap and rebirth in the purple dye gave the Bran cult its double emblem. Its lore binds the tree to kings, poets, and the fairy race at once.

:::
```
