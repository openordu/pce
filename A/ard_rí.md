``` tab [group1:Info]
::: magazinestyle
Ard rí is an Irish social role of the high king. Irish forms include ard rí, ard righ, and ardrig. Many Irish narratives show the ard ri ruling all Ireland from Tara, where he receives his crown. The question of the power of the king of Tara over other Irish kings is far from settled. A king drew power from his tuath, his people and their land. He drew further power from union with the goddess of the land. The king of Tara held no central political authority. He held a ceremonial and religious office. He could not command other kings to follow his orders. He kept Ireland fertile by following his sacred vows and taboos, his buada and geasa. The term does not denote a national sovereign or an over-king. It functioned as an honorific title for powerful local leaders. Countless legends and pseudo-histories push the title back to the earliest times. The early Irish law corpus, the Brehon Laws, does not support that claim. Francis J. Byrne argued in 1973 that the title was pure political fiction. He saw it as propaganda that served the claims of the Meath dynasty seated at Tara. Other commentators accept an early sacral kingship at Tara, less grand than later narratives imply. The first claimant to the title was Máel Sechnaill, who died in 862. Romantic tradition knows him as Malachy I. Figures known as ard rí include Diarmait mac Cerbaill, who died in 565. Sources treat him as the last monarch of pre-Christian Ireland. A later Máel Sechnaill, who died in 1022, ruled last without unbroken opposition from powerful local princes. Ruaidri Ua Conchobair of Connacht, called Rory O'Connor, held the title last at the Anglo-Norman Conquest of 1169. Many high kings figure in myth, including Niall of the Nine Hostages, Conn of the Hundred Battles, and Conaire.
```
