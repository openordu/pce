``` tab [group1:Info]
::: magazinestyle
The apple is the most magical fruit to the Celts and a central symbol in myth, legend, and folklore. It stands for fruitfulness, harmony, immortality, abundance, and love. It serves as a talisman of good fortune and prosperity and sometimes as a means to immortality. The Old English word names the pome fruit and its tree, Pyrus malus. The fruit hides in the word Avalon, the Arthurian Otherworld. Certain medieval narratives of Welsh attribution give Avalon the name Insula Pomorum, meaning Isle of Apples. One gloss on Emain Ablach, the magical Irish island, yields Emain of the Apples. In the Ulster Cycle the soul of king Cú Roí rested in an apple inside a salmon's stomach. That salmon returned once every seven years. A rolled apple let Cúchulainn gain his escape when he followed its path. From the grave of Ailinn, the tragic lover, there grew an apple tree. In Echtrae Conli, the Irish adventure tale, a fairy lover fed an apple to Connla of the Golden Hair. Connla was the son of Conn. The Irish call such a lover a leannán sidhe. For a full month that apple fed him food and drink without any decrease. It also stirred his longing for that woman and for her beautiful land of women. Oidheadh Chlainne Tuireahn is a Mythological Cycle story. Its first task sends the Sons of Tuireann after the Hesperides' Apples. The tale offers Hibernia as an alternative name for those Apples. Afallennau, a twelfth-century Welsh narrative poem about apple trees, deals with Myrddin. Konorin, a Breton pseudo-saint, won rebirth by means of an apple. Druids made wands from apple wood or from yew wood. In the Cotswolds of Britain, an apple tree in blossom out of season foretold coming death. Irish forms run from Old Irish uball and ubull to Modern Irish ubhal and úll. Scots Gaelic gives ubhall, Manx ooyl, Welsh afal, Cornish aval, and Breton aval. Scholars connect the word apple with the god Apollo. They derive the form Apellon from the same source as the word apple.

:::
```
