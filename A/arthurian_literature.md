``` tab [group1:Info]
::: magazinestyle
Arthurian literature names the body of writing about King Arthur and his court. In Wales it holds the earliest native stratum of the legend. Arthur already leads the British kings in the ninth-century Historia Brittonum. He appears there as the victor of twelve named battles against English settlers. Medieval Welsh poetry also celebrates him. Culhwch ac Olwen, a prose tale written about 1100, gives the fullest early portrait of his enterprising heroism. It lists his close comrades Cai, Bedwyr, and Gwalchmai and describes his court at Celliwig in Cornwall. A still older poem, Preiddiau Annwfn, records Arthur's raid on the Otherworld to free a prisoner and win a treasure. Only seven of his men return. This heroic image contrasts with a negative one found in some saints' lives and didactic verse, where Arthur behaves as an arrogant tyrant. Geoffrey of Monmouth then reshaped the figure into an emperor and chivalric king. French romance, led by Chrétien de Troyes, spread this courtly Arthur across Europe. Welsh writers adopted these foreign models in their own romances and their renderings of the Grail. The matter proved so potent that it survived into the early modern age. It drew other heroes' names into itself and bound them to landmarks in the landscape. 

:::
```
