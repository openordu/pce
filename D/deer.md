``` tab [group1:Info]
::: magazinestyle
The deer was a symbolic animal for the Celts. They saw the swift woodland herds as the wild form of their own cattle herds. Cattle followed a strong bull, and deer followed a virile stag. The two animals were tied to gods of masculine power, Esus for cattle and Cernunnos for the stag. The doe and the cow ran in parallel. Their goddesses Flidais and Bóand both stood for maternal love and abundance. Deer offspring appear in myth in the story of the transformed Sadb. She gave birth to her son Oisín the bard. Oisín's name still means 'fawn' in Irish. In the Scottish Highlands, giant goddesses tended vast deer herds. The island of Jura was home to the Seven Big Women who ran with the deer. The shape-shifting Cailleach Beinne Bhric could take the form of a gray deer. MacKillop 2008 adds the worship record. J. G. MacKay reported a deer cult in early Scotland. It centred on the Lochaber region of the Highlands. Deer commonly drew heroes away into the country of the gods. Sálbuide, a Munster king's son, died in a deer chase. The party lost thirty warriors, thirty attendants, and thirty deer-hounds. A jealous woman turned one hundred girls into deer in another Irish story. Mortals and fairies alike could be turned into deer. Lugaid Laigde, king of the Érainn, chased a fawn. That fawn was the divine shape of Ireland herself. The shape-shifter Mongán took the form of a deer. Aige went through a change into a fawn. Pryderi and Gilfaethwy lived a year as a stag and a hind inside the fourth Mabinogi branch. Their fawn was named Hyddwn. Sadb lay with Fionn mac Cumhaill while shaped as a deer. Their son Oisín carries the name little fawn. The Irish goddess Flidais drove a chariot pulled by deer. Tuan mac Cairill held the kingship of the deer in Ireland. An early Christian prayer-poem credited to St Patrick is The Deer's Cry, also called St Patrick's Breastplate. It tells how the saint got away from his enemies while he went about shaped as a deer. MacKillop reads it as a Christian use of the druidic power féth fiada. The Old Irish word for deer was fiad. Modern Irish keeps fia and fiadh, Scottish Gaelic fiadh, Manx feeaih, Welsh carw, Cornish carow, and Breton karv.
```
