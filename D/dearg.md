::: magazinestyle
The word dearg is the Modern Irish form of red, and it descends from Old Irish derg. MacKillop records it in a short lexical article rather than a long narrative one. His article defines dearg as the modern Irish word for red. It sends the reader on to Old Irish derg as the older written form. The colour range of the term ran wide. Old Irish derg implied a ruddy shade, the red of blood, and the red of flame. It also covered the tawny orange of ale and of gold. The same hue reached into ritual, naming the red ale of sovereignty poured for Irish kings. Within the lore of Ireland the plain colour word also names a mythic figure of dark power.

The name Dearg preserves the older form Derg. On its own it signifies little more than the hue. Tradition nevertheless bound the figure to slaughter and to the approach of death. The surviving records attach no single settled personality to him. By the literary age the name had hardened into a narrative device. An author reached for it to fill a tale with dread. The tale of the hostel of Da Dearga shows the device at full strength. It was in that hostel that the high king Conaire met his bloody end. MacKillop indexes the story under its Old Irish title, Togail Bruidne Da Derga. The title marks the tale as a destruction story. The text itself addresses Da Dearga by the shorter title Derg. It reads the opening element da as a shortened form of dia, the Old Irish word for god. Three horsemen, each called Derg, arrive before the disaster and presage the king's death. They ride the horses of Donn, the god of the dead. MacKillop places that god's house, Tech Duinn, on a rocky island off south-west Ireland. The tale pairs the house of Derg with the house of Donn. The matching phrases argue a single master behind both houses. These signs tie Dearg to the far better known death-god Donn. Dearg served as the death-god's name wherever a violent fate impended. Leinster storytellers treated it as their customary name for Donn.

In several passages Dearg serves as an epithet beside the name Daire. Daire shares the role of presiding partner of the Daghdha. Donn, Daire, and the Daghdha form a fellowship of ancestral powers. Dearg belongs to that company as both a title and a presence. Inside the tradition of Fionn mac Cumhaill he turns into an enemy. He stands there as a power from the other world. That hostility grows from the old contrast between dark Donn and bright Find, the prototype from whom Fionn descends.

An eighth-century written form preserves one further tale. It gives Dearg a servant named Derg Corra. The story keeps Derg Corra in company with a blackbird, a trout, and a stag. Beasts of the air, the water, and the land attend him there. Scholars compare that trio with the horned Cernunnos of the continent. Deer imagery runs through the same tradition. A doe came to Fionn, and the daughter of the other world wore that shape.

MacKillop's own article for dearg holds no myth of its own. It is a lexical note that defines the word and issues cross-references. It prints no tale, no genealogy, and no cult. It closes by sending the reader to two further articles. Both point onward inside the same letter of the alphabet. One names dáir derg, another compound built on the colour word. The other treats the lake called Lough Derg. The lake name keeps the old colour word on the map of Ireland. A reader who wants the figure must follow the cross-references. The brevity of the entry measures the distance between vocabulary and myth. The dictionary preserves the hue. The older lore preserves the dread.
