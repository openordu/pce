::: magazinestyle
Danebury was an Iron Age hill-fort on the chalk uplands of southern England. Its inner earth wall enclosed 5.3 hectares. Excavations ran on the site every year from 1969 to 1988. They cleared away over half of the occupied ground. Work began in the late sixth century BC as a palisaded enclosure. A rampart and ditch later replaced the palisade. That wall carried doors to the east and the south-west. A steep glacis joined the defenses during the fourth century BC. Danebury held dense occupation in the third and second centuries BC. Ramparts, round houses, and storage pits filled the interior. Two shrines stood near the centre of the settlement. Special deposits of grain, animals, and tools sat in old pits. The finds point to a farming economy. Salt, iron, bronze, and quern-stones arrived through exchange. The fort lost its settlers in the early first century AD.

:::
