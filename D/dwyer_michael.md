The story of Dwyer Michael, better remembered today as Michael Dwyer, is that of a figure born in 1772 who rose to prominence as a leader in the uprising of 1798 in County Wicklow. When that rebellion collapsed, he refused to submit, and for five more years he defied the English authorities from the hidden fastnesses of his own mountain county. Eventually he won terms of surrender that struck him as tolerable. Taken afterwards to Dublin, he passed two years in prison there, and in due course agreed to be transported to Australia, choosing to spend the closing chapter of his life in that far land.

As a partisan commander Dwyer was brave, inventive and strikingly capable, and the public exalted him into a grand popular idol. Legends multiplied around his quickness and his readiness in slipping free of his pursuers. He delighted in disguise and could baffle the redcoats in a dozen varied ways. Once, tradition tells us, he threw a whole troop into full flight merely by planting hats upon hayricks, so that any watcher would suppose a great company of fighters stood beside him. And when every clever deception failed him, his superb running and swimming carried him away to safety.
Much of the lore that encrusted his name carried a desperate, wry comedy. Soldiers are once said to have ringed the cottage where he was lodging in the Glen of Imaal. With no better escape open, he crept into the piggery and hid behind a big, fierce sow. The troops opened the sty to peer in, and the brute reared onto her hind legs and lashed out at them. He shoved her onward at the visitors, and the bewildered troops finally concluded that nobody could remain alongside such an ill-tempered beast, and marched off.
Climbing was likewise among his arts, for he scaled steep slopes to exhaust his enemies and to hurry his own passage. The wild landscape even seemed to side with the ragged hero. One tale describes how he passed a night inside a cavern on Lugnaquilla Mountain. On waking to a lovely clear morning, he began to light his pipe, and a robin darted inside and stirred up his blanket. When he made to seize the bird it vanished, then returned with a fierce air as though set on striking him. Going to the cave mouth and peering out, he saw the hillside turned red with close-packed soldiers. He pulled free his mighty sea-whistle and blew a loud blast, whereupon those soldiers, certain he was summoning a large body to fall upon them, scattered and ran. Dwyer himself wasted nothing, and hurried down the far slope of the mountain, grateful to the small bird for its timely warning.
So desperately did the crown crave his capture that a whole new road, permitting troops to be moved swiftly, was driven through the Wicklow Mountains. Called the Military Road, it ran southwards right across that range, yet proved of no use toward snaring him. The legendary pursuit was recalled by Luke Cullen, Charles Dickson and Padraig O Tuathail, each of whom took up his fame in later years.
