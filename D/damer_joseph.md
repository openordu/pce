::: magazinestyle
Filed in this collection under the heading damer joseph, the subject was born in 1630 and made his name as an English businessman who crossed over to Ireland in 1662 and acquired sweeping tracts of country from the settlers of the Cromwellian period in County Tipperary. He covered his new holdings with flocks of sheep and built up a highly profitable trade shipping wool abroad. He likewise opened a banking concern in Dublin and grew so wealthy that even the English crown found itself owing him enormous sums.

The name carried a reputation for stinginess. Dean Swift, who held him in low regard, remarked that he padded about in a shabby cloak and took his meals at the expense of other people. He wedded no one, and when he died in 1720 he divided his riches between two nephews, granting the Roscrea lands to his namesake Joseph and his dwelling with the grounds surrounding it at Shronell to John. The will came to close on half a million pounds, a staggering figure in that age, and out of it grew the enduring phrase "as rich as Damer."
Wonder and guesswork bred a mass of tales about the way he had first amassed his fortune. He began his working days as a clerk under Cromwell, and folks said he had chanced upon the hoard of one of the monasteries dissolved in Ireland, where the monks had tucked the wealth into barrels smeared over with tallow. It was imagined that Damer, having worked as a chandler, bought these tallow barrels and, to his astonished joy, uncovered their contents. When Joseph passed away the duty of archetypal magnate fell to nephew John, who took a wife in 1726. The match went badly, and rumour insisted his bride fled with an army officer; this Damer too left no children. About 1740 he started to raise an enormous house at Shronell, of which only part of the wall survives, and it boasted supposedly a window for each of the year's three hundred and sixty-five days.
Tales of John carried a strong streak of farce. He was said to wander in tatters while dressing his servants magnificently for show, and sometimes came disguised before his own staff, entreating them for money. A concealed chamber, locked behind heavy bolts, guarded an astonishing store of golden sovereigns he loved to contemplate and let slip tenderly through his fingers, alone for hours at a time. A story circulated, truly a form of one of Aesop's fables (Humorous Tales, Type 1305A), of a roaming tailor who offered to cut him a fine suit and asked modest payment of a single glance at the gold. Pleased with such a bargain, the magnate accepted, and when the garment was done he paraded the tailor before the coins. Asked what benefit he drew from merely looking, the tailor answered that the sight helped him exactly as much as it did Damer.
When John died in 1768 his huge fortune went on to another nephew, Joseph again by name, and that man's son was a hopeless spendthrift who squandered the inheritance on gambling, drink and costly apparel. The pattern of futility stood complete, and legend kept heaping up its stories of the gold of "great Damer," in truth a composite figure joined together from the many odd members of the clan. In Munster tradition all manner of acquisitive motifs latched onto the name. A well-known international folktale recounts a trickster contracting for as many sovereigns as his hat could hold, and Damer was said to have made just such a pact with the devil over his soul. The crafty man cheated the fiend by cutting a hole in the hat and laying it over a gap in the floor, beneath which lay a great chamber, so the coins went on pouring down until the exasperated devil quit the bargain and let him keep the lot.
One more tale made Damer a penniless rat-catcher taken on by a wealthy woman to clear her house, who stole a great many sovereigns by planting them within the corpses of the rats she disposed of. The ultimate dissolution of his riches drew curiosity too, for jackdaws were said to slip into the vault and carry off the gleam of metal bit by bit, so that timber-cutters nearby reportedly grew rich from what the birds lined their nests with. Some reason exists to think the accusation of cold-hearted greed was undeserved in the instance of every single namesake. Still, the mighty fortune stood out sharply against the plain circumstances of daily life then, a dramatic wonder and perfect stuff for folk memory, which loves to give ideas a solid shape.
