::: magazinestyle
dyn hysbys is a Welsh phrase whose literal sense is wise man, and the phrase functions as a title rather than as a personal name. Across Wales it remains the most common word for a wizard, and the title is attested in many districts. Welsh tradition holds that the soul of the druid, judged neither fit for Christian heaven nor fit for hell, migrates into a dyn hysbys form. He can see what lies hidden and bring that hidden knowledge to others, with a particular focus on events touching love and death. Those powers extend to everyday matters, such as recovering lost money or helping a Welshman escape from an English gaol. The dyn hysbys is also credited with the power to break spells, undoing the harm that witches and others have wrought. He heals sick animals and sick people through charms and incantations. His powers work most strongly on the threshold days when the spirit world draws close to the human world, including Calan Mai, Midsummer Day, and the eve of winter. Welsh tradition recognises three kinds of dyn hysbys: the clergy, scholars of esoteric books, and those who received the art by descent. Welsh tradition also names the wizard by consuriwr (conjurer), dewin (magician), and swynwr (charmer). The names of many dyn hysbys survive in Welsh record, and a few were renowned beyond their own time and place.

:::
