``` tab [group1:Info]
::: magazinestyle
Derbfine meant "true kin" in early Irish law. It named the closest kin group. The group held the descendants of the same great-grandfather. Four generations of a family formed this circle. The system ran in early Ireland and in Gaelic Scotland. These kin shared land. No member could sell that land without the consent of the others. The group carried joint responsibility for wrongs. It answered for the honor price, called the éric, for injuries its members caused. The fine was often paid in cows. The derbfine was the normal property-owning unit in that society. It also served as the unit for dynastic succession. Any male of a royal derbfine could succeed a king, whether a son, an uncle, a brother, or a nephew. Primogeniture did not prevail in this system. Property and power did not pass automatically to the eldest legitimate male. Scholars treat the derbfine as the core of kingship and inheritance in early Ireland.

:::
```
