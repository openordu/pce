``` tab [group1:Info]
::: magazinestyle
Dindshenchas is an Irish mythological text. The name means lore of high places, and later usage writes it dinnsheanchas. These texts describe myths tied to places in the landscape, showing how a river, hill, fort, or plain received its name. The poems form a body of place-lore. Koch 2006 explains that part of the lore grew directly out of mythology, such as the catalogue of landscape features found at the close of Táin Bó Cuailnge, born from the fatal duel of two supernatural bulls. In an earlier scene the hero Cú Chulainn, standing on a hill, has his charioteer name the chief forts he can see between Tara and Kells. Such a triangulation of Ireland mattered to a society that needed to know where one local kingdom, or tuath, ended and its neighbour began, since ordinary people lost legal protection outside their own territory. Hills often marked those boundaries, and people chose them for ritual and assembly. Poets who had finished eight years of training were expected to narrate the traditional stories and say how each place had come by its name. Koch 2006 names Mac Liacc, Cinaed ua hArtacáin, and Cúán ua Lothcháin as authors of dindshenchas poems, and notes that an 11th-century compilation shows up in many important Irish manuscripts. Edward Gwynn edited the metrical version in five volumes, and Whitley Stokes edited anonymous prose pieces from different collections. The later editions were planned to hold verse and prose together. Koch 2006 observes that the stories often touch the mythological tradition and the folk of the síd, that references to the Ulster sagas and to the tales of Finn mac Cumaill outnumber those to the Kings' Cycle, and that a few saints appear in them, among them Colum Cille and Patrick. Many poems end with religious stanzas that praise the coming of Christianity. Place-name stories kept circulating in Irish oral lore into the modern era. The date of the earliest form is unknown, and the Dindshenchas may have been recited aloud long before the twelfth-century Book of Leinster and other medieval manuscripts gave it written form. Many ancient Irish stories reach us only through this text.
```
