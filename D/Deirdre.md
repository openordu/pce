``` tab [group1:Info]
::: magazinestyle
Deirdre (also Derdriu or Deridriu) was an Irish heroine. People called her "Deirdre of the Sorrows." Her tale ranks among the Three Sorrows of Ireland. The other two are the Children of Lir and the Sons of Tuireann. Deirdre was born during a feast. Her father Fedlimid mac Daill held the feast for Concobar mac Nessa, king of Ulster. The druid Cathbad foretold her fate. She would grow into the most beautiful woman Ireland had seen, he said. Her beauty would destroy the kingdom. Concobar chose to rear her as his prize. He gave her to Leborcham at Emain Macha. One day Deirdre saw a raven drink the blood of a calf on fresh snow. She wanted a man with hair like the raven, skin like snow, and lips like blood. That man was Noísiu, son of Uisneach and a nephew of the king. Deirdre and Noísiu fled with his brothers Ardán and Ainnle. They crossed to Scotland and lived near Loch Etive. Concobar lured them back to Ireland. On landing, Noísiu and his brothers were killed. Deirdre reproached the king for the deed. MacKillop 2008 sets out the deeper record. Her Irish tale is Longas mac nUislenn, the banishment of the sons of Uisnech. It survives in the twelfth-century Book of Leinster and the Yellow Book of Lecan. It stands as a fore-tale, the rémscél, before the Táin Bó Cuailnge. The story stayed popular in the oral tradition of Ireland and Scotland for centuries. Within the Ulster Cycle she is not only tragic and beautiful. She acts as a catalyst who brings vengeance and ruin on the warriors of the province. The gloss trouble-er does not hold. The name may be only a diminutive of der, meaning daughter or girl. Concobar spared the child at birth and fostered her at a distant stronghold in the care of Leborcham. He planned to make her his wife when she grew up. Deirdre answered the young man's greeting with a heifer-and-bull exchange, and the lovers eloped. In earlier texts the exiles travelled with one hundred and fifty warriors. They also had one hundred and fifty women and one hundred and fifty hounds. A local king in Scotland later demanded Deirdre for his wife. Conchobar sent the spy Gelbann, and Noísiu put out his eye with a fidchell piece. The king then sent Fergus mac Róich as a surety of safe conduct. Deirdre feared deceit but consented, singing the famous farewell to Alba before she boarded the boat. Conchobar used a trick to part Fergus from the lovers. His men killed Noísiu and his brothers. They also killed the sons of Fergus who had stayed behind. They spared Deirdre. She lived a year at Emain Macha and never smiled. She kept her head bowed on her knee. She rebuked the king for killing all she held dear. He gave her to Eógan mac Durthacht, the man she hated most. While riding in Eógan's chariot she leapt out and struck her head on a stone. Other versions have her stab herself and throw the knife into the sea. Oral tradition buries the lovers side by side at Armagh. Two yew stakes driven through the graves grew and intertwined. Conchobar's best warriors deserted him for Ailill and Medb of Connacht after his treachery. They fought against him in the Táin, and so Cathbad's prophecy at her birth came true. Deirdre's story drew a long line of Anglo-Irish retellings. Ferguson, Joyce, de Vere, W. Sharp, Trench, Lady Gregory, G. W. Russell, W. B. Yeats, Synge, Stephens, and Coulter all reshaped it. Moireen Fox recast her as a redeeming shape of love in The Fire-Bringers of 1920. H. V. Fackler traced the whole tradition in That Tragic Queen. A ruined fort stands at Inverfarigaig by Loch Ness. It once carried the name Dún Dèarduil, Deirdre's fortress.
```
