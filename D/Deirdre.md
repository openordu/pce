``` tab [group1:Info]
::: magazinestyle
Deirdre (also Derdriu or Deridriu) was an Irish heroine. People called her "Deirdre of the Sorrows." Her story ranks with the two other great sorrows of Ireland, the Children of Lir and the Sons of Tuireann. Koch 2006 records her as the beautiful daughter of the storyteller Feidlimid mac Daill, and as the focus of a tragic love triangle with the young hero Noísiu mac Uislenn, with Conchobar the ruler of Ulaid. Koch also explains her name in Longas mac nUislenn, where the druid Cathbad hears the unborn girl cry out in the womb and takes her cry for a portent, a verb otherwise unattested that probably relates to the Old Irish words for noise and murmuring. Her story was born during a feast. Her father Fedlimid mac Daill held the feast for Concobar mac Nessa, king of Ulster. The druid Cathbad foretold her fate. She would grow into a woman of unmatched beauty, he said, without equal in Ireland. Her beauty would destroy the kingdom. Concobar chose to rear her as his prize. He gave her to Leborcham at Emain Macha. One day Deirdre saw a raven feeding on a calf's blood spread over fresh snow. She wanted a man with hair like the raven, skin like snow, and lips like blood. That man was Noísiu, son of Uisneach and a nephew of the king. Deirdre and Noísiu fled with his brothers Ardán and Ainnle. They crossed to Scotland and lived near Loch Etive. Concobar lured them back to Ireland. On landing, Noísiu and his brothers were killed. Deirdre reproached the king for the deed. Koch 2006 places the earliest surviving version of her story in the Old Irish saga Longas Mac nUislenn, the tale of how the sons of Uisliu went into exile, and he counts her fate among the three great sorrows of Irish storytelling, which the Children of Lir and the Sons of Tuireann share with her. He compares her unhappy love with that of Gráinne and Diarmaid, of Tristan and Isolt, and of the Galatian priestess Camma. The tale stayed popular in the Gaelic world, and Koch 2006 credits this popularity with the widespread use of the name Deirdre today. J. M. Synge, writing in English, staged his version of Deirdre of the Sorrows in 1910, and Alexander Carmichael published an oral Scottish Gaelic telling of the tale.
```
