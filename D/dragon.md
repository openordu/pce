``` tab [group1:Info]
::: magazinestyle
The dragon is a monstrous beast of medieval legend. It breathes fire, bears wings, and eats maidens. MacKillop gives it a strong place in the Welsh imagination and a weaker one elsewhere in Celtic letters. Wales held snakes. Ireland held none. That split may explain the imbalance. In Ireland St. Patrick fought and killed three monstrous beings: Corra, Caoranach, and Oillipheist. Storytellers called these creatures serpentine even though snakes never lived in Ireland. Dragon tales appear most often in Wales, a nation symbolized by a red dragon. The red dragon is Y Ddraig Goch. A separate word, gwiber, names the viper-like dragon in other contexts. The Welsh king who became Arthur's father bore the provocative name Uther Pendragon, which may mean dragon's head. MacKillop reads the agnomen as a mark of the beast's fine qualities.

Dragons usually embody elemental power, and often the power of the earth. In Celtic tales a dragon haunts the depths of lakes or stands guard over trees. With the spread of Christianity, the dragon came to represent paganism. The story of St George made that reading famous. The word worm once meant dragon in Norse and Saxon speech. These dragon stories have no certain Celtic origin. Interpreters often read them as a coded tale about early Christians wiping out pagan beliefs.

MacKillop adds story detail. Two dragons harass Britain every Calan Mai midnight in the tale of Lludd and Llefelys. Lludd entombs them at Dinas Emrys. At the opening of the Cattle Raid of Fráech, the Irish hero Fráech slays one of these beasts. Another dragon swallows Dáire, son of Fionn, and the boy escapes. The great beast Oillipheist fled from St Patrick and cut the Shannon valley in its flight.

:::
```
