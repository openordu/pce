``` tab [group1:Info]
::: magazinestyle
A dwarf is a folkloric figure of small stature. Dwarfs are little people who appear across most Celtic lands. Folk tradition treats them as immigrants from Scandinavia or Germany. There they resembled common characters like trolls. MacKillop stresses that not every small adult in Celtic literature is a dwarf. Many beings of fairy land stand smaller than mortals, yet they are not dwarfs. Non-fairy dwarfs remain rare in the Celtic world. Norse and Arthurian traditions record such figures far more often. The most celebrated Celtic dwarf is the satyr-like fenodyree of Manx folklore. Welsh dwarfs look less attractive than Irish or Gaelic Scottish ones. The Welsh word cor gave the name Coraniaid. Those beings sent a plague across Wales in the tale Cyfranc Lludd a Llefelys. Culhwch must win back the vessel that Gwyddolwyn the dwarf owns. That vessel keeps the blood of the black witch warm. Eiddilig Gor can shift shape, and Celtic tales call him an enchanting dwarf. In Brittany the word korr lives on in the roguish korrigans. Irish lore keeps a different picture. There a dwarf was either a fairy or a small person. The fairy dwarves were not true dwarves. They were shapely small versions of normal humans. Cnú Deiréil, the dwarf harpist at the court of Fionn Mac Cumhaill, has golden hair. Small-people legends also inspired one great English satire. Jonathan Swift wrote Gulliver's Travels. Its hero Gulliver stood like a giant among the small Lilliputians.

:::
```
