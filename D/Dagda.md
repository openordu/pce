``` tab [group1:Info]
::: magazinestyle
The Dagda is an old god of the Tuatha Dé Danann, Ireland's supernatural people. His name means the good god, which in this case points to skill and fine work rather than charm. He serves as artisan, warrior, diviner, farmer, and king inside one body. He owns a cauldron that never empties, and he drags a huge club mounted on a cart. His pigs cook and come back to life, and his orchard bears fruit in every season. The Fomoiri mock him as gross and greedy. They pour porridge into a pit and order him to eat it. He clears the pit to the last scrape of his finger. Yet they misjudge him. He is a master of craft and magic whose powers span death and rebirth in the same stroke. His title Eochaid Ollathair, the father of all, is often compared with the Norse All-Father Odin. He sires Oengus, the god of youth and beauty. The goddess Brigit, whom we also call Bríg. And sons named Áed, Bodb Derg, and others. MacKillop records the alias set Dagda Mor, Daghda, Dagdae, and Daghdha. The name may pair the words good and god. People often spoke of him with the definite article, the Dagda. He also answers to Eochaid Ollathair, the father of all. His names add Ruad Rofhessa, the lord of great knowledge, and Deirgderc, the red eye of the sun. Some sources make him a son of Eithne, the same mother as Lug. He was proclaimed the Good God not for moral worth but because he was good at so many endeavours. Before battle he declared that all promises made to him he would do for himself. His cauldron came from Murias. It counts among the four treasures the Tuatha De Danann carried. It never failed his followers. His club was so huge that it rode on wheels. It gouged a rut as deep as the line between two provinces. That weapon recalls the striker of Sucellus in Gaul and the hammer of Thor. He kept two marvellous swine, one always cooking and still alive, and ever-laden fruit trees. For all his powers he often appears uncouth and benevolent, dressed in a peasant tunic that barely covered his rump. His Fomorian spying came apart over his well-known love of porridge. He is often seen married to Boand, although her usual husband is Nechtan. The parents asked Elcmar to foster the child Angus Og and so hid the affair. An alternate version has him court Eithne in the persona of Eochaid Ollathair, with Midir as foster-father. Brug na Boinne first belonged to the Dagda and passed to his son Angus. His best-known tryst was with the war-goddess the Morrigan at Samain. She stood astride the river Unshin in Connacht, washing the heads and limbs of the doomed. The goddess promised her help in the coming battle. In Cath Maige Tuired he slaughtered many Fomorians, and his magical harp Uaithne killed nine more. Yet Caitlin, the buck-toothed wife of Balor, killed him in that battle. Later folk belief gave him four great palaces under hollow hills and a reign of eighty years. As an ancestor deity he is sometimes identified with Donn, the ruler of the dead. Early twentieth-century speculation cast him as a sky-god, as a storm-god, as an earth-god, or as the sun itself. Commentators have also matched him with Cronus, Hercules, and Dis Pater.
```
