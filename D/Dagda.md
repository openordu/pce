``` tab [group1:Info]
::: magazinestyle
The Dagda is an ancient Irish god of the Tuatha Dé Danann. His common title is "the good god", because he is good at many things at once. Koch 2006 derives his name from a Celtic compound meaning excellence and god, and tradition usually calls him the Dagda with the definite article. His alternative names include Eochaid Ollathair, understood as all-father or great father, together with the byname Ruad Rofhessa. The name Eochaid Ollathair invites comparison with the Norse god Óðinn, who is similarly versatile. He works as artisan, diviner, husbandman, warrior, and wise king. He is also benevolent and rules over abundance and fertility. The Dagda owns a cauldron that never empties. He drags a huge mallet behind him on a cart, and Koch 2006 describes a club that deals death to the living and restores the dead, so enormous that wheels are needed to move it. He keeps pigs that can be eaten again and again, each time reviving themselves. He holds an orchard that always bears fruit, in any season. Koch 2006 treats him as a transfunctional deity who joins craft wisdom to the passage between death and renewal. The fullest surviving description of him occurs in the great tale Cath Maige Tuired. His usual guise is gross and uncouth. Sent to the enemy camp in that tale, the Fomoiri make sport of him and compel him to gulp a vast bowl of porridge that has been tipped into a pit in the dirt. Such is his greed that he eats every last spoonful and wipes the hollow clean with his finger, yet he still manages to seduce the daughter of his enemy and so secure victory for his own people. Scholars compare his many roles with those of the Gaulish Sucellus and of Heracles, and in the Dumézilian scheme he stands for the third function, the provision of wealth and food. He has also been identified with Donn and Dis Pater as a god of the Otherworld. His usual consort is Bóand, and their children include Brigit and Aonghus óg, whom Koch 2006 names as Oengus Mac ind Óc. He also fathers the fairy king Midir, the magician Bodb Derg, and other children of the Mythological Cycle, including Áed Menbhrec, Cermat, and Ainge. He mates with many goddesses, including the Mórrígan at the River Unshin, who agrees to back his side at Mag Tuired. At the second battle Cethlion, wife of Balor, kills him. He then reigns from the otherworld, still fed by his cauldron and orchard. His seat is at Brú na Bóinne, the great earthworks on the Boyne. He buries his slain son Áed Minbhrec at Grianán Aileach, in modern County Donegal.
```
