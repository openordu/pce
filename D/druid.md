``` tab [group1:Info]
::: magazinestyle
The druid stood at the head of the priestly class of the Celts. MacKillop frames the druids as male and female priest-philosophers across mainland Europe, the Mediterranean world, Great Britain, and Ireland. Classical writers ranked them with Roman flamines and the brahmins of India. Caesar saw them as magicians, poets, philosophers, and lawyers. Druids directed divine worship and presided at sacrifices. Some classical accounts add human victims to those rites. They also judged legal matters and taught elite youth along with their own aspirants. Acorns, once eaten, prepared a druid's mind for prophetic work. The order stood exempt from taxation and from the summons to fight. At great assemblies they spoke before kings, as in the hall of Conchobar mac Nessa. Teaching stayed oral. Pupils memorized a great store of verse, and study ran as long as twenty years. Ogham signs served when words needed marking, yet memory carried the main load. Many lessons came as riddles. The curriculum covered astronomy and natural science. Their reckoning of time put night before day. Druids held a form of metempsychosis, in which souls pass into new bodies. Classical observers likened that belief to the doctrine of Pythagoras. As healers they kept ties to mistletoe and to its ritual gathering. In Ireland they wore the airbacc giunnae tonsure. The name may mean oak or wise. Both men and women served as druids, and the Irish word bandrui means woman druid. Rituals took place in sacred groves called nemetons. Romans destroyed the groves on the isle of Anglesey. Later revivals range from the Gorsedd poetic contest in Wales to modern shamanic societies.
```
