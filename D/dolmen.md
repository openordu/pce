``` tab [group1:Info]
::: magazinestyle
The dolmen is a symbolic stone structure. MacKillop describes it as a large prehistoric monument found throughout the Celtic world. Doorways of stone stand in fields and pasture across the Celtic lands. They rest on rocky hills and in green valleys. The Breton word dolmen joins tol, table, with men, stone. Only a giant could eat off most dolmens. Archaeologists prefer the term portal tomb. In Wales the same structures are called cromlechs, from words meaning bent and flat stone. Cornwall favors that word too. There it may imply a more circular monument. People also call them Druid altars. MacKillop adds the fuller label druids' tables. Both rest on the unfounded belief that the stones date from the druids. But they were built thousands of years before the Celts and their priests arrived. Modern archaeology credits the building to pre-Celtic peoples. Some 6,000 years have passed since stone uprights met huge crossbeams. Capstones may weigh up to 40 tons. The builders were so precise that many structures still stand.

Burials appear in recesses under the dolmens. This is why archaeologists call them tombs. Yet burials were few compared to the population. Those buried beneath may have been victims of human sacrifice. Or they may have been people of high birth deemed worthy of a special grave. Similar stone structures in the Canadian arctic mark geography and open shamanic doorways. MacKillop lists more than 161 dolmens in Ireland. Other examples stand at Callemish in the Outer Hebrides, at Carnac in Brittany, and at Essé in Brittany. Comparable monuments reach as far away as Japan.

In Ireland these stones are called the beds of Diarmait and Gráinne. The eloping couple was said to sleep on a different one each night. They fled Fionn mac Cumhaill, her angry intended husband. This legend links dolmens to fertility and sexuality. Irish folklore adds a warning: women who wander close to the stones were said to risk barrenness. In Cornwall people said cromlechs were the quoits or playthings of giants. Recesses in the stones held the marks of giants' fingers. Folklore claims the stones cause sterility, or that they raise the chance of conception.
```
