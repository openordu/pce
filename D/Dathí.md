``` tab [group1:Info]
::: magazinestyle
Dathí is remembered as Ireland's final pagan ruler. He was a nephew of Niall of the Nine Hostages, and he ruled the province of Connacht for 23 years. When Niall died, Dathí took the kingship at Tara, the great ceremonial seat of the high kings. Tradition places his grave at Cruachan, the provincial capital of Connacht, beneath a standing stone. That stone, say the stories, long predates the reign it is said to mark. Dathí is often dated to the fifth century C.E. There is scholarly doubt about his crown. The more likely truth is that Dathí was never a king. He was a successful warrior who raided the Continent across the sea. Later annalists were eager to praise a patron. They raised this warrior to the rank of king and wrote him into the royal line at Tara. MacKillop dates the shadowy king to about 445 and sometimes gives him the patronymic mac Fiachrach. In the annalistic stories he invaded Strathclyde and then the Continent. He crossed the Alps, and lightning struck him down after he seized the tower of Formenus. That Formenus was a hermit styled king of Thrace. Samuel Ferguson retold that legend for the Royal Irish Academy in 1884. The traditional Dathi may bear no relation to the sage of that name in W. B. Yeats's poem The Blessed of 1897. Mary Genevieve Hogan examined the legend in 1933 as a tale answering Arthur's chronicle story.

:::
```
