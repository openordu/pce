``` tab [group1:Info]
::: magazinestyle
The Celtic idea of death was a cosmological concept rather than a single doctrine. Archaeological evidence points to permeable boundaries between this world and the next. The hearth and the burial ground served as openings where the dead could reenter this world. Caesar recorded a continental Celtic belief in reincarnation. Other classical writers claimed Celtic warriors fought bravely because they expected to fight again. Scholars still debate whether those reports record genuine Celtic doctrine. The classical observations on the Celtic afterlife remain incomplete. No Celtic tradition paints a full portrait of that realm. Folklore from every Celtic land testifies that the dead hold power to come back among the living.

Burial practices document these views. In the late centuries B.C.E. the continental Celts buried their dead with grave goods such as mirrors and weapons. Some of the greatest archaeological finds come from those early graves. Later communities turned to cremation. The record cannot show whether that shift marked a change in belief or an adoption of Roman custom. Among the insular Celts no burials appear before the first century B.C.E. That absence suggests cremation or excarnation, the exposure of the body. Myth hints that people preserved some remains, especially heads, notably for warriors killed in battle.

The dead did not occupy a sealed realm like the Christian Heaven or Hell. They kept a relationship with the living that could be happy or destructive. In the Scottish Highlands the dead returned to punish living people who had wronged them. Death marked a change in form rather than a termination. The Otherworld, home of gods and fairies, later merged with the Christian realm of the dead. The dead themselves became confused with fairies. Tradition also imagined the dead resting quietly in their graves, ready to come forth when the season was right. They returned most readily at Samhain on November 1.

The Celtic realm of the dead sits close to the Otherworld without becoming identical with it. Named gods who preside over the dead are rare in the surviving record. The Gaulish god Sucellus shows funerary aspects. The sixth-century Byzantine historian Procopius reports an island named Brittia, probably Britain. There the souls of the dead could be ferried across. The realm of the dead usually lies in the west. Tradition often set that land in the western sea, sometimes on an unstable floating island. Breton belief set the Bay of Souls at Raz on the peninsula's far western tip. In Cornwall the water near Land's End belonged to the dead. The pseudo-history Lebor Gabala can instead set the land of the dead in Spain. In Ireland the realm of Donn stands on a rocky island in the south-west. That island realm, Tech Duinn, the House of Donn, sometimes receives the dead. The Welsh Annwfn can also hold the realm of the dead. Angau personifies death in Wales. The Breton Ankou corresponds to Angau as a bringer of death. The Breton religious festivals called pardons honor saints across the peninsula. Learned opinion reads them as celebrations of the dead that took Christian form. Anatole le Braz described that fusion in The Land of Pardons in 1906.

A disputed belief in reincarnation would explain why the Celts showed little concern for the final home of the dead. Their traditions nonetheless return to that home again and again. Death opened onto a western shore, a rocky island, or a quiet grave rather than onto a closed heaven.
```
