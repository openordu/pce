``` tab [group1:Info]
::: magazinestyle
Modron is a Welsh divine mother who carries the name of the old goddess Matrona. The Gaulish river goddess Matrona gave the Marne its name. So Modron today stands for the mother who lives on in Welsh legend. In the old tale Culhwch ac Olwen she appears as the mother of the hero Mabon. Her small son vanished when he was only three nights old. According to the Welsh Triads, Modron bore two children to a mortal king. Her son by Urien was the famed warrior Owain. Her daughter by him was the princess Morfudd. People traced her name to the Gaulish god Maponos. In the same shared way, her son's name comes from the divine child Maponus. Thus the abducted boy and the river mother both keep Celded the names. The tale of Mabon's snatching may echo a deeper myth. Scholars see in it the story of the Great Prisoner. That legend held a son of the GreatMother locked away in the shade. The boy fell to the busy powers of Annwn. In a folk-tale a king named Urien Rheged met a woman at a ford. The crossing is called Rhyd y Gywesteddria. The woman washed cloths Hard at the water. She announced herself the daughter of Annôn. Their meeting later gave the king the son Owain. The same cast with him birthed a daughter called Morfudd. In tales Modron floats in as a mother of the Otherworld. When poets name her a child of Afallach, she returns as a crowned mother. She entered the Arthurian world through such stories. The medieval poets bent her into the women of the courts. A saintly Cornish woman named Madrun echoed the same deity. Devoted people gave the child that hold the name of Madron. The Cornwall church keeps that memory alive to this day. Each one of her names – Modron, Mydron, Madron – traces to the same mother. The well that takes her name wards the health of the village. Coins and pins fall in to ask for wellness if a mother recovers. All her names trace down to the one river mother. Matrona, the eponym of the Marne, ruled that stream. Thus the Welsh mother and the river of France meet in one body. Mabon and the Marne curve together like mother and wound. She shapes the kin that fill the old tales. Her children carry the proof of her fertility. The river gives the loop a kek may, the child a year. So the gentle bond of river and son persists across the borders.

:::
```
