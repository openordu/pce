``` tab [group1:Info]
::: magazinestyle
Morfran, also written Morvran, was a Welsh god or hero. The name means great crow or sea crow. He was the son of Tegid Foel and the great goddess Ceridwen. His mother hoped to grant him the power of poetic inspiration, yet he was hideously ugly. To make up for his looks she began brewing a collection of herbs that would bring him wisdom. She set her servant boy Gwion to watch the cauldron. Gwion, later to become the poet Taliesin, accidentally drank some of the brew. He absorbed the wisdom and drew down Ceridwen's wrath. The gift meant for Morfran thus passed into another hand. The cauldron itself would later grow famous in verse as a vessel of knowledge. Gwion, transformed in the chase that followed, came at last to a new life as the great poet Taliesin. So the boy who guarded the pot became the singer who carried its secrets. The story keeps the same cast across its versions, though the roles shift with each telling. Welsh myth otherwise says little of Morfran. Sources also call him Y Fagddu, or Afagddu, a name meaning utter darkness. That title points to his black looks and the fears they stirred in others. Morfran also appears as a warrior in Welsh Arthuriana. No warrior would strike him in battle, fearing he might be the devil. His ugliness proved useful at the battle of Camlan, where everyone mistook him for a devil and let him escape. In the crowded field of Camlan his strange face became a kind of shield. So a quality that seemed a curse kept him alive when others fell. In later retellings the meaning of his names stayed linked to darkness and the crow. MacKillop points readers to Rachel Bromwich, Trioedd Ynys Prydain. The revised edition came out at Cardiff in 1978. See Afagddu.

:::
```
