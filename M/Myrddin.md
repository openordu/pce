``` tab [group1:Info]
::: magazinestyle
Myrddin, also written Merddin, is a fictional Welsh poet and prophet of the sixth century, made to stand just before the better known Merlin. The Arthurian conception of Merlin begins with Geoffrey of Monmouth in the twelfth century, and Myrddin stands as the older figure who feeds that later one. He kept a place in the court of the king Gwenddolou fab Ceido. The death of his lord in the battle of Arirderydd bruised him deeply, and he fled into the Caledonian forests of Scotland, which the Welsh called Celyddon. There he lived wild in the wood for close on fifty years, keeping company with animals and hiding in fear of the one who had stood against his lord, the man named Rhydderch Hael. At the end of that long torment he received the gift of prophecy. Several early mantic poems carry his name, and the song of the apple trees is one of them. Because of his exile he could bear the title of the mad one, and the stories link him to the Irish figure of the wandering madman in the old song of his sorrow, the text called the Frenzy of Suibne. The oldest text that names him is the prophecy of Britain written in the tenth century. For most of Welsh story after Geoffrey, the poet and the magician become hard to tell apart. The gift of the wild man gave the later Merlin his strength, and that strength made him the leading figure of the royal books of wonder. The battle at Arfderydd took place in the year 573 or 575. That fight settled the fate of the region they called the Old North. Grief grew so great that madness overtook his mind. The Welsh called that state Gwyllt, and the title stuck to him. A song of the apple trees ranks among the early mantic poems given his name. Those verses keep his voice alive in the oldest Welsh poetry. His story also echoes an Irish tale of a similar wanderer. The madman Suibne of the Frenzy of Suibne shared his fate. Both men fled to the wild after their world broke. The earliest written record of Myrddin comes from the tenth century. That source, the Prophecy of Britain, already knows him by name. Welsh writers after Geoffrey often merge him with the wizard Merlin. Yet the grieving poet of the wood stays the older and wilder figure.
```
