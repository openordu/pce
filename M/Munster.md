``` tab [group1:Info]
::: magazinestyle
Munster is the great region of the south and the south-west of Ireland, and it ranks as the largest of the four provinces. Its area reached nine thousand three hundred and seventeen square miles. Cartographers fixed the borders of such provinces only in the seventeenth century, so older tellers saw them as places of story more than drawn land. Munster in the tales held music and song, while Leinster held wealth, Connacht held wisdom, and Ulster held battle. A fifth province named Meath marked the centre, and the tellers treated it as a symbol of the myth rather than a real map. In the old speech the province took the name Mumu, and under Cóiced Muman it could count as one of five or as two, depending on how the tellers dealt with Meath. The people sometimes divided Munster into an eastern and a western portion. The western portion took the title Iarmumu or Irmumu, and the eastern portion took the title Aurmumu or Ormond. In the twelfth century a real split took place between the ruling house of the north and the ruling house of the south, the first naming itself Tuadmuma or Thomond and the second Desmuma or Desmond. Within those borders sat the counties of Clare, Cork, Waterford, Tipperary, Limerick, and Kerry. A good share of early Munster's wealth rose with the great family of the region. The early mediaeval province in theory kept three capitals that served in turn, one in Tipperary, one in Cork, and one near Limerick, while Temair Luachra acted as a smaller seat. Many land goddesses stayed linked to Munster, among them the great queen of the province and the well-known figure of the older woman, the Cailleach Bhéirre. The mountains of the south carried ties to these goddesses, and the hills kept their names. These goddesses made the province a home of the divine in the old tales. The valleys and heights of Munster therefore held both song and sanctity in the memory of the storytellers.

:::
```
