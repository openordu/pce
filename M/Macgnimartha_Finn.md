``` tab [group1:Info]
::: magazinestyle
Macgnímartha Finn is an Irish title for medieval Fenian narratives about the boyhood of Fionn mac Cumhaill. English renderings call the same material The Boyhood Deeds of Fionn, also named The Youthful Exploits of Fionn. MacKillop lists the variant spellings Macghniomhartha Finn and Macgnimrada Finn.

The surviving texts are incomplete. Commentators find in them the influence of stories about the boyhood of Cúchulainn, the premier hero of the Ulster Cycle. The borrowings show that medieval writers shaped the young Fionn on an older heroic model.

The narrative follows a fixed arc. The boy is reared in hiding under the name Demne. He then passes through a series of adventures that ready him for the work of a leading chief. Three achievements stand out. He wins the salmon of wisdom. He earns the name Fionn, meaning the fair-haired one, during an athletic contest. He gains a sword, a banner, and an aegis. Strangest of all his gains is the crane bag, the corrbolg. Commentators read that treasure as implying the beginnings of Irish writing.

The adventures close with two coup scenes. Fionn overcomes Goll mac Morna and becomes the unchallenged chief of the Fianna. He then travels to Tara, where he slays Aillén mac Midgna, the burner. The deed prepares his rise within the wider Fionn Cycle.

Kuno Meyer printed an edition under the title Boyish Exploits of Finn in Ériu in 1904. Joseph Falaky Nagy analysed the material in The Wisdom of the Outlaw, published at Berkeley in 1985. James Stephens reworked much of this boyhood material for his Irish Fairy Tales of 1920.

:::
```
