``` tab [group1:Info]
::: magazinestyle
Medb was an Irish goddess and heroine. Her name appears in many spellings. Writers render it as Maeve, Medbh, Medhbh, Meadhbh, and Meave. The Irish root means she who intoxicates. Scholars compare it to the Greek word for wine and the Latin for mead. Medb ruled Connacht as a warrior queen. She held her royal seat at the fortress of Cruachan. She stands as the most vibrant female figure in all of Celtic mythology. Early historians took her for a real ruler. Modern scholars read her as an apotheosis of older forces. Those forces include goddesses of territory, fertility, and sovereignty. She seems to owe something to Mór Muman of Munster. Like a Gaulish mother-goddess she is often shown with creatures. A bird and a squirrel perch on her shoulder. Literary convention paints her pale and long-faced. She wears a red cloak and carries a spear that may flame. She can outrun any horse on the plain. The mere sight of her drains two-thirds of a man's strength. Medb mastered men by force of will and by desire. She always followed her own mind. The Ulster Cycle makes her its leading woman. In the Táin Bó Cúaílngé she commands the armies of Connacht. She pushes the whole cattle raid into motion. Her husband in that text is Ailill mac Máta. She shows more resolve than he does at every turn. Her favourite lover was Fergus mac Róich. Translators gave her the name Medb of the friendly thighs. She claimed that thirty-two men were needed to satisfy her. Medieval scribes built her a full biography. They named her father Eochaid Feidlech, an early king of Tara. Some accounts name her mother Cruacha and derive the fortress Cruachan from her. Her sisters were Clothra, Eithne, and Mugain. All four sisters were once married to Conchobar mac Nessa. Medb struck down her sister Clothra, who carried the child of Conchobar at that time. That treachery would one day bring Medb's own death. Her sons with Ailill all bore the name Maine. Her daughters included Finnabair, Cainder, and Faife. Her love-children with Fergus gave their names to the land. Their sons gave their names to Kerry and to Connemara. Medb's struggle with Cúchulainn drives the human story of the Táin. She came to respect him as a worthy foe. Her plans to trap him kept failing against his skill. She finally set the children of Cailitin against him. That campaign would help bring Cúchulainn down. Medb met her death on a lake island in Lough Ree. A child had survived when she killed her pregnant sister. That child was Furbaide Ferbend. He grew up on an island in the lake. Medb later came to live on that same island. She went bathing there each morning. Furbaide saw a beautiful woman go to the water one day. He learned the woman was Medb, the killer of his mother. He took a hard piece of cheese and set it in his sling. He shot and struck her squarely in the forehead. Medb died on the spot. The land still keeps her memory in place-names. A great cairn stands on Knocknarea above Sligo. It is called Miscaun Maeve. In another form she is Medb Lethderg, a goddess of sovereignty at Tara. She married the successive kings of Tara in that guise. She stands at the center of the Irish idea of the land and its queen.
```
