``` tab [group1:Info]
::: magazinestyle
The Matres were Continental Celtic mother goddesses. Their Latin title means Divine Mothers. The name gives a common label to countless images of motherhood. Their statues rank among the most common goddess figures across the Celtic lands. Carvers often showed two mothers together. The usual form showed three. Those triads could appear as a maiden, a fertile mother, and an aging crone. Some carvers repeated the same figure three times over. Scholars call this repetition triplism. Each Mother wore a long robe. One breast often stood bared in the stone. The Mothers usually carried a basket of fruit in their laps. Some held a cornucopia spilling bread and produce. Others cradled a sleeping baby against their chest. These objects marked the goddesses as powers of plenty. They served as unmistakable signs of earthly fertility. The Mothers tied the abundance of the land to the people who relied on it. They also guarded the home, the hearth, and the running springs. A few commentators read the goddesses as virgins. That reading sits oddly beside the nursing images. Mother-goddesses elsewhere in early Europe wore more than one face. They combined the powers of fertility with the duties of war and death. The Roman world linked the Matres to Epona, the horse-goddess of the stables. The Mothers appeared too as Dea Nutrix, the nourishing goddess. She holds a child to her breast in the nursing pose. Another form, the pseudo-Venus, showed a single woman. She clutched the symbols of fertility close to her body. Roman soldiers and traders carried the cult of the Mothers across the empire. Their shrines stood beside roads, springs, and mountain passes. The people set up the stones at wells, at crossroads, and on high ground. The very ordinary nature of the sites shows how near the Mothers stood to daily life. Farmers prayed to them for a good crop and for strong calves. Women called on them in childbirth and for the health of their children. The Mothers watched over the harvest and the wellbeing of livestock. They held the powers that kept families and fields alive. Some figures raise both hands in blessing, palms turned outward. That gesture marks them as protectors who give and who keep the people safe. Three identical figures seated in a row became the visual signature of the cult across the western provinces. The same design turns up from the banks of the Rhine to the roads of the Alps. Wherever the Roman army built a fort, the Mother stones often followed. The cult proved so wide that no single local name ever won out. Instead the plain Latin title, meaning the Mothers or the Divine Mothers, bound the whole movement together.
```
