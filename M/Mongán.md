``` tab [group1:Info]
::: magazinestyle
Mongán ruled as a partly mythological Irish king during the seventh century CE. His name comes from mong, a word for a long and abundant head of hair. The story says he was born already wearing a heavy mane on his head. Poets linked that word to moing, a mane that also served as a kenning for the sea. Medieval tales placed him in the Cycle of Kings. Yet his persona drew on a real Dál nAraide warrior who died about 624. His seat of rule stood at Ráth Mór, also called Rathmore. It lay in the district of Mag Line, or Moylinny, near Lough Neagh in County Antrim. Mongán does not appear in the Ulster Cycle. The annals say he spoke well and loved to court women. He died when Artur ap Bidor, a man of Welsh birth, felled him with a stone. By the written record he was the son of the powerful Dál nAraide king Fiachna mac Báetáin. His mother was the king's queen, Caintigem. Stories from the eighth century onward instead made the sea god Manannán mac Lir his true father. They told how Manannán cuckolded Fiachna in three ways. He took another shape each time to win a night with Caintigem. The god claimed the boy when he was just three days old. This magical conception echoed the claim that he was a reincarnation of the hero Fionn mac Cumhaill. The flight of Gráinne with Diarmait mirrored the story of Guinevere and Lancelot. From his divine father Mongán inherited shape-shifting and free movement between this world and the Otherworld. At three days old he joined Manannán in Tír Tairngire, the Land of Promise. There he gained secret knowledge. He learned to take the form of a deer, a salmon, a seal, a swan, and a wolf. He left that land at twelve and returned to Ulster at sixteen. One account names his wife Fintigema, also Findthighearn, meaning fair leader. Others give him Breóthigema, also Breothigeam, meaning flame leader, and Dub Lacha. However real his reign, Mongán seems to reflect an ancient story of the goddess of Sovereignty.
```
