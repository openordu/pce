``` tab [group1:Info]
::: magazinestyle
The Isle of Man is a small island nation of 220 square miles lying in the Irish Sea. Its Manx names are Mannin and Ellan Vannin. The island belongs to the United Kingdom but keeps its own parliament, the Tynwald, which is older than the Parliament at Westminster. With about 65,000 people it is the least populous of the six Celtic lands, smaller even than the Highland settlement in Nova Scotia. Irish folk reached the island in the fourth or fifth century. Some said they named it for the sea god Manannán mac Lir, but scholars now hold that the god took his name from the island, not the reverse. Man's oldest ties run to Leinster rather than to the lake country of Lough Neagh. One tale shows Fionn mac Cumhaill pulling up a great sod of earth, which gap became Lough Neagh while the thrown clod became Man. Norse kings ruled the isle from the eighth to the twelfth centuries and then held the Hebrides until the battle of Largs in 1263. Their settlers mingled with the older Goidelic folk. The island speaks English now, and the last native Manx speaker, Ned Maddrell, died on 27 December 1974. Early boat travel was dangerous, so Manx Gaelic drifted apart from Irish. Folk venerated Manannán into the twentieth century, and the island's three-legged triskelion is often tied to his power to ride the waves. John Kelly made the first record of local tales in the eighteenth century. In Manx legend the patron saint Maughold descends from the divine warrior Mac Cuill.
```
