``` tab [group1:Info]
::: magazinestyle
The Mabinogion is a collection of medieval Welsh prose tales.
Its heart is the Four Branches.
The Four Branches are the Pwyll, Branwen, Manawydan, and Math.
The original word is mabinogi, singular.
It comes from the Welsh word for a son or boy.
The name means a story of youth.

The earliest complete copies lie in two books.
The first is the White Book of Rhydderch.
It is dated to about 1350.
The second is the Red Book of Hergest.
It is dated to about 1400.
Pieces of branch two and branch three also last in an older book.
That book, Peniarth 6, is dated to about 1250.
Scholars think the tales were first written down about 1050 to 1120.
Before that they moved by mouth.
The teller of tales, the cyfarwydd, kept them.

The author of the tales is unknown.
The cyfarwydds made them from old material.
The sound of the old myths runs through the Four Branches.
The people of the mother Don answer to the Irish Tuatha De Danann.

Beyond the Four Branches stand the other native tales.
Culhwch ac Olwen has Arthur help the hero win his bride.
Peredur, Geraint, and the Lady of the Fountain are Arthurian tales.
They follow the knights of Arthur, not Arthur himself.
They show the pull of the French romances of Chretien de Troyes.

The name Mabinogion is almost certainly a scribal error.
It stands in a single manuscript.
The true word is mabinogi.
The suffix -ion reads as a plural, so the name stuck.
Lady Charlotte Guest made the name famous.
She gave the world an English translation of the tales in the 1800s.
Her book bore the title Mabinogion.

:::
```
