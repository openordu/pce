``` tab [group1:Info]
::: magazinestyle
Mogons is a title given, in Roman times, to a native Celtic deity. The spelling Mogounos also appears in the sources. The name sits close to a word that means the Great One. Many commentators translate the title in just that way. Evidence of the cult spreads across a wide region. Inscriptions survive from north Britain. Further examples come from eastern Gaul. Still more appear in what is now Germany. The sheer number of stones points to widespread worship. The spellings themselves vary from place to place. Records offer Mogunos, Mountus, and Mogtus among the forms. That wide variety of spelling leads some experts to a cautious reading. They propose that Mogons was a title applied to many deities. It may have served as an honorific rather than one god's true name. The same title could cover several separate gods at once. Such gods include Belatucadros and Coddius among the known names. The evidence suggests a shared label more than a single figure. His name is sometimes tied to the north British god Vitiris. A stone from Castra Exploratorum near Netherby carries that link. The site stands close to the west end of Hadrian's Wall. Netherby itself sits beside the old Roman frontier road. An independent deity of the same name is attested at Voreda. Voreda is the old Penrith in the modern county of Cumbria. The name rests on the very old stem for the word great. That same root lies behind the place-name Mogontiacum. Mogontiacum is the city now called Mainz on the river Rhine. Kenneth H. Jackson discussed the name in his book Language and History in Early Britain. The study appeared in Edinburgh in 1953. Jackson examined the grammar and the growth of the title across the province. His work set the frame for later study of the cult and its spread. The stones that carry the title mark the old roads too. Travelers, soldiers, and settlers all set up the same dedication far from one another. That spread gives the name a hold across the whole northern arc of the Roman world.

:::
```
