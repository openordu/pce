``` tab [group1:Info]
::: magazinestyle
Midsummer was a calendar festival of the Celts. The Celts did not mark the solstices, the longest and shortest days, nor the equinoxes, the days of equal light and dark. They marked the days at the center of each season, such as Imbolc, Beltane, Lughnasa, and Samhain. As they met earlier peoples who measured the year by the sun, their own festivals took on a new shape. Koch notes that Midsummer's Day falls on the summer solstice, 24 June. It is also the feast of Saint John the Baptist, exactly six months from Christmas. The date is important in modern folk tradition across the Celtic countries, but no direct evidence shows that the ancient Celts marked it. The same folk customs that accompany Beltaine and Samain appear around Midsummer. Scholars think the celebration may have come from the English or the Norse, whose fires at this time were strong. The common form of the celebration is a bonfire. People also practised divination. They placed the flower rose-root, called midsummer men, in clay in clay overnight to learn their health for the coming year. In Brittany the date was a time to form farming and household contracts. In Upper Brittany a man or a woman named Jean or Jeanne always lit the fire, and the embers were dropped into wells to purify the water. As the fire died, people leapt over it. In Ireland the way a person jumped revealed the crimes they might have committed, and people spread the ashes across the fields. Fergus Kelly linked the ploughman's festival, Féil na n-airemon, to Midsummer's Day, though Máire MacNeill placed it at Lugnasa. In all the Celtic countries the date was a good time to gather medicinal herbs. Koch keeps the date at the solstice and names Saint John, placing it exactly six months from Christmas as Beltaine and Samain stand six months apart. He finds no proof that the ancient Celts kept the day and credits an English or Norse origin. He records rose-root set in clay to read the coming health, a man named John or woman named Jane lighting the fire, and embers lifted from the blaze. People crossed the dying flame, and in Ireland the leap and the scattered ashes judged a crime. Some place the ploughmen's festival beside this date, against the Irish feast of Lugh, and the day serves the gathering of healing herbs.

:::
```
