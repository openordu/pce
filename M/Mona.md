``` tab [group1:Info]
::: magazinestyle
Mona is another name of Anglesey, a sacred British and Welsh island. The island has used the official Welsh name Ynys Môn since 1995. Earlier, from 1284 to 1974, it formed a separate county called sir Fôn. The narrow Menai straits split it from the Welsh mainland, crossed now by two bridges. Its soil stays fertile and habitable, yet the rugged Snowdonia massif shuts it off from easy overland travel. Looked out to sea, the island stands at a crossroads across the Irish Sea that joins Wales, Ireland, the Isle of Man, and Scotland south-west. A ferry still runs from Anglesey toward Dublin. In AD 60 the first strike into north Wales fell upon this island. The Roman historian Tacitus tied that move to the druids' anti-Roman ideology, which held deep roots here. Romans tore down sacred groves where druids offered human sacrifice. Rich watery deposits at Llyn Cerrig Bach reveal that pre-Roman druids kept an island sanctuary from early times. Anglesey's bogs and lakes have yielded votive troves in Celtic design, which druids likely hid during the Roman assault. Druids ran a college on the island. Celtic women priestesses, robed in black, screamed like the raven-goddess Mórrígan while the priests fought. Romans elsewhere had already cut sacred groves and struck down clergy. The druids of Anglesey knew their homes and sacred trees faced ruin, so they died in battle or were executed, and the great forests burned. The island stayed holy even before the Celts, since an ancient cairn rises on the hill of Bryn-Celli-Dhu. Diana Paxson wrote of the destruction in her Arthurian trilogy.

:::
```
