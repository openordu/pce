``` tab [group1:Info]
::: magazinestyle
Minerva was a Romanized Celtic goddess of healing. The Romans cast her in the image of the Greek warrior goddess Athena. But the Celtic Minerva was no fighter. She turned only against disease and pain. The Romans found many similar goddesses among the Celts. Their healing power grew from the headwaters of rivers. It also grew from hot springs and other waters. One such image is carved into the Roman quarries near the River Dee in Chester. She wears an owl and a Gorgon's head. Those objects belong to the Greek Athena. The owl represents watchful wisdom. The Gorgon's face turns aside harm. Julius Caesar wrote that the Celtic Minerva taught arts and crafts to people. Caesar held this goddess high among the native gods. He still placed her below Mercury, Apollo, Mars, and Jupiter. Caesar said she schooled folk in the founding principles of the arts. The surviving images do not show that teaching clearly. Sometimes the Romans kept the local name of the goddess. Other cases lost the name altogether. One Minerva, styled the goddess of the Wood Smite, confirms part of the goddess. Another Minerva, called Vitrix, the victor, stood in reliefs as a helmeted female head. The Romans also linked her to a goddess of the border fight. That goddess was Belis for close by. The most famous Celtic Minerva was Sul, keeper of the hot pools of Bath. Some connected her to the darks of war. She drove the disease from the sick. She eased the limbs of the lame. She watched over the birthing and the small child. The Roman lords of Bath joined her name to a local healing power. The joined goddess was called Sulis of the spring. The combined use of the two names stood in the old Latin claims. The tablet gave her the title Most Savouse. People once dropped coins into the waters for her. So Minerva took her place as a guardian of springs and pools. Water was her home and her weapon.
```
