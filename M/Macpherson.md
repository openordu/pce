``` tab [group1:Info]
::: magazinestyle
Macpherson is the surname of James Macpherson, the Scottish writer who lived from 1736 to 1796. MacKillop styles him a translator inside quotation marks. His Poems of Ossian appeared between 1760 and 1763. The work leaned on some Gaelic oral material while claiming the status of a vanished Celtic epic. Despite the title, the writing is prose.

Macpherson published the work serially under three titles. The opening instalment, Fragments of Ancient Poetry, printed at Edinburgh in 1760, gathered Highland material. Fingal followed in 1762, and Temora in 1763. Critics challenged the authenticity of the Poems from their first appearance. The originals he later produced in support of his claim turned out fabricated.

Yet the characterizations plainly draw on figures from Irish and Scottish Gaelic tradition. Fingal corresponds to Fionn mac Cumhaill. Darthula corresponds to Deirdre. Ossian corresponds to Oisín. Through these masked borrowings the poems carried Irish narrative material into European letters.

Modern readers often judge the prose turgid. The eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries did not. The Poems of Ossian electrified Europe and drew admirers of every kind. Napoleon admired them. Goethe translated them into German. William Blake, Thomas Jefferson, and Henry David Thoreau praised them. Brahms set them to music, among many other composers.

MacKillop grants the forgery little standing and the influence much. He concludes that, bogus though the work was, Macpherson turned learned Europe toward Irish and Scottish Gaelic traditions. His work also carried the name Oscar to the front rank of names known from Celtic storytelling.

The standard studies are George F. Black's 1926 account of the Ossian controversy, Derick S. Thomson's 1952 work on the Gaelic sources, and the 1987 essay on ballads becoming epics in The Heroic Process. Macpherson's Breton counterpart is Hersart de La Villemarqué.
```
