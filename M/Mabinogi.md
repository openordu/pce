::: magazinestyle
Mabinogi is the preferred form of the title for the masterwork of medieval Welsh literature. The word may mean a tale of youth, and scholars link it to Maponos, a divine youth. Mabinogi abbreviates Pedair Cainc y Mabinogi, the Four Branches of the Mabinogi: the branch-tales of Pwyll, of Branwen, of Manawydan, and of Math. The form Mabinogion has wide currency in English. Lady Charlotte Guest coined it for her three-volume translation of 1838 to 1849, and the authoritative 1949 translation made by Gwyn Jones with Thomas Jones kept it. Mabinogion denotes twelve tales: the four branches plus Culhwch ac Olwen, Breuddwyd Macsen Wledig, Cyfranc Lludd a Llefelys, Breuddwyd Rhonabwy, and stories of Geraint, Owain, Peredur, and Taliesin. The four branches draw on extensive oral tradition and hang together loosely. Possibly one hand assembled them before the mid-twelfth century. Two complete manuscripts survive: the White Book of Rhydderch and the Red Book of Hergest, with fragments in older collections. Branch one follows Pwyll, prince of Dyfed. He trades shapes and kingdoms with Arawn, king of Annwfn, for a year, kills Hafgan in single combat with one blow, and wins the Otherworld title Pen Annwfn. Later he meets Rhiannon on the mound at Arberth, outwits her suitor Gwawl with the bag game that revellers call badger in the bag, and marries her. Their son Pryderi vanishes as a newborn and returns, raised first as Gwri of the Golden Hair by Teyrnon. Branch two tells of Branwen, daughter of Llyr. Her forced marriage to Matholwch, king of Ireland, collapses after Efnisien mutilates the Irish horses and later throws her son Gwern into the fire. The cauldron of regeneration breaks when Efnisien dies inside it. Bendigeidfran wades the Irish Sea as a human bridge for his army and falls to a poisoned spear. His severed head keeps guard for the island, and Branwen's heart breaks at the ruin of two islands. Seven Welshmen survive, among them Pryderi and Manawydan. Branch three follows Manawydan, who weds Rhiannon and lives in Dyfed with Pryderi and Cigfa. A mist empties the land. The four live by craft in England until jealousy drives them home, where a white boar leads Pryderi and Rhiannon into an enchanted fort and pins them to a bowl. Manawydan catches a mouse at the harvest and stages its execution at Arberth. Ransoms climb from a scholar, a priest, and a bishop until the bishop admits the mouse is his own transformed wife. The captor is Llwyd, son of Cil Coed, who revenges Gwawl. He lifts the enchantment, and the prisoners walk free. Branch four, Math son of Mathonwy, is the longest and most tangled. Math rules Gwynedd and must rest his feet in a maiden's lap unless war calls. His nephews Gwydion and Gilfaethwy engineer a war with Pryderi over phantom swine so Gilfaethwy can force Goewin, the foot-holder. Gwydion kills Pryderi in single combat. Math weds Goewin to spare her shame and punishes the brothers by turning them yearly into deer, pigs, and wolves until three animal sons are born. For a new foot-holder, Arianrhod fails the virginity test and bears Dylan, who takes to the sea, and an unnamed boy Gwydion rears. Arianrhod curses the boy with three denials: a name, arms, and a wife. Gwydion tricks out each curse. The boy wins the name Lleu Llaw Gyffes, takes arms, and gains a flower-wife, Blodeuedd, whom Math and Gwydion make from blossoms. Blodeuedd takes Gronw Pebyr as a lover and learns the one way to kill Lleu: a year-long spear, a special tub, and a billy goat under one foot. Wounded, Lleu flies off as an eagle. Gwydion finds him, restores him by a touch of the wand, and turns Blodeuedd into an owl. Lleu's spear then pierces the stone Gronw hides behind and kills him. Lleu takes back his land and rules it well. Standard Welsh text: Ifor Williams, 1930. Translations: Jones and Jones 1949, Gantz 1976, Ford 1977.
