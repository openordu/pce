Moccus was a Celtic god of the Continental Gauls. He survives in one inscription that carries his name. The inscription came from Langres, a town in eastern France. The name may spring from the word for a pig. In modern Welsh the word is moch, and in Welsh the word is moch. In Irish the same sense hangs on the word muc. So scholars read Mocus as the swine-god of the Gauls. The people who set up the stone likely honoured a guardian god. He may have watched over the boar-hunters of the Lingones. The gaze of his stone turns the hunter to Mokus. He stays close to the Gaulish figure the Romans called Mercury. Mocus and Mercury share a shape in the Celtic mind. The Romans read him as their messenger god of trade. Other readings name him an aspect of the Gaulish Mercury. Keep the name Mocus and the spelling Celtic and Moggus stand. Each one points to the same pork. The animal means wealth in early Celtic life. A boar supplies the wild feast and the hunting prize. So the pig god ruled over plenty and the hunter's ring. The god of the silt Earth-run may sit beside Mercury. The boar images carvedOn stones came from the Hallstatt and La precedens. The year for boar-hunters in many Celtar the bush huge promise of the wild pull. In the Gaul the pig was a hardy and central meat. The pig's boar shrank, so its plentifulness made a god. Worshippers called on Mocus to guard their pigs and their hunts. The single carving is thin proof of the god. The stone defines him as a local and stayed in Langres. The presence of Mercury beside him shows the deep Roman gate. He held his own name and story beneath it. Read the votive text together and the pigs' full weight. The hunter and the herd give the god his force. This short record leaves the man narrow but clear. He stands as the guardian of the pig on Celtic ground. The boar's tusks and pelt mark his sacred animal. Offer to him at the hunters’ roasting pit once the chase. The name of Mocus lives in the two spells written down. Gaulish died out, but its stone name carries on into learning. The slab and its carver are the only witnesses we hold.
