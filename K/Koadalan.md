``` tab [group1:Info]
::: magazinestyle
Koadalan is the hero of a Breton folk-tale of magic and a lost chance at deathlessness. The name joins the Breton words for wood and for a man's given name, and it finds a Welsh echo in Coed-Alun. Commentators sometimes give the long story the title the Saga of Koadalan. Collectors took the tale from oral tradition in the nineteenth century, yet it still shows parallels with medieval Welsh writing. The boy Yves Koadalan leaves his poor parents at sixteen to seek his fortune. A nobleman turns him away the moment he says he can read. Koadalan hides the skill by turning his coat inside out, then wins the place. The pair rise into the air and land beside a handsome castle. A warning scrawled on a leaf says that whoever enters never leaves. Inside the lad rests on a feather bed and finds a cloth that yields any food he asks. He must keep a hot fire under a pot, strike a lean mare with a holly switch, and never open two doors. The mare, named Teresa, begs mercy, then helps him. She sends him through the forbidden doors to three red books that raise him to the rank of the world's great wizards. Washing in the courtyard spring turns his hair to gold. When the wizard Foulkes steals the books, Teresa gives aid again. Near his end Koadalan plans rebirth: his body is to be chopped, sealed in a pot under hot dung, and fed for six months with a nurse's milk. The nurse falls asleep while the last three days remain, so Koadalan is never made whole. Scholars read the mare as a double of Rhiannon and the escapes as kin to the changes of Ceridwen and Taliesin.

:::
```
