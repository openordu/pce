``` tab [group1:Info]
::: magazinestyle
Kingship in Celtic tradition binds the ruler to the life of the land. The king must stay physically whole. A blemish can end his reign. Annals record that bees partly blinded Congal Caech, so he forfeited the kingship of Tara. Nuadu lost his reign over the Tuatha De Danann after injury in battle, though he wore a well-wrought silver arm. Justice, named in Irish law, keeps the streams full and the harvest rich. The wolves keep peace with their prey under a wholly good king. A false verdict brings the wasteland instead. The old terms ri and tuath define each other. A ri rules a tuath, and the tuath gives a ri his duty. The root of ri means to reach out straight, that is, to rule. The king's whole body and the plenty of his land stand or fall together. MacKillop ties kingship to a sacred union with the land. In the rite a ruler takes the earth to wife. She appears as a queen of power, and the king must win her. One story shows her as a plain hag who turns lovely after the wedding night. The Irish name for this wedding feast of kings is the banis. The ceremony is a holy wedding of the sovereign to his realm. At Tara the crowning of the high king came through a night-long rite. The title of high king fit a strong local lord, not a master of all Ireland. Blue is the colour of Irish king and rule. The flag of the old kings carried a blue field. The name blue lady holds the same bond between rule and land. 

:::
```
