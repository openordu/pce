``` tab [group1:Info]
::: magazinestyle
The kelpie is a Scottish fairy water-creature of long-standing folklore. Sailors once blamed storms at sea on these beings. Most accounts paint the creature as mischievous, and many go further and call it malevolent. The being first belonged to lonely, fast-moving streams, and only later spread to any stretch of water. The Minch's blue men set the sea boiling so sailors kept clear. Other kelpies stirred the water only to cause trouble. A calm river or pond could become dangerous once someone stepped into it. A kelpie could leap onto a horse crossing a ford, drive it mad, and put the rider in danger. Vessels could fall to malevolent kelpies. A skipper who completed rhyming couplets could force the kelpie away. The creature usually took the shape of a horse. At times it wore a human form. In horse-shape it lured riders onto its back, then bore them down into dark water where they drowned. Its tail struck the water in thunder and the being vanished in a flash of lightning. Scholars describe the kelpie as the Scottish version of the Breton korrigan. The korrigan more often appears as a luring maiden, while the kelpie shows less sexual urge. Scottish Gaelic links the word to colpach or calpach, terms for a young cow, bullock, or colt. Kentigern names the saint to whom the kelpie stands as a watery counterpart. Variants of the name include kelpy and waterkelpie.

:::
```
