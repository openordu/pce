``` tab [group1:Info]
::: magazinestyle
Knockfierna is an Irish mythological hill in County Limerick. It carries the further names Cnoc Firinne and Knockfeerina. The Irish name Cnoc Firinne translates as hill of truth. The peak rises to about 948 feet and lies two miles east of Ballingarry. The mound formed from volcanic activity. Many writers count Knockfierna among the most celebrated otherworldly seats in Munster. Travellers long pointed to it as the gate of another world. Irish story set a god upon the hill called Donn Firinne. One folk version makes him a leader among the ageless folk of the Tuatha De Danann. A second version names him a fairy king. He was said to draw mortals into his keep at Knockfierna and few returned. A folk meaning of his name adds the senses of the just and the truthful. The scholar Muller Lisowski argued that this Donn Firinne grew from the older god Donn. Donn rules the dead and calls the dying to his halls. Knockfierna stands as a sister to the sacred hill Knockainy, seven miles distant. Each hill offered an opening into the Otherworld. Crowds gathered each year for Lughnasa, the great festival of the first harvest. The assembly drew folk from the whole region. The summit was long known in the oral life of the district as a place of many faerie deeds. Storytellers set whole episodes there. The fairy king Donn Firinne kept a seat upon this height. Some people join this god to the king of the Otherworld, who also bears the name Donn. MacKillop cites the headword ierna alongside this entry. Under that headword he records the Arthurian romance figure Igerna, also spelled Igerne, Igraine, Igrayne, Ygerne, and Yguerne. She is the mother of Arthur. Married to Cornwall's duke, who goes by Gorlois in some tellings and by Hoel in others. She conceives Arthur with Uther Pendragon, who reaches her wearing her husband face and likeness through the arts of Merlin. After the Duke falls, she weds Uther. Her Welsh forerunner is Eigr, famed in old Welsh verse as a woman of rare beauty.
```
