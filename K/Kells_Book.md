``` tab [group1:Info]
::: magazinestyle
Kells, Book of (Codex Cenannensis) names the illuminated Gospel manuscript today kept at Dublin, Trinity College Library, as MS 58. It ranks among the most richly decorated of the big Latin gospel-books of the British Isles. Those books appeared from the 7th into the 10th century. Scholars mostly hold that work began in the later 8th century at the Columban monastery on Iona. The book then travelled inland to Kells in County Meath. A Columban house rose there at the start of the 9th century. MacKillop describes Kells as a market town and former monastic site about fifty-five miles northwest of Dublin. The name itself comes from an Irish word meaning the head fort. Kells owns two famed treasures of the early Christian age in Ireland. They are the Gospels known as the Book of Kells and a staff called the Crozier of Kells. The monastery enters written history about the year 804. That came after the repeated sackings of Iona and the slaughter of its people split the Columban church. Several narratives mention the house, among them the tale of Buchet's Hall. Up to four scribes wrote in a bold insular majuscule script. The overlap of lettering with painted ornament hints that the same hands supplied the pictures. The decoration joins Celtic, Anglo-Saxon and Mediterranean styles in the insular manner.

:::
```
