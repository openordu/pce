``` tab [group1:Info]
::: magazinestyle
The rowan is the mountain ash, a protective tree. People tied rowan crosses to the necks of cattle. Elder leaves set on windows and doors turned back fairy power. The tree grows near stone circles and old ruins. People planted it by homes and along property lines. Its wood guarded against witches and the evil eye. A fairy can leave its mark in the dew. People gathered that dew and used it. MacKillop calls the rowan the small deciduous tree known to science as Sorbus aucuparia. Folk names include the quicken tree and mountain ash. White flower clusters and orange-red berries mark the species out across Celtic lands. Within early Irish ogham the birch-letter Luis stood for rowan. Irish druids preferred rowan, hawthorn, and yew where Gaulish druids preferred oak. MacKillop credits the Tuatha Dé Danann, a semi-divine race, with bringing the rowan to Ireland. They brought it out of Tir Taimgire, the fabled Land of Promise. In the tale Tochmarc Étaíne the jealous Fuamnach reduces Étaín to water by hitting her with a rowan wand. Ailill mac Máta sends Fráech to fetch the tree, much as Gráinne insists that Diarmait bring some back. Rowanberries were credited with renewing youth. A man of 160 years could be brought back to his prime by their honeyed taste. The blessed dead lie beneath woven roofs of quicken boughs. The Salmon of Knowledge feeds on the berries. Across every strand of Celtic fairy lore the rowan gave the strongest defence against enchantments and witchcraft. On the Isle of Man twigs were bound into crosh cuim. They were hung over doorways. They were tucked into the long tails of cattle for protection. Manx folk also carried rowan boughs at Beltaine, the May Day rites. Linguistic variants include Old Irish luis, Scottish Gaelic caorunn, Manx keim, Welsh cerddinen, Cornish kerdhynen, and Breton kerzhinenn. The Brúidhean Chaorthainn tale and the ash tree entry offer further detail.

:::
```
