``` tab [group1:Info]
::: magazinestyle
Ruadán is a double figure who carries one name into history and into myth. The historical side is a sixth-century saint credited with a curse on Tara. His feast falls on 15 April, yet his historicity is thin. A Latin life written centuries after his time now reads as unreliable. Many writers call him a fanciful saint. He was said to be born in Leinster. There he founded the monastery of Lothra. That site lies inside modern Tipperary. The story of his curse was invented by later churchmen. In it the king Diarmait mac Cerbaill insulted him during a quarrel between Church and State. Diarmait was the last king to hold the pagan feast of Tara. Ruadán answered with his curse. Soon after, a roof-beam struck the king down, and Tara fell out of use. The tale does not stand against the record. Tara kept hosting the crowning of the high king and an annual fair for long after. An older Monaghan account gives the quarrel another face. In it Diarmait condemned Ruadán for sheltering a kinsman who faced a murder charge. The curse followed, and Tara never regained its old strength. The mythic side of the name is very different. Rúadán is the son of Bres and of Brigit, who bore that name in the form Bríg. He attacked Goibniu the smith-god and failed. Goibniu struck him dead in revenge. His mother wailed over him with the first keening Ireland ever heard. Writers connect that killing to the Welsh tale of the smith Gofannon slaying his nephew Dylan.

:::
```
