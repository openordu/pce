``` tab [group1:Info]
::: magazinestyle
The Dream of Rhonabwy, Breuddwyd Rhonabwy, is a Middle Welsh prose tale. It turns on a dream of a hero of Powys. The prince sends Rhonabwy and other warriors to catch his rebellious brother, who has gone raiding in England. They ride through wet country and lodge at night in the squalid hall of Heilyn Goch. The floors there are fouled with cow dung and urine. Exhausted, Rhonabwy sleeps on a yellow ox hide. In his dream he walks toward a ford of the Severn. Two richly dressed horsemen overtake him, and the first names himself Iddawg, called the Embroiler. Iddawg helped bring about the Battle of Camlan. He twisted the peaceful messages Arthur had sent to Medrawd. Guided by Iddawg, Rhonabwy finds Arthur with Bedwin the Bishop. The king sits enthroned on an island mid-river. Arthur calls the newcomers little fellows and mourns that such men now prevail in Britain. A stone in the ring of Arthur will let Rhonabwy remember all he has seen.
The dream then renders the whole army of Arthur in close detail. Enemies from Norway and Denmark await the army of Arthur. The king himself stays fixed at the gwyddbwyll board against Owain son of Urien. As the game runs, news arrives that the knights of Arthur are harrying the ravens of Owain. Arthur answers only with an order to play the game. Owain raises his banner, and his ravens begin to kill the men of Arthur. Then it is the turn of Owain to insist the board game continue. In the end Arthur asks Owain to withdraw his birds, and peace returns. Men bring tribute to the king, and Rhonabwy wakes to learn he has slept three nights.
Scholars date the work with care. The frame belongs to the reign of Madog, son of Maredudd, who ruled Powys and died in 1159. Others set it later. The poet Madog Dwygraig already names the dream around 1370 to 1380. That naming sets a firm upper limit. The single surviving text stands in the Red Book of Hergest. It stands as the final tale told in the wholly native Middle Welsh line. It also reads as the most self-aware literary work of that company. Modern readers do not treat it as a true story. It has little plot, a narration that will not move, and no clear end. Scholars read it as a satire on the older heroic style and a fine piece of craft. Wherever the date, the tale holds a full and lively portrait of a royal court.

:::
```
