``` tab [group1:Info]
::: magazinestyle
Whether the Celts took up a belief in reincarnation stays open to debate. The term reserves a soul for a fresh body in each life, with the deeds of one life falling on the next. The Hindu school calls that path karma. When a soul may rest in a bird or a beast, the better word is the migration of souls, or metempsychosis. Koch notes that the Celts, like other peoples, likely held no single simple view of the fate of the soul after death. The Irish tales give many examples of change of shape and of rebirth. The sage Tuan recalls his long life and, in some tellings, becomes a salmon. The hero Mongan is said to have passed through the forms of a stag, a salmon, a seal, and a wolf before living again as a man. In the tale of Etain, the heroine is changed into a pool of water, then into a fly, and later is born again as a mortal child, though she does not remember her past life. In the Welsh tradition, the poet Taliesin tells that he was once many shapes, a sword, rain, and starlight. The witch Ceridwen sought him through many changes before she bore him again as a child. The druids, according to some Roman and Greek writers, taught that the soul lives on. Koch repeats a line from a seventh-century text that speaks of the foolish tales of the druids, who said their early kin moved through time in the shape of birds. This memory of former lives gave authority to accounts of the distant past. The stories concern a few exceptional people, not the whole of humanity, so scholars read them as a sign of shape-shifting and rebirth among the Celtic peoples.

:::
```
