``` tab [group1:Info]
::: magazinestyle
Banba is an Irish goddess. She is one of three earth goddesses, with Fódla and Ériu, who met the Milesians. The medieval lore of women and the Book of Invasions group the three as daughters of Fiachra or of Ernmas. The Milesians were the mythological invaders seen as the first Irish Celts. The Book of Invasions places the meeting at Slieve Mis in County Kerry. Some sources place her on the plain from which the hill of Tara rises. Banba offered the Milesians happiness and wealth if the land bore her name. The Milesians instead gave precedence to Ériu, who made the same promise from the hill of Uisneach. The sources name a god or hero for each goddess as her husband. Banba's husband was Mac Cuill, whose name means son of Hazel. Legend holds that the salmon of wisdom fed on hazel nuts. Koch calls Mac Cuill a king of the Tuath Dé and a grandson of the Dagda. Geoffrey Keating, drawing on a lost Old Irish manuscript, tells another arrival tale. In that telling Banba and her sisters arrived as daughters of Cain, fifty women and three men with them. All of them died in a plague. The name may join ban, woman, with ba, an old word for death, for gain, or for cow. That last sense echoes Bóand, the river Boyne, whose name means white as a cow. The pig is an emblem of Banba, and her name resembles the Irish word for piglet, banb. Banba said she had walked Ireland before the time of the biblical Noah. In some tellings the Milesians drove her out with sung spells. Her name survives today as a poetic name for Ireland.

:::
```
