``` tab [group1:Info]
::: magazinestyle
Brug na Bóinne is a fairy palace beside the River Boyne. The Irish name means the hostel of the Boyne. The name also appears as Brug na Bóinde and Brug maic Ind Óc. The spellings brug and brugh are variants of bruig. The site now carries the name Newgrange. The passage-grave there dates to 3200-2600 BC. The name can also take in the nearby graves at Dowth and Knowth, both of matching size and age. All three stand in the Boyne valley, two miles northeast of Slane in County Meath. Boand and the Dagda held the otherworldly residence first. Angus Óg, god of youth and poetry in Irish belief, held it later and most famously. Angus made the palace his home. He won it from the master Elcmar. Elcmar first ruled the place as its lord. He also raised Angus as his foster son. The goddess Boand, who became the Boyne, conceived a child outside her marriage. She hid that fault by asking Elcmar to care for the boy. Some old tales treat Elcmar as a form of Nuadu Airgetlám. In another version Elcmar married Boand and the Dagda came to want her. The Dagda sent Elcmar on a trip that felt like one day and one night. Nine months truly passed while the master stayed away. Boand bore Angus in that long pause. Elcmar returned and knew nothing of the birth. Angus then asked the lord to live in the hall for a single day and night. That brief visit gave young Angus the palace forever. Hundreds of Irish stories show the hall welcoming countless guests. At times the name stood for the power and generosity of Angus Óg. The hall gave endless ale, three trees always in fruit, and two pigs, one living and one cooked. Variant texts state that the hero Lug Lámfhota lies buried there. One version throws Elcmar out like wicked angels flung from heaven. The master then settled at Sidh Clettig beside the southern flank of the Boyne.
```
