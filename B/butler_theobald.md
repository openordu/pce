``` tab [group1:Info]
::: magazinestyle
Theobald Walter, who died in 1206 and is recalled in tradition as butler theobald, stands as the ancestor of the famous Butler family, the house from which the Earls of Ormond were descended. He was the brother of the Archbishop of Canterbury and one of the closer courtiers of King John. When that king came across to Ireland, Theobald joined his following and was rewarded with extensive country in north Tipperary and the southern part of Wicklow. Equally telling, he received the lucrative wine-patent for Ireland, an office from which the family name de Bouteillier, meaning bottle-bearer, gaelicised as de Buitléir, has its source. The patent gave the family a payment on all wine brought into the country, and the privilege stayed with them down to the year eighteen eleven, at which point the Crown purchased it back.Much later the powerful Earls of Ormond chose to give their ancestor a heroic image. In a genealogical book written in the sixteenth century it is said that Theobald first landed at an tInbhear Mor, the bay of Arklow, and that as he came ashore he drew his sword and set its point in the ground. He made a pledge that he would not return the sword to its sheath until he reached the furthest part of Ireland, unless death came first. The story holds that he kept his word, never stopping until he came to Inis Umhaill, that is Innishowle in west Mayo, and then he returned to Arklow, sheathed his sword, and said the coast there was so fine that he would live and be buried there.This affair is plainly made up, turning Theobald into a great conqueror and giving him events from the wider family history. The author neatly changed the wine-patent into a bigger thing, presenting it as the very first royal warrant from an English master that allowed a conquest to be undertaken on Irish ground. King John did give Arklow to him, but he was not buried there, although his heirs who came after him were. The writer took his picture from the tales told of Amhairghin Glungheal, counted the earliest of the Gaels to tread on Ireland and the one credited with raising the stone causeway that crosses Arklow. The kindred did lay hold of territory in County Mayo through force of arms, yet only after Theobald had long since vanished, and the piece of ground served the writer because it came under Ormond rule in the sixteenth century. By that time the Butlers governed nearly the whole of the two counties of Tipperary and Kilkenny, and their stately strongholds remain standing in those parts today.
```
