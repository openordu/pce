Butler Margaret, whom her own people called Mairéad Ni Ghearoid, was a celebrated noblewoman of her day who lived from about 1466 until about 1539. Her father was the great Earl of Kildare, Geardid Mor FitzGerald, and from that descent came the Irish name by which she was usually greeted. When she wedded Piaras Rua Butler, destined to become the 8th Earl of Ormond, the match was intended to bind together the two great rival Norman kindreds, the Geraldines and the Butlers. Yet the alliance never delivered the peace it held out. Margaret witnessed the English authorities, with her husband abetting them, lay waste to her nearest blood relations. To gain the Earldom, Piaras Rua first had to overcome a cousin, and through those hard years the Kildare Geraldines stood solidly behind him. For a long spell he lived in a desperate fashion, and accounts close to his own lifetime say he was forced to hide among the trees and thickets of the forests. Margaret accompanied him throughout that troubled time, and the old narratives relate how once, in the wilderness when she was far gone in pregnancy, she entreated him to fetch her some wine. The plea moved her husband to a fresh burst of valour, and he killed his rival and thereby took the Earldom. It is evident that in her day Margaret carried a name for an enormous appetite. Edmund Campion, writing in 1571, admired her as a rare woman whose good sense was equal to ruling a kingdom, had her stomach not ruled over her. Richard Stanihurst, six years later, was harsh toward her because she took her husband's side while he intrigued against her Kildare kinsfolk. Even so Stanihurst conceded that she had a mixed nature, esteeming her a sure friend and a bitter enemy who did not easily abandon what she had taken to, nor take to what she disliked, and depicting her as strong-bodied, tall, rich and generous. Other writers praise her as devout and giving, and one credits her with being the foremost lady of her age for hospitality and munificence. The Butler faction that Piaras had bested was likely angrier at her than at him, since she was the channel through which he obtained the means to defeat them; the Geraldine household, for its part, held her to blame for abetting the ungratefrul conspiracies that Piaras spun against them. In folklore she has come down to us as a powerful yet grasping and vengeful figure. Her tales were told in County Kilkenny down to quite recent times, where people knew her as Mairéad Ni Gheardid and likewise as the great Countess. A nineteenth-century account declares that she rivalled, if she did not beat, her husband in feats of arms, and says she kept a large following of armed retainers, well drilled and equipped, with whose aid she squeezed tribute from feebler neighbours. All the same the folk stories inclined toward the intimate, delighting in the stratagems the Countess devised to prise goods out of one person after another. While she dwelt in Granagh Castle in the south of Kilkenny, the tradition runs, she paid many visits to the chief of the Mandevilles, whose handsome estate lay at Ballydine near Carrick-on-Suir. There she came upon a contrivance that set a bell ringing through the mansion whenever a salmon was hauled from a net her host's servants had laid in the river Suir. Consumed by envy, she cried that Ballydine would one day be hers, but Mandeville rebuffed her sharply. Undaunted, the Countess lavished the praise of the envious upon his sixteen fine sons, and before long every boy had died. The legend draws on two sources: the motif of a bell fixed to a salmon-net, which recurs in the stories of Cano mac Gartnain and of Comhdhan, and the folk conviction that a spiteful tongue can bring physical harm. It was very likely this belief that won her the name an Chuntaois mbhallaithe, meaning the cursed Countess. Another legend carries her to the seat of the Walsh leader, styled an tOidhre Breathnach, at Inchacarran Castle. A bountiful host, he once bade her name a dish she fancied and he would have it dressed for her. She answered that above all things she craved a plate of dainty morsels, of the kind drawn only from a tiny part of an ox's thigh. Walsh knew he had been beguiled, yet his given word left him no honourable escape, and he had to slay as much of his herd as the pleasure demanded. In that manner seven whole herds were spent to gratify the ravenous hunger of the Countess of Ormond. It is added that she set her heart on his lands, and once more, while entertained merrily in his house, begged as a trivial favour the use of as much ground as a bottle of flax-seed could be sown over. He granted it at once, then saw he had been beguiled again, for she meant to fling the seed across the breadth of his fields. This time he was ready, so as she arrived, servant at her side bearing the bottle, he pointed her to begin sowing amid the thick undergrowth of Coolanass, where the scattered seed would quickly come to nothing. It was also told that she abducted the son of the Walsh chief, an outrage that stirred up bitter enmity between them. Their loathing grew so strong that the Countess and Walsh each hanged the envoys the other had despatched. The tree on which Margaret strung up her doomed messenger was remembered for ages as Sceach na Cuntaoise, the Countess' Bush, a great white-thorn standing in the parish of Tullahought. Farther north in Kilkenny she was said to have swung offenders from a stone that jutted out before her stone seat on top of Ballyragget Castle. Even if some kernel of truth behind the tale endures, such a cruel name was hardly earned; it reads instead as a remnant of the violent temper of her age and of the perilous corner she occupied in it. That she was a Geraldine set down among Butlers finds its echo in a legend of her own sept, one that Kilkenny directly attached to her. So it is said that from the great castle at Carrick-on-Suir she one day spied Butler of Kilcash bowling along in his carriage, and by her magic his horses sank into the paving; he in turn raised horns on her brow, which left her wedged fast in the window frame, to the vast amusement of the onlookers.
