``` tab [group1:Info]
::: magazinestyle
The bull is a symbolic animal in Celtic tradition. Its image appears across Europe before the rise of the Celts. That wide spread points to a pre-Celtic origin. The Celts took up the bull in image and in story. They joined it to ferocity on one side and to farm plenty on the other. MacKillop ranks the bull below the boar and the stag in Celtic esteem. Persian, Syrian, Minoan, and Iberian cultures gave the animal a larger place. Some Continental sanctuaries show traces of bull sacrifice. Bull skulls sometimes guarded the entrances of shrines. The god Esus was tied to the bull and could appear as a woodsman cutting trees among bulls. Scholars pair the tree and the bull as twin images of sacrifice. Celtic art shows the bull from the Urnfield period around 800 BC. Early pieces often set the bird called the egret beside the bull. The word for the animal holds nearly one form across all Celtic languages. Old Irish tarb, Scottish Gaelic tarbh, Manx tarroo, Welsh tarw, Cornish tarow, and Breton tarv all name it. Celtic settlers in Asia Minor called their divine bull Deotaros. The Gauls honored Tarvos Trigaranus, a bull with three horns. A carving of that bull survives in the Cluny Museum in Paris. Bulls appeared on Gaulish coins. The name Donnotaurus, meaning brown or kingly bull, points to their veneration. Stone figures of bulls stand near Burghead in Morayshire. Heroes such as Furbaide Ferbend wear bull horns in the stories. The bull stands for strength and ferocity in Irish texts. Ulster loses its cattle in the Táin Bó Cúailnge because of one bull. That bull, Donn Cúailnge, was a transformed swineherd. Its only equal was Finnbennach, the white-horned bull of Ailill mac Máta, also a transformed swineherd. The two bulls fight, and the victory of the brown bull closes the tale. Commentators judge both bulls divine in origin. The bull as a sign of plenty joins the wider weight of cattle among the herding Celts. Gaelic Scotland knows the water bull tarbh uisge, and the Isle of Man knows the tarroo ushtey. Both water bulls are less malign than the water horses. A tradition at the stone called Clach nan Tarbh tells of a Scottish bull that beat an English rival.
```
