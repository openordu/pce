``` tab [group1:Info]
::: magazinestyle
The banshee is a female death wraith in Irish and Scottish Gaelic tradition. Her name comes from the Irish bean sí, an older ben síde, meaning a woman of the Otherworld. The name also carries the sense of a woman of the fairy mound. Long ago she was a goddess of the land and of sovereignty. Folk tradition narrowed her to one duty. She wails for a family about to lose one of its own. She foretells a death in a household but does not cause it. Her cry is her clearest sign. It is a keening or lament, full of the grief of kin and community. The wail most often resembles the keening of Irish mourners. In the south east the sound can also alarm and threaten. It comes at twilight, midnight, or dawn. It is heard at the edge of a farm, a townland, a well, or a stream. The sound stays near the old family home and land. Neighbours hear it even when a kinsman dies far from home. The kin and the neighbours hear the cry, not the dying person. People accept death as certain once they hear the cry. People picture her as a small and old woman with long white hair. That image fits her place as the ancestor of the family she follows and her link with death. In the south east she can instead be a tall, beautiful young woman. That shape recalls the women of the Otherworld in old Irish writing or the sí-bhean of 18th-century vision poetry. Portrayals in literature stayed uncommon before the 17th century. Since then writers have drawn her so often that broad generalizations fail. She may appear as a beautiful maiden in tears over a loved one about to die. She may instead be a gruesome hag who foretells the loss. Lady Jane Wilde argued in 1888 that the beautiful form appears more often in Ireland. She judged the ugly form more common in Scotland. Fedelm in the Táin Bó Cuailnge looks like an early form of her. Both figures often wear white. In Meath her dress is a white gown and red shoes. She can also wear a grey cloak over a green dress. Weeping has left her eyes fiery red. The lovely form strokes her long hair with a comb of gold or silver. Scottish names for her include ban-sith, bean-shith, and bean sith. A close Scottish relative is the baobhan sith, a figure that can carry succubus or vampire traits. The Manx ben shee stands closer to the Irish figure. Older and regional spellings include badh in east Munster, banshie, bean si, bean sidhe, and ben side. English speakers carried the tradition onward, and the figure then blended with the White Lady known in other folklores. The word bean means woman yet echoes bán, white or fair-haired. Behind her stands a line of old goddesses of the land. In the eighth-century tale Táin Bó Fraích, the women of the Otherworld cry out the death of the hero. At their head is his mother, the goddess Bé Find, sister of Bóand, who gave the Boyne its name. She is tied to the land-goddess line and to the very kingship of the territory. Her oldest shape is the washerwoman. The old war-goddess washes the blood-stained clothes of men who are doomed in battle. In later lore she still beats clothes in a stream the night before a fight.
```
