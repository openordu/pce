``` tab [group1:Info]
::: magazinestyle
A bruiden is a great lodging house in the Irish legends. The word takes different forms across the languages. Old Irish has bruiden, plural bruidne. Modern Irish has bruidhean, and Scottish Gaelic has bruighean. The Old Irish term can name a hostel, a large banqueting hall, or a mansion. That sense can imply the Otherworld or leave it out. A second Old Irish meaning of bruiden is fight, contest, or quarrel. The later forms often name a fairy residence. They can also mean a hostel, a caravanserai, or a royal castle. The story of Mac Dá Thó's pig lists the hostels of Ireland. It names the houses of Mac Da Réo, Da Choca, Da Derga, Da Thó, and Forgal Manach. A sixth hostel, called Bruiden Blaí Briugad, appears in the tale of Da Choca's hostel. MacKillop counts five or six such bruidne in early Ireland. Da Derga kept one in Leinster, among the men of Cualu. Writers usually set it on the River Dodder in County Dublin. Others identify it with ruins at Stackallan Bridge in County Meath, inside the Boyne valley. Forgal Manach kept one beside Lusk, north of Dublin. Da Réo kept one in Bréifhe, also called Bruiden Mic Cecht Da Réo. Da Choca kept one at Breenmore Hill near Athlone in County Westmeath. Cormac met his death there in Togail Bruidne Da Choca. Mac Da Thó kept the fifth, the hall at the center of Scéla Mucce Meic Dá Thó. Anne Ross held that the dread around the bruiden grew from burning human offerings in wicker figures before Christianity. The tales show the rich hospitality of these halls. Seven doors lead into the building. Seven ways run through it. Seven hearths keep warm seven cauldrons, and every cauldron holds a whole ox with a side of bacon. Such details set out the great generosity given to guests in these legendary Irish halls.
```
