``` tab [group1:Info]
::: magazinestyle
The head cult treats the severed head as a sacred object. This custom grew strong in southern Gaul. Northern and western reaches took it up later. The cult may have spread with Roman influence. Roman Britain kept the practice until late antiquity. Oil soaked skulls lay near the Wroxeter forum. That town dates from the fourth century. The Irish Ulster Cycle records such head hunting. Cath Maige Tuired sketches a gifted physician. He could heal any wound except a severed head. Classical authors describe these habits in detail. Diodorus says the Gauls severed enemy heads. They hung heads from the horses' necks. The best ones went onto house walls. Posidonius tells of cedar oil preservation. Proud owners refused even gold for a head. Medieval law texts carried a face price. Old Irish called this value log n-enech. Welsh named it wynebwerth. Livy tells of a Roman defeat by the Boii. The Roman general lost his head in battle. The tribe mounted the skull with gold. They raised it as a cup for the god. Severed heads adorn Continental Celtic sculpture. The Pfalzfeld pillar shows carved heads on each side. A lost head once crowned that pillar. Tripple-headed gods appear across religious sites. Sacred enclosures kept these grisly trophies. This theme persisted through many Celtic lands.

:::
```
