``` tab [group1:Info]
::: magazinestyle
The hazel is one of the most revered trees among the Celts. Species of the genus Corylus. The Celts believed the hazel bore nuts of wisdom. The nuts grew where the tree stood at the sources of great rivers. Fish ate the fallen nuts. The salmon above all else ate them. Each nut left a bright spot on the flesh of the fish. The fish with the most spots carried the most wisdom. Whoever ate that fish gained that wisdom in turn. The Boyne and the Shannon both held sacred hazel groves at their sources. Uneaten nuts rose again as bubbles of inspiration. The Shannon source in County Cavan bubbles up from underground springs that feed the pool. Hazel trees shaded Connla's Well in County Tipperary. Anyone who ate the nuts there became a brilliant bard and a brilliant scholar. The Well of Segais, named for the source of the Boyne, held nine hazel trees. Mortals who ate the hazelnuts or the blessed salmon gained the gifts of prophecy and poetry. Bards carved their staffs from hazel wood. Burning the wood of the hazel was taboo. Hazel twigs still guide water-witches to underground streams. In the Americas the unrelated witch-hazel, genus Hamamelis, carries the same reported power. Hazel and thorn are trees that fairies were thought to inhabit. In Ireland people once described Tara as a hazel grove. In Scotland the place-name Carlton, from the old Celtic Calltuinn, marks the site where a sacred hazel grove once thrived. MacKillop adds that the hazel bush or small tree has shaped Irish and Welsh tradition. It has shaped that tradition through both its wood and its nuts. Hazel leaves and hazel nuts appear in early British burial mounds. They appear in shaft-wells too, especially at Ashill in Norfolk. The early name Fordruim, a byname for Tara, sits at the heart of a place-name story. That story paints Tara as a pleasant hazel wood. That wood grew around the royal seat. In the ogham script of early Ireland the letter C was carried by hazel. The Old Irish form of the word is coll. The hazel also marked the ninth month in the Old Irish calendar. That month ran from 6 August to 2 September in the older reckoning. Young warriors of the Fianna had to defend themselves. They bore only a hazel stick and a shield. In the Fenian legends the leafless hazel stood as an evil thing. It dripped poisonous milk. It gave a home to vultures. Across Ireland and Wales the hazel was a fairy tree. Its wood was sacred to poets. For that reason it was a forbidden fuel on any hearth.
```
