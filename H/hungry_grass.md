``` tab [group1:Info]
::: magazinestyle
The hungry grass is an Irish folkloric site born of the Famine called An Gorta Mór. English occupation denied Irish farmers land ownership and bound most as tenants of absent landlords. The potato, a South American import, thrived in rainy Irish soil. Less than an acre fed a family. A single crop invited disaster when blight struck. From 1848 to 1850 an airborne fungus rotted stored potatoes into black slime. Cattle stood ready for market but belonged to landlords. Tenant farmers starved in the millions. Ireland still exported food through those years. Between one and one and a half million people died and a similar number emigrated. The current Irish population sits at half its pre-famine figure. Patches of soil infected with the famine memory strike anyone who steps on them with a hunger no meal satisfies. Such patches grow over unmarked graves and along roadsides where the hungry died. MacKillop records the Irish names féargortach and fódgortach. He traces the belief to oral tradition of unknown date, perhaps the Famine years of around 1846 to 1848. He describes a bewitched tuft that inflicts the same unquenchable hunger on whoever walks across it. A related and less familiar tradition tells of the fear gortach, the hungry man. He asks passing strangers for charity. He pays back those who give. The hungry grass also passes under the name fairy grass. Twentieth century Irish literature returns to the image often. Donagh MacDonagh published a 1947 London poetry collection titled The Hungry Grass. Richard Powers released a 1969 novel of the same name, published in London and New York. MacKillop points readers onward to the entry for fóidín mearaí.
```
