``` tab [group1:Info]
::: magazinestyle
Heilyn is a minor hero of Welsh legend, and one word for a cup-bearer carries more than one of the name. Not every use of it marks the same man.

The fullest of them, Heilyn the son of Gwyn, shows up on the far isle of Gwales. A small band keeps a good table there after the war with the Irish while a sealed door of the west stands shut against the storms of the world. Heilyn draws that door open with an idle hand, and the spell of the quiet land breaks at once. From that hour the company must feel the grief they had put off. His cup-bearer title still speaks of pouring, but his part in the tale is the unlucky opening.

The other of the name, Heilyn the Red, stands beside a sleeper rather than a feast. He has the charge of a climbing bed, and into that bed the mind of a dejected knight falls when the tale of his dream turns to the telling. The whole vision sits inside that shabby room, and the Red one is its keeper. His name says he waits on the cup, and the red in it comes from his own coloring. So the same word does a quiet duty in both stories.
The two Heilyn are not the same man, and the shared title binds a cup-bearer of the high tale to the keeper of a low room. In the tale the son of Gwyn keeps company with the handful left alive after the Irish war under Matholwch, and his idle hand at the west door ends the long forgetting on Gwales.

:::
```
