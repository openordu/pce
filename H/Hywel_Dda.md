``` tab [group1:Info]
::: magazinestyle
Hywel Dda was a Welsh hero and king. His name means Hywel the Good. He ruled in the tenth century, from about 910 to about 950. He began as king of the south-west region of Dyfed. Through his reign he took control over the north and other lands. He gained power over Gwynedd and Powys. His reach soon spread across nearly all of Wales. The English Crown made no move to stop it. He chose to keep the peace with the English kings rather than to fall in with the Vikings who fought them. He kept his true aims hidden behind a quiet, simple manner as he visited the English courts. This close tie with the West Saxon house gave him standing and kept his kingdom free. The death of the English king Æthelstan in 940 raised his power still further. Hywel alone among early Welsh kings placed his image on a coin. A short penny struck at Chester copied the coinage of Wessex and Mercia. Its style set it near the coins of Eadmund, Æthelstan's brother. Hywel Dda is best known for a body of law. In the Middle Ages every Welsh law was called the Law of Hywel. The law books tell that he called six wise men from each hundred region of Wales, with the chief clergy, to a meeting at the White House on the Taf in Dyfed. The men prayed and fasted so that God might bless the change of the old Welsh laws. In time the story grew, but its heart stayed firm. He had a clear part in the first written Welsh law. His power over nearly all of the island grew after his new order over the north in 942. The laws hold a trace of Old English too. The Welsh word distain, a steward, comes from the Old English disc-thegn. The Welsh word edling, the heir, comes from Old English aetheling. Such marks mirror his close work with the court of Wessex. The title Good came to him long after his life, first in the twelfth-century book of the church of Llandaf. He is the only Welsh king to carry it. The name Hywel itself, made from a word that means see, first meant open and honest, and so fits a ruler who was open with his people.

MacKill sees him first as a lawgiver. The Good King set a native body of rule for Wales that the books carry as a counterpart to the Irish Brehon code. The laws live in over seventy manuscripts, most of them written down before the sixteenth age, when they were still put to use. He is held to be the only Welsh king to have cut his own image into a coin.
```
