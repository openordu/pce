``` tab [group1:Info]
::: magazinestyle
Fairy ring is a folkloric motif. MacKillop describes it as a circle on lawn or pasture land said to come from fairies at their dancing. Science reads the mark plainly. The ground fungus Marasmius oreades spreads its mycelia outward and lays down the circle. Sometimes the ring shows only as a shallow dip in the grass. At other times mushrooms spring up along it. A human who steps inside is pulled into the wild dance of the fairies. The dance seems to last a few minutes to the dancer. In truth it runs seven years or longer. A circle of darker green on a grassy field marked where fairies danced about their queen. The ring kept its charm even with no fairy present. Livestock were kept off it, and the plow did not touch it. Tales tell of plagues that struck cattle which grazed a fairy ring. Others tell of people who stepped into a ring and danced until they could not stop, some to death. The MacKillop rescue matches the older tale. A helper outside the circle could save one by seizing the coat-tails that trailed beyond the ring. Anyone who reached inside felt the same urge to dance. Celtic tongues carry the idea widely. Irish speakers say fáinne sidhe, and Scottish Gaelic gives fàinne sith. Manx uses fainey shee, and Welsh offers cylch y tylwyth teg or twmpath chwarae.

:::
```
