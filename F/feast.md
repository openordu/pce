``` tab [group1:Info]
::: magazinestyle
Feasts were central to Celtic social and religious life, both as ritual banquets and as festival days. Archaeology shows that Iron Age aristocrats drank wine from Greek and Etruscan vessels and placed rich drinking gear in their graves, as at Hochdorf and Vix. Classical writers such as Athenaeus, drawing on Posidonius, describe the banquets of the Gaulish leader Lovernios, where praise poetry earned gold, and the custom of giving the choice cut of meat to the best warrior, the champion's portion. Early Irish tales make the feast a stage for rivalry: in Fled Bricrenn, Bricriu's feast goads the Ulster heroes into competing for precedence, and contention over the champion's portion could end in bloodshed. The Tuath Dé won immortality at the feast of Goibniu, and the feast of Tara, Feis Temro, marked the inauguration of kings. Celtic words for feast, such as Old Irish fled, Welsh gwledd, and Breton gloé, all descend from Proto-Celtic *wlid-. The Irish word feis, often translated 'feast', literally means 'spending the night' and carried a ritual sense of royal union. Feasts also came to mark holy ground: churches rose over old holy sites, pagan divinities were 'canonized' as saints, and Ireland followed the same pattern as the other Celtic lands.

:::
```
