``` tab [group1:Info]
::: magazinestyle
Fairy belief was never the same for all people. In the Celtic lands it stayed alive for long and even the priests argued about it. The names people used show care and fear. Folk called the beings the Good People, in Irish Na Daoine Maith, and in Welsh the Fair Family, the Tylwyth Teg. The old Irish word sid points to the fairy mound. From it grew words like side and sidheog, and the people of the mounds, the aes side. That folk of the mounds ties close to the old gods, the Tuatha Dé Danann. Green was the fairies' colour, so people turned from green dress. Iron also kept them away, a habit some read as a memory of older peoples. Fairies were taken for a danger, able to harm or even kill. A person carried into their realm might dance for what seemed an hour and come back to find years gone. A sick child was sometimes held for a fairy changeling. In Brittany the dead and the fairies were bound close, and many small beings counted part of the community of the dead, the Anaon. In Scotland a fairy queen ruled, known as Nic Neven. The Isle of Man had its brownie, the fynnoderee, and its water horse, the glashtyn. Wales knew the pwca, a restless sprite, and the wandering Tylwyth Teg. The piskies and the treasure-guarding spriggans belong to Cornwall. One older book sets out these beings of the countryside and other wonders. That survey of fairy folk set down the common beings first, and it came from a New York press in the year 1976.

:::
```
