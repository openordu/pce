``` tab [group1:Info]
::: magazinestyle
Fairies are supernatural beings of Celtic folk belief who appear in many sizes and forms, from tiny sprites to giants, and who dwell in mounds, caves, lakes, and other natural places. Folk tradition explains them in several ways: some say they are the souls of the dead, others hold that they are fallen angels, and still others treat them as nature spirits. Fairies bring both good and ill. They reward musical skill and second sight, yet they also steal healthy children and leave changelings in their place, and they were blamed for strokes, nightmares, and mental illness. People avoided offending them by using euphemistic names such as the Good People or the Welsh Tylwyth Teg, by shunning green clothing, and by keeping iron close at hand. Time inside the fairy realm runs faster than mortal time, so a night of fairy dancing can cost a human decades of ordinary life. Each Celtic country has its own fairy kinds: Ireland has the sí folk of the mounds, Scotland knows NicNeven as fairy queen, the Isle of Man has the fynnoderee and the buggane, Wales has the Tylwyth Teg, Brittany has the korrigans and the dead-like Anaon, and Cornwall has piskies, spriggans, and mine knockers. Tales of marriage between fairy women and mortal men, as in the story from Wales of a man who married a lake fairy at Llyn y Fan Fach, explain the rise of lucky families and of healing dynasties. David MacRitchie once argued that fairy belief preserves the memory of earlier peoples such as the Picts; scholars have abandoned that idea, but it lives on in popular culture.

:::
```
