``` tab [group1:Info]
::: magazinestyle
Fasting was a Celtic ritual with real force. In Irish it took the name troscad, and in Welsh ymprydio. To fast against an enemy stood as a tradition of protest in Ireland and Wales. Old Irish narrative uses ritual fasting, or the hunger strike, as a frequent device. Those stories mirror early Irish custom. Under the Brehon Laws, ritual fasting held standing as an established way to compel justice and to establish individual rights. Tradition calls it an infallible way to open the gates of a great warrior fortress. A victim of a wrong sat on the doorstep of the wrongdoer. He took no food and told passersby of the crime. Even persons of lower rank could press for redress this way. They sat at the door of the person accused and refused food until justice was paid. Kings and bards could dodge the eric, or honour price. For the harmed person, fasting stood as the only reprisal. The protest often worked and set matters right. Christian saints, so tradition says, fasted at the grave of Fergus mac Róich. He rose from death and then recited the Táin for them. The practice is not uniquely Celtic. Hindu tradition knows the same rite as dharnia. It returned time and again as nonviolent protest. Irish political life saw it recur through the 19th and 20th centuries.

:::
```
