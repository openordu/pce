``` tab [group1:Info]
::: magazinestyle
Fosterage was the practice of handing a child over to adults other than its own parents to be raised for a set span of years under fixed terms. When Ireland emerges in the historic period, the custom is already well established, and its terms carry affection: aite for foster-father, muime for foster-mother, altram for fosterage itself, with its sense of feeding and nourishing, and dalta for the foster-child. Irish law recognized three ages of fosterage: up to seven years, from seven to twelve, and from twelve to seventeen. Fosterage was a formal contract, either for payment or for affection. The fee was fixed by rank, took the form of a cattle payment, cost more for a female child, and the foster-parents handed it back when the child went home; the fosterer lost two-thirds of the fee if a required skill was not taught, or if the child was blemished. Education matched rank: the sons of freemen learned farming crafts such as kiln-drying and the use of the quern, while the children of higher grades learned board games, horse-riding, and embroidery. Both the maternal and the paternal kin shared the cost, and the mother's kin could protest a placement. Foster-parents held real legal power over the child, including the right of witness and judgement in proceedings. On completion the foster-child received the sét gertha, a payment that ensured the foster-parents' upkeep in old age. Many held the bond of fosterage as five times more durable than the bond of blood, and the most intense bonds in Irish mythology run between fosterlings: the combat of the hero Cuchulainn against his foster brother Ferdiad reaches Ferdiad's death after three days. Fosterage also existed in Wales, where the law said a child is 'at his father's dish', and where fostering with a bondman could, with the lord's consent, let the foster-son inherit land if the boy stayed past a full year and a day. The Mabinogi tale of Pwyll counts the support of a foster-son among the benefits of the custom. The Welsh term cyfaill came to mean 'friend', while its Irish counterpart comaltae meant 'foster-brother'.
```
