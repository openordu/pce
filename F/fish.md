``` tab [group1:Info]
::: magazinestyle
Fish held a thin place in the Celtic record. A famine fragment joins a protective fire ritual. In County Limerick, dancers received a tap from a cut reed to ward off illness. The reeds went into a fire, where the contagion burned away. Dancers leapt over the flames. People then used the ashes to bless their harvest and livestock. In Scotland, flame shielded and cleansed at the same time. One ritual carried a flame around a house, moving sunwise, to guard the building and its people. When famine or epidemic threatened, the need-fire served. Each hearth in that area was put out. A team of men rubbed planks of wood to strike a flame. Each family took a flame from the fresh fire home. The men doused the old fire and spread its ashes on cattle for protection. Pictish and Gallo-Roman altars rarely carried the sea creatures. Most named sea creatures in Celtic lore stand outside fish in the narrow sense. The salmon stands as the chief of them. St Brendan and his followers landed on the whale Jasconius.

:::
```
