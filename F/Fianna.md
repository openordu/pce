``` tab [group1:Info]
::: magazinestyle
The Fianna are a band of Irish warriors. Fían is the older singular word for a band. The plural form fianca entered later. Young men and outcasts filled the ranks. These bands hunted and raided the border lands. They shared the gain among the whole host. Old songs keep their deeds in verse.

The band fills a real social role. It draws restless young men from their home kin. Old tales also name young women in a band. The host grants a life outside the settled world. It trains those men toward adult duty. They learn the craft of the hunt and the war. They learn the fair rules of camp life. Some learn the poet's craft as well. This same shape is far from a single land. Many old peoples of one root share it. Meanwhile the band is bound to its men. It runs beyond the king's own grip. So it stood apart out on the edge.

An oath and pledge bind the members. Each warrior keeps his own fixed gear. To break that pledge draws a failing fall. The wolf and the deer stand as its emblems. A new man passes a deep rite of the path. That rite brings him to the duties of the man. A chief, the rígfhéinnid, holds all the band. His word holds the host as a single body. Other hosts keep chiefs of their own. A rival man, Fothad Canainne, leads a rival host.

Hunting and war shape each single day. Raids pull in loot and clear a score. The band plays hard games of skill and pride. The age does not own a standing royal host. A ruler can still hire the band as force. That force is a ready, needed arm.

Their deeds grow into a great cycle. The word Fiannaíocht is the body of that story. It is a long and lasting cycle in the lore. The lore of both lands carries its tales. It fixes the rise and the fall of heroes. Fionn mac Cumhaill holds the center. Finn mac Cumaill keeps his earlier name. He is the warrior, the leader, and the poet. He reads the seer and knows the times.

A later tale meets the old with the new. The old men who survived the world meet the saint. They recall their lost best, and take the faith. They turn to the promise of the bright good news. The page marks the end of the old way firm.

Later ages hang new names on the set. The Fenian host names it in modern speech. That word draws from the old name for the fían. The Ossian name comes from a scholar of the north. He gave one hero, Oisín, a place in his verse. The lore says Oisín is a son of the hero. A sharp quarrel arose around his work. It turns the whole theme into a field of study.

One old tale keeps a false man of a demon name. He leads the whole band to a thin, weak horse. The horse draws the host into a deep fold. There they remain bound and kept for many years. Fionn opens the long way back at last. The whole band steps out to home. The false man is given no part in the host.

:::
```
