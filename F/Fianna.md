``` tab [group1:Info]
::: magazinestyle
The Fianna are the great warrior band of Irish tradition, and in Old Irish the word fían meant a warring and hunting band, above all a company of young men and social outsiders bonded together on the border zones between territories. These warriors, called féinnidi, lived by hunting, fighting, and raiding, and they trained in martial and athletic games, the fíanchluichi, and even in poetry. The institution prepared young men for adult duties, much like male-bonding groups in other Indo-European societies, and it appears in law tracts and annals as a real presence in early medieval Ireland; clerics condemned the destructiveness of fían bands, and saints' lives show holy men converting roaming raiders known as the sons of death. The wolf and the deer served as the mascots of the fían. The band of Fionn mac Cumhaill became the most famous fían of all. In one tale, the figure Abarta tricked the warriors onto his frail old horse, which carried them to the Otherworld, where he held them hostage; after many magical exploits and battles, Fionn freed them, and afterward did not invite Abarta to join the band.

:::
```
