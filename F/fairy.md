``` tab [group1:Info]
::: magazinestyle
fairy names the diminutive supernatural beings in human form that appear across modern Celtic traditions. English uses the single word fairy where the Celtic languages each carry a fuller vocabulary. That mismatch has long hampered careful discussion. Celtic fairy beings may be clever or mischievous. They can help or hinder human endeavour. Learned observers like Giraldus Cambrensis recorded the earliest references. He worked around 1146 to 1223. Oral tradition supplied the great bulk of later lore. The portrayal shows a quasi-orthodoxy that runs through English Scandinavian and Continental material. Scandinavian parallels include the hulda-folk. The absence of a single shared Celtic term for fairy implies the absence of one discrete Celtic tradition. Irish offers sidheog in unreformed form, síóg in reformed form, sheogue as the anglicized shape, and boctogai. Scottish Gaelic uses sithiche. Manx uses ferrish. Welsh uses tylwyth teg, rendered in English as fair family. Cornish records spyrys for spirit. Breton records korriganez and boudig. Courtesy euphemisms for the fairy include several forms. Irish offers daoine maithe, the good people. Irish also uses daoine sídhe, also written áes sídhe or aos si, people of the mound. Welsh euphemisms include bendith y mamau, mother's blessings. Breton uses the equivalent bonnes dames. Cornish uses the phrasal fairy people. Manx lists ny guillyn beggey, ny mooinjer veggey, and ny sleih veggey. Scottish Gaelic uses daoine sith for the parallel mound people.

:::
```
