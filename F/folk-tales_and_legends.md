``` tab [group1:Info]
::: magazinestyle
Folk-tales and legends are the two great branches of traditional narrative collected across the Celtic countries. In folklore scholarship, the folk-tale is a fictional story told for entertainment, with a linear plot and casual magic, while the legend is a tale regarded as plausible within the traditional worldview, from saints' lives to urban legends; myth, by contrast, tells of sacred events held to be true. Because no narrative literature from the pre-Christian period survives in the Celtic languages, Celtic mythology is known mainly through later art and literature. The Irish collections are among the richest in Europe: the index Types of the Irish Folktale catalogues more than 43,000 versions of over 700 tale types. Collectors such as Thomas Crofton Croker, correspondent of the Grimm brothers, Lady Wilde, W. B. Yeats, Jeremiah Curtin, and Douglas Hyde gathered the Irish repertoire, while the professional storyteller, the seanchaí, passed on lore that mixed genealogy, history, and tale. Scottish Gaelic tradition was recorded by John Francis Campbell and later by Edinburgh's School of Scottish Studies, and Manx, Welsh, Breton, and Cornish collectors built their own archives, from Sophia Morrison's Manx Fairy Tales to the Breton work of François-Marie Luzel and the Cornish collections of Robert Hunt and William Bottrell. The Welsh Mabinogi, the story from Wales of the fairy bride who left her mortal husband at Llyn y Fan Fach. The legend of Gelert shows how deeply folk narrative shaped the Celtic literary imagination.
```
