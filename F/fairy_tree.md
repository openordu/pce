``` tab [group1:Info]
::: magazinestyle
The fairy tree stands as a key religious symbol in Celtic belief. Nearly every species in the Celtic countries carried special power or sheltered the fairies. MacKillop ranks the trees by esteem. The magical trio of oak, ash, and thorn holds first place. Fruit-bearing trees follow next, the apple and the hazel. The alder, the elder, the holly, and the willow come after them. The ranking shifts across the Celtic world. On Man the phrase fairy tree names the tramman, the local elder. The hawthorn, or thorn, binds most tightly of all to the fairy world. Its spiky thorns, white blossoms, and red haws won favor with the Good People. Some thorns marked fairy land. Thorns above old mounds, known as raths, counted as fairy land. So did a thorn standing alone in a stony field. A set of three thorns also marked the ground. Planted beside an oak and an ash, a thorn framed the most sacred grove. Stories told of harm falling on anyone who felled a fairy tree. A hospital planned at Kiltimagh in Co. Mayo met such a case in the 20th century. The only field there held two fairy trees, and no one would cut either. At last one man agreed, and he fell to a fairy stroke the next day. The hospital still rose without incident. An American-owned factory in Ulster stood over the roots of a sacrificed fairy tree. Its ruin foreshadowed the fall of the DeLorean car company. D. A. MacManus gave Irish fairy trees a chapter in The Middle Kingdom, printed in London in 1959. Reverence endures in Ireland. In 1999 the schoolmaster Eddie Lenihan pressed planners to move a road so it would spare a Co. Clare fairy thorn. The Newmarket-on-Fergus thorn still stands beside the road out of Shannon Airport.
```
