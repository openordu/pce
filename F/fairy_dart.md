``` tab [group1:Info]
::: magazinestyle
Fairy dart is a folkloric motif also called fairy arrow or saighead sithe. MacKillop records gáe sidhe as the Irish name for flint arrowheads turned up beside raths. Those raths are ring-forts built in prehistoric times. People rarely used the Irish form of the phrase. Gáe sidhe survives in reformed spelling as ga si. Speakers in Ireland favored the English label fairy dart for these flint points. Fairy dart also serves as the colloquial name for a strange swelling in the joints, hands, and feet. Folk belief blamed malevolent fairies who flung their darts at humans for this ailment. Tiny well-made arrows littered the ground around fairy places in Scotland and Ireland. The term may once have named prehistoric stone arrowheads. A fairy smith forged the darts to fight people. Their tiny size did not spare the victims. In an Isle of Skye tale, a couple reaped at moonlight. Their tools slipped from bleeding hands with no clear cause. The next day they found the small darts the fairies had flung. The pair had drawn too near hidden hollows of the Good People. In the Scottish telling, a dart strike ranked as a fairy kidnapping. The outer shell of the victim stayed behind while the real self made its home in Fairyland. A cow struck by a dart ate as a rule but gave no milk. The darts came from the forge with hellish aid and were sharpened and polished by the wicked one. They struck both people and beasts, from spite or as pay for treading on fairy ground. The wounded rarely felt the dart at first, but their hands ached a month later with a painful swelling. The dart pierced skin without leaving a puncturing mark, so the wound stayed hard to find. Untreated, it always proved fatal. One cure was to find the dart and drink water while holding it below the surface.

:::
```
