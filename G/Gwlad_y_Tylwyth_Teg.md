``` tab [group1:Info]
::: magazinestyle
Gwlad y Tylwyth Teg is the Welsh phrase for the land of the fair folk. In everyday speech the same words can also name sleep or the grave. The phrase points to the home of the Tylwyth Teg, the fairies of Welsh custom. It thus links a place of the imagination with the country of the dead. MacKillop enters gwlad as a Welsh word for land or country. The term may name an independent small kingdom in Wales before 1282, the year of the English conquest. The term may also name a cultural region. There folk with a common dialect and a common set of customs dwelt for ages. The reader may consult T. M. Charles-Edwards for the broader scholarly treatment of gwlad. His 1993 Oxford study, called Early Irish and Welsh Kinship, sets the kinship field alongside the land-word. MacKillop notes that the same article gwarwyn a throt once served as a hidden name. It served in Monmouthshire, now Gwent. It named the tylwyth teg, the Welsh fairy folk. That nickname, meaning the white-naped one with the trot, kept the fairy folk out of plain speech. MacKillop's fuller entry on tylwyth teg sets out the wider lore. Tylwyth teg is in plain Welsh the typical name for fairies, literally the fair folk. People often called them by the euphemism bendith y mamau. That phrase means mother's blessings, and folk used it to avert kidnapping, especially in Glamorgan. Most tales about them come down through oral tradition. Written notices of them appear as early as the work of Giraldus Cambrensis, who lived from about 1146 to 1223. The tylwyth teg are described as fair-haired, and they love golden hair. They crave mortal children, and blond or fair colouring draws them most. Gwyn ap Nudd is their usual king. They are generally portrayed as benevolent, though they remain capable of occasional mischief. Later tales even claim improved behaviour. Some tell of fairy gifts of silver for maidens who keep their households in good order. Unlike other Celtic fairies, they are tied to lakes above all. The most famous of those lakes is Llyn y Fan Fach in the south of Wales. They also fear iron. Folk warn that babies yet to be christened must be kept safe from them. A poker over the cradle keeps the danger off. Like other fairies they possess magical cattle. The most famous of those beasts is named the Speckled Cow of Hiraethog. A common tale tells of a mortal young man who seeks to marry a beautiful fairy host daughter. She sets two conditions on her consent. He must never touch her with iron. He must not strike her with three blows. None of those three blows may be needed. The tylwyth teg and lakes such as Llyn y Fan Fach share a famous bond. That bond has led some commentators to a striking theory. They argue the tylwyth teg descend from a dark-skinned, short, early race of Britain. That race lived in crannogs, the primitive lake dwellings. That view matches one among four general theories of fairy origin. Smaller still than the tylwyth teg are the ellyll. That class may draw on a yet older stock.
```
