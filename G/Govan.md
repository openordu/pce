::: magazinestyle
Govan, an old parish and former burgh absorbed into the city of Glasgow in the southwest of Scotland, sits on the southern shore of the River Clyde and ranks among the very oldest Christian places in Scotland. During the era of Ystrad Clud, the final Brythonic kingdom in Britain, it probably served as the principal royal capital of Strathclyde, where the last kings of the Britons held court. That faded political glory is best remembered today in Govan Old Parish Church, built upon a raised oval enclosure whose boundary ditch has been radiocarbon-dated to the ninth century. The church preserves Scotland's third largest collection of medieval carving, the Govan Stones: some forty-seven pieces dominated by massive tenth- and eleventh-century gravestones, four monumental crosses, the largest known hogbacks dating to about ad 900 to 1000, a great spread of cross-marker stones, and an immense monolithic sarcophagus decorated with interlacing and a mounted warrior at the hunt. Evidence hints that the name derives from Brittonic gwo- and ban, signifying 'small hill', and that Doomster Hill nearby served as a court hill where rulers exercised law. Govan thus stands as the seat of Strathclyde power after the fall of the rock of Dumbarton in Viking raiding around ad 870 and before masonry on Glasgow cathedral began early in the twelfth century.

:::
