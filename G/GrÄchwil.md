::: magazinestyle
Grächwil is a site about ten kilometres north of Bern in Switzerland. It lies within the western Hallstatt region. Two large Hallstatt tumuli were dug there in 1851. The first mound measured roughly thirty metres across and five and a half metres high. It was probably much larger in antiquity. Later secondary burials lay in the upper layer. These date from the post-Roman migration period. They contained a sword, a knife, and other metal pieces. The lower levels held several burials covered by quarried stone. The central interment held the iron wheel tyres of about four wheels. It also held the iron attachments of a chariot. Fragments of the bronze hydria lay close to these. The second tumulus was smaller and built in the same way. It held only material the nineteenth century diggers considered insignificant. It probably dates from the Hallstatt Iron Age, about 750 to 475 bc.

The Grächwil hydria is a bronze water jar. It has been called the most important piece of Greek art found north of the Alps. Scholars identify it as Spartan in style. It was probably crafted around 580 bc at Taranto, a Greek colony in Puglia, Italy. Only the upper parts survive. This preserved section holds the two side handles and the central handle. Elements of the rim, shoulder, and body also remain. The lower half and foot are lost. The middle handle shows a winged female goddess. She is the queen of wild beasts. She wears a long belted dress and crown, and holds a hare in each hand. Lions accompany her, with an eagle perched on her crown. The hydria records trade links from the central Alpine area to the Mediterranean. This trade ran in the later Hallstatt period, about 600 to 475 bc. It offers evidence of classical Greek influence on the Celtic aristocrats of the Alpine region.
