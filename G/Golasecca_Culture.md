::: magazinestyle
The Golasecca Culture was an Iron Age society that lived in northern Italy. It thrived roughly from the eighth to the fifth century BC. Its settlement stood against the southern tip of the road that crossed the Saint Gotthard Pass. The culture linked the western Hallstatt peoples north of the Alps with the Po Valley dwellers to their south. Golasecca land stretched from that pass down to Lake Como and Lake Maggiore, and across the Po Valley plain. This position let the culture carry early Etruscan-Celtic traffic across the Alps in both directions. Four-wheeled wagons from Golasecca graves match Hallstatt wagons. Yet their iron tires, wheel hubs, and metal parts point to central Italy. Bronze vessels, situlae, and Etruscan jugs moved along routes that reached Champagne, the Moselle, and the middle Rhine. Some exports went as far as west France and north Germany. This culture yielded the oldest known Celtic-language inscriptions. The Lepontic writings use the Lugano Alphabet. Early texts arise in the sixth and fifth centuries BC. The last ones belong to the second and first centuries BC. Strong Hallstatt influence from the seventh century BC makes some scholars propose an early Celtic migration. The record of Livy holds the first Gauls entering the valley under Tarquinius Priscus. Other writers stress steady local continuity. How a Celtic tongue reached Golasecca remains unsettled. The culture likely matches the Insubres, Oromobii, and Lepontii whom Greek and Roman writers name.
