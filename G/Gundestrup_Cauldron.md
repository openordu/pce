``` tab [group1:Info]
::: magazinestyle
The Gundestrup Cauldron is a great silver vessel of the old world. Peat cutters found it in May 1891 in Rævemosen, a bog beside the hamlet of Gundestrup in Himmerland, northern Jutland, Denmark. Within two days the crown claimed it as treasure trove, and it went to the Danish National Museum. Someone had placed it in the marsh, perhaps as an offering to the gods or to hide it in danger. The bowl has thirteen silver plates, several of them partly gilded. Scholars date the bowl to the second or first century before the common era, though the date is still argued. Many call the vessel a Celtic work. The technique and style point to Thrace: animal fur is picked out with punch marks, and the silver is heavily beaten. The motifs are Celtic all the same, with Celtic helmets and carnyces, the long battle trumpets. The Cimbri may have carried it north on their raid. Figures in relief show a goddess on a cart, a horned god, a wheel-bearing bearded man, and an antlered god holding a torc and a ram-headed snake. One plate shows warriors led toward a large figure who dips a man into a vessel. That scene recalls the cauldron of rebirth in the Branwen tale, and it may picture a rite of initiation or sacrifice. The images echo the ones the Celts knew in other lands.

:::
```
