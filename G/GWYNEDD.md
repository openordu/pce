``` tab [group1:Info]
::: magazinestyle
Gwynedd was a kingdom in the north-west of Wales (Cymru). It took shape as Roman command over Britain grew weaker. The oldest known trace of its name in a Latin shape, Venedotia, sits on a carved stone from the sixth century at Penmachno. That stone records a citizen of Gwynedd. The name comes from Celtic speech, though scholars differ on its exact sense. Some connect it with an Old Irish word for free men. Gwynedd looked to Snowdonia (Eryri) as its centre. That mass of mountains made the land simple to defend. King Maelgwn, ruling in the middle of the sixth century, is the first lord with written proof. A new royal line, led by Merfyn ap Gwriad, came to the throne in 825. His son Rhodri Mawr then drew other Welsh kingdoms under one hand, though only for his own years. Hywel Dda of Deheubarth took over, and Norse lords from Dublin may have held sway in the tenth and eleventh centuries. Revival came in 1039 with Gruffudd ap Llywelyn. He controlled more of Wales than any lord before or after him. Owain Gwynedd ruled from 1137 after his father Gruffudd ap Cynan passed on a stronger realm. Llywelyn ab Iorwerth came to stand out later. His grandson Llywelyn ap Gruffudd was acknowledged Prince of Wales under the 1267 pact of Montgomery. Two wars in 1276 and 1282 broke that power. Llywelyn fell in battle in 1282, and the English then took and killed his brother Dafydd in 1283, ending independence. The English crown took Gwynedd, and the statute of 1284 split it into three shires, Anglesey, Caernarfon, and Merioneth. Those three were joined again as the county of Gwynedd in 1974.
```
