``` tab [group1:Info]
::: magazinestyle
The ganconer belongs to the folk traditions of Scotland and Ireland. Gaelic forms include ganconer, gancanagh, gean-cannach, and geancánach. The English rendering is love-talker. The figure resembles a leprechaun but proves false as a lover. MacKillop calls him an amorous leprechaun-like false fairy of Irish folk tradition. Other names are gean-cannah, gan-ceann, and gean-canach. Scottish maidens were told to avoid lonely country roads, especially in the mountains. They might meet a handsome fairy there. A clay pipe marked him, and Irish tradition names the pipe dúidín or dudeen. He made promises so sweet that the girl pined away after he vanished. The ganconer was the male form of the fairy lover. He haunts lonely valleys and directs his courtship at milkmaids and shepherdesses before abandoning them to pine toward death. In Ireland the same being was the geancanach, which favored milkmaids. Ethna Carbery portrayed the figure in her poem The Love-Talker, which appeared in Four Winds of Erin, published in Dublin in 1902. W. B. Yeats mentions ganconers playing at hurling, recorded in his volume Irish Fairy and Folk Tales, which appeared in London in 1893. Scholars catalog him under folk motif F301.2. The ganconer is confused with but distinct from the gan ceann. The word also described boastful human men.
```
