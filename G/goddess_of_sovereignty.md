``` tab [group1:Info]
::: magazinestyle
The goddess of sovereignty pictures the might of a kingdom as a woman. To win her, the king often gives a sexual union. The idea is older than any literature written in a Celtic tongue. In a Sumerian hymn of the second millennium BC, the king mates with the goddess Inanna. The day is New Year's, the place her own residence. The rite of hierogamy, the sacred marriage, ends with his union with her. Early Indo-European culture echoes that union of a male king with a divine female sovereignty. The pair of Vishnu and Sri-Lakshmi carries it as far as India. In the Celtic world, rites of sovereignty that involved horses survived in Ireland to the eve of the Normans. Giraldus Cambrensis reports them with shock in his Topographia Hibernica of 1188. The ritual banquet of kingship, the banais righe, stands in the early Irish texts. The sovereignty bride poured the libation. The king then knew Sovereignty herself. At Tara the high king's installation included the rite called feis Temrach, the spending of the night with Tara. Tales of a king's love-making with the goddess run wide in Ireland and far past it. The story carries its own folk-motif number, D732. Chaucer's Wife of Bath's Tale belongs to the same stock. The usual steps run as follows. The hero meets an ugly hag who asks him for his body. Her loathsomeness repels him at first, and it may picture the burdens of kingship and of age. He relents at last and lies with her. The morning after, a beautiful maiden stands in the hag's place. In Irish tellings the hag sometimes beats the hero with a riddle beyond his answer. She sets him a geis beyond his strength, and failing that he must pay with the union itself. Proinsias Mac Cana held the Irish sovereignty story political by nature, never far from the propaganda of an interested dynasty. In the Echtra of Eochaid Mugmedón's sons, Niall of the Nine Hostages lies with the loathly hag Flaithius. That union seizes power for him ahead of his half-brothers. Lugaid Laigde wins his precedence the same way, with a hideous sorceress who is Sovereignty in disguise. Conn Cétchathach meets her in the tale called the Phantom's Frenzy. After he leaves Tara he finds an otherworldly hall. Its ridge-pole is of white gold. Lug Lámfhota sits on a throne as the image of sacral kingship. On a crystal chair nearby sits his beautiful consort. The tale leaves her name unspoken. Commentators read her as the goddess Ériu. She asks her question about the red ale. Who should serve it? The words pun on ale and on sovereignty. Lug answers by naming all of Conn's successors in the kingship. The contact stays symbolic here. Conn receives his drink in a cup of gold. Other tales make the meaning plain. Many other women of early Irish literature carry the same tie, often bound to a specific territory. Mór Muman embodies Munster's sovereignty. Medb Lethderg personifies Tara's. Macha, Medb, and the Mórrígan all hold sovereignty associations in the older literature. The foremost sovereignty figure of later and oral tradition is the Cailleach Bhéire. The colour blue implies sovereignty, doubled in the name Gormfhlaith, blue sovereignty. Branwen is the sole Welsh figure with sovereignty affiliations.
```
