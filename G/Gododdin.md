``` tab [group1:Info]
::: magazinestyle
The Gododdin is one of the oldest poems in the Welsh language. The set is a body of laments for dead warriors. The verses tell of a battle at Catraeth in the north of Britain. A war leader gathered his companions and rode out. They met an English host that outnumbered them. Nearly all of the men died in that battle. The verses give a roll of the fallen one by one. For each hero they offer praise and sorrow. The work stands as a central Welsh war memorial. The men who went to Catraeth left a fame that lasted long in story.

The writing survives in a single named medieval book. The volume is the Book of Aneirin, also called Llyfr Aneirin. It dates to the thirteenth century. Scholars divide the verses into three parts. The parts are marked Text A, Text B1, and Text B2. Text B2 holds the oldest shape of the verse. The earliest layer of the poem may reach back to the age around the year 600. The lines survive with gaps and copyist guesses. Marginal notes stand beside many of the readings.

The name Gododdin comes from an older form. The Roman age knew a tribe called the Otadini. Their land ran from north of the Forth down to the Wear. The southern border touched the old kingdom of Bernicia. The land took in the great hilltop of Anderson. That hill stood as a heavy fort in Roman days. A hoard of late Roman silver was found on its slope. Some read the silver as pay for service to the Romans. Others read it as loot taken in a raid. The hill was left empty in the fifth century. A district called Manara Lingoododdin appears by the Fort. That district held Stirling and the home of Cunedda. Cunedda and his sons founded the first line of the kingdom of Gwynedd.

Traditional account holds the bard Aneirin as the poet of the set. He belongs to the early order of the Cynfeirdd. A Welsh source, the reciter's list of the Five Poets, places him early. The poem names the place of the great feast. For a whole year the warriors shared wine and mead. The feast was set at the hill fort Din Eidyn. Din Eidyn is the old name for Edinburgh. The same name still crowns the city. The feast gave each man a debt to his lord. He paid that debt with his courage and his life. To pay the mead came to mean to die in arms. The hero traded his young life for lasting fame.

The work holds a leading place in Celtic studies. It is a large body of the oldest Welsh verse, over one thousand lines. It records people and events that are otherwise lost. It speaks of the time when England and Wales took shape. It gives a steady vision of the heroic spirit of war. The poem grew into a literary classic in Wales. It cast echoes into the court poets of the Middle Ages. Modern soldiers and poets have turned its words to their own dead. The Gododdin keeps its strength as a source for old poetry.
```
