``` tab [group1:Info]
::: magazinestyle
The Gododdin is one of the oldest poems in the Welsh language. The poem tells of a battle at Catraeth in the north of Britain. A war leader gathered his warriors and led them against the English host. Almost all of those warriors fell in the fighting. The verses mark the names and praise of the fallen men one by one. The surviving heroes of the Gododdin built a fame that lasted in story for centuries. Traditional Welsh lore names the bard Aneirin as the poet who composed the lines. The same Aneirin appears in the early list of the five great poets, the Cynfeirdd. Scholars date the poem to around the year 600, in the age of the northern Britons. The fallen men came from the lands of the Votadini, a Roman-era British people of the Firth region. The poem survived in a single Old Welsh book known as the Gododdin, saved by later copies and notes. The one surviving book holds the text with gaps and guessing among the lines. The oldest layer of the poem may be older than the earliest Welsh prose. The work stands as a central source for the study of early Welsh poetry and war memory. Its tone is lament and honor joined, and it gives a living picture of a host that went to die.

:::
```
