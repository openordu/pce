``` tab [group1:Info]
::: magazinestyle
The goat was a symbolic animal for the Celts. Evidence shows the goat was sacred and useful for sacrifice. A curse kept goats away from the abbey at Kildare. That site was first a place of Celtic worship. The goat may stand for the male force. Women held the sanctuary, so men could not enter. The horned god Cernunnos had goat's legs but stag's horns. The male goat is famed for its sexuality. Christian art shows the Devil as goat-footed. It does this to mock the male sexual force. The goat of Celtic lands is the genus Capra, a horned, bearded, cloven-hoofed mammal. It often stands for fertility in older tales. Later readings turn that fertility into sexual force and aggression. Gods with goat horns show up in Gaul and Britain. A Gaulish Mercury stands beside a goat in the carvings at the Glanum sanctuary in Provence. Cemunnus, the antlered god, carries goat legs and rides a goat in some pictures. In the 1970s a small bronze carving of a horned beast came to light in south-west Scotland. Plenty of folkloric terrors are fully or partly goat. The Irish bocanách belongs to that company. The Manx goayr heddagh belongs to it. The Scottish glaistig, half-woman, belongs to it. The ùruisg, half-man, belongs to it. The monstrous goat-head people called Gaborchend or Goborchind in Irish folklore may descend from the Fomorians.

:::
```
