``` tab [group1:Info]
::: magazinestyle
Medieval Wales kept a body of teaching written for the training of its poets. Called the Gramadegau’r Penceirddiaid, this text in English bears the name grammars of the chief bards. It is written in Middle Welsh. The work joins several short studies, each aimed at some part of the poetic craft. These sections cover spelling and the sounds of letters. They treat the syllables and the double vowels of the language. Word classes and the ordering of sentences earn their own attention too. The writer then sets out the proper use of praise and ornament. A poet learns how he should carry himself. The older metres of song are listed and explained. Faults in making verse are shown so the learner may avoid them. Triads that gather teachings on metre close the book. The priest called Einion Offeiriad put this whole together about 1320. Within a decade the cleric Dafydd Ddu of Hiraddug wrote a bolder copy of the plan. His survey survives in a manuscript far older than the first copy of Einion's own version. By the middle of the fifteenth century the poet Dafydd ab Edmwnd had reworked the ruling metres. His skill touched the echoing craft named cynghanedd. Around 1570 Simwnt Fychan compiled his Pum Llyfr Cerddwriaeth, five treatises on the poet's craft. Each writer in turn left a lasting mark on what later readers knew of Welsh verse.

:::
```
