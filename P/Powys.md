``` tab [group1:Info]
::: magazinestyle
Powys is a county in eastern and central Wales. It takes its name from a medieval kingdom of the same lands. The name goes back to Latin pagenses, the land of the country-dwellers. The kingdom grew out of the lands of the ancient Cornovii. Its heartland lay in the valley of the upper Severn and its tributaries. The royal power moved between centres such as Mathrafal and Meifod. Powys reached its height under King Madog ap Maredudd, who reigned from 1138 to 1160. In those years the borders ran north to Pulford near Chester and east over the upper Severn. The bards praised the kingdom for its welcome to minstrels. The poetry of Llywarch Hen in the 9th and 10th centuries calls it the Garden of Wales. The kingdom faced the Mercian frontier to the east, marked by Clawdd Offa. Its rulers descended from a line the chronicles call the Cadelling. Powys also felt pressure from Gwynedd to the north. In time the old kingdom was absorbed into the broader Welsh political world.

Evidence of the Powys dialect survives in the Second Branch. The wording of that branch preserves regional forms. Rhonabwy's dream tale, Breuddwyd Rhonabwy, takes its setting from Powys. The scribe composed that text after the kingdom had ceased to exist. A castle beside Welshpool also bears the name.

The modern county of Powys carries on the name. Modern Powys dates to 1974, when Montgomery, Radnor, and Brecknock merged. The new county includes much land that never belonged to the medieval kingdom. It is the most thinly populated county in modern Wales. It holds Brecon, Builth Wells, Hay-on-Wye, Newtown, and Welshpool. The Brecon Beacons National Park lies in its south. The heroic tone of the regional stories led Alwyn and Brinley Rees in 1961 to link Powys with Ulster.

:::
```
