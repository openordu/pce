``` tab [group1:Info]
::: magazinestyle
The pooka is an Irish folkloric figure. The being is a shape-shifting spirit with varied animal forms. It often appears as a white horse, a black dog, or a goat that leaps tall fences. In Wales the creature is called pwca. In England the same being is pouke or puck. The name may derive from the Old Norse word Puki. The Irish puck is a shape-shifter and often takes human form. A friendly pooka helps farmers who treat it well. In one story a boy befriended a pooka, and soon several worked in his barn. The pooka appears often in the Munster region. Castle Pooka near Doneraile is said to be haunted. The pooka also stars in the novel At Swim-Two-Birds by Flann O'Brien. In Croker's 1825-7 tales the pooka is hostile and demonic. It ruins blackberry crops and appears as a horse. That horse gives unsuspecting travellers a wild ride. Lady Wilde's 1887 collection Ancient Legends paints the pooka as kindly and useful, much like the Scottish brownie. Scholars often equate the pooka with the English folk figure Puck, though the Welsh pwca offers a closer Celtic cognate. Cornish bucca and Manx buggane are also related forms. The unseen rabbit in Mary Chase's 1944 Broadway comedy Harvey is named in the play as a pooka.

:::
```
