``` tab [group1:Info]
::: magazinestyle
Prophecy is the formal foretelling of the future. It tells the same truth as divination but takes shape in words, spoken aloud or sent in writing. It does not need the reading of signs nor a sacrifice. This sets it apart from the omen, which falls on a person as an unwished gift. Prophecy is a deep and lasting feature of every traditional Celtic culture and of its prose and verse. Proof of a shared old heritage lies in the common vocabulary of the region.

In the Celtic tongues the word for the wise called a druid carries a strong note of prophecy. The vates, the seer of ancient Gaul, spoke as a prophet. Their name matches the Old Irish word for a prophet and the Welsh word for an inspired poem. When a king sought the future, the druids often moved into a trance. This state let them see hidden things. In Ireland the imbas forosnai and the bull-sleep served the same end. A druid might dream a fate inside a bull's hide. Thus the craft serves at once as a sacred art and as a tool of office.

Prophecy also shaped the work of the court. Rulers turned old hints into proof of their own right to the throne, and reworked the words to suit a changing present. So the art of prophecy joined religion, fate, and rule of the realm.

:::
```
