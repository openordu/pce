``` tab [group1:Info]
::: magazinestyle
Patrick is the name of the Irish saint and missionary bishop who came to Ireland in the fifth century. He flourished in that age as evangelist and national saint of Ireland. In Irish his name takes the forms Pátraic, Pádraig, and Pádraic. Latin Patricius means well-born or patrician. Koch records him as a third-generation Christian from Roman Britain, not yet a firm believer. His family held slaves and owned a house and a farm near the town of Bannaventa. Latin was not his first language, and his broken schooling left him weak in it. When he was about sixteen, Irish raiders seized him with his father's servants and thousands of others. Six years of slavery followed. He tended livestock alone on lonely hills, and in that hardship he came to faith. A voice told him to escape, and he walked a great distance to reach a ship. He boarded only after refusing a rough rite. The crew landed in a desolate place and lost the way. They nearly starved until Patrick found food. He reached his family safely in Britain. Scholars still dispute whether he spent time in Gaul during the quiet years after. Five documents anchor his life. His own Confessio and his Epistle to Coroticus enjoy firm credit, while three memoirs grew up long after him. Later, in a vivid dream, a man named Victoricius appeared bearing many letters. He is sometimes linked with the bishop of Rouen. In that vision Patrick read the voice of the Irish people calling him home. He sailed back as missionary bishop, travelling the land and winning many people to the faith. Two works by him survive, the Confessio and the Letter to the Soldiers of Coroticus. Two dark threads run through his story. First, he faced financial suspicion. He paid the sons of Irish rulers to ride with him as a safe escort, yet he refused all offerings. Second, his writing held an apocalyptic side. In the letter he scolded raiders who had slain recent converts, abducted the women, and sold them to the Picts. He also cut off those half-hearted Christians. Muirchu's Vita, from about 690, gives him four names. It tells of service to a druid. It records time in Gaul. It also gives a night of Easter near Tara and a meeting with King Loegaire. It also names Armagh as his church. The annals give two dates for his death, near 462 and near 493. Binchy's scholarly essay marked a turning point, since it showed the later lives were often pleasing fictions. The Acallam na Senórach adds another face to his fame. There the aged saint holds sharp dialogues with the Fenian survivor Oisin. The lost pagan world, championed by Fionn mac Cumhaill, argues its case against the new faith. The colloquy paints the new faith as stern and joyless, and Patrick often loses the argument. His nickname there is Tálcend, adze-head. The name jests at his pointed mitre and at his stubborn refusal to listen to the opposing side. Tradition also gives him a bell named Finnfaidech, which orders time.
```
