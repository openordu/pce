``` tab [group1:Info]
::: magazinestyle
Pan-Celticism gathers the movements built on Celticism. This is the belief that the Celtic peoples share a common ethnic and historical heritage. The modern form grew beside the renewed use of the word Celt. It rose with Celtic romanticism and so-called Celtomania. These grew in the closing decades of the eighteenth century. In the 1820s antiquarian bodies traded letters. This happened across Scotland, Wales, and France. The Welshman Thomas Price, known as Carnhuanawc, ran the first Pan-Celtic venture. In the early 1820s he collected funds for Le Gonidec's Breton Bible. In 1864 Charles de Gaulle issued Les Celtes au dix-neuvième siècle. He argued for fresh institutions. These included a museum, a university, and a journal. All rested on Pan-Celtic ideas. The Pan-Celtic Association was created in Dublin in October 1900. It drew together enthusiasts from every Celtic country. It put out the journal Celtia from 1901. The Celtic Congress was revived in 1917. It gained fresh life in Dublin in 1947. The Celtic League was established in 1961. The Plaid Cymru leader Gwynfor Evans headed it. The League sought closer links between national movements. Later expressions include the Inter-Celtic music festivals. These began in 1971 at Killarney and Lorient. A travelling festival of Celtic cinema and television started in 1979.

:::
```
