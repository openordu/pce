``` tab [group1:Info]
::: magazinestyle
The Picts were a distinct people of the British Isles. Their Roman name, Picti or painted men, appears only in the late 3rd century AD. Roman soldiers coined the name from the tribal taste for tattooed bodies. Other Celtic peoples shared that custom. The Picts left no record of their own name for themselves. The P-Celtic forms Preteni and Pretani and the Q-Celtic forms Cruithni, Cruithnig, and Cruthin all carry the elements -r-t-n-. That root may lie at the base of the name Britain. The Picts differed from the P-Celtic Britons whom Rome conquered. Early scholars pictured them as strange arrivals of a kind with the Basques, and pinned them to northern and north-eastern Scotland. Recent scholarship rejects both notions.

Some scholars count the Picts among the earliest Celtic speakers of the islands. Others name tribes such as the Caledonii and the Maecatae among their ancestors. Kenneth H. Jackson argued in 1980 that the Picts spoke two languages. One was P-Celtic, which Gallo-Brittonic settlers carried across from the mainland. The other was a non-Indo-European tongue that took in some Celtic words. T. F. O'Rahilly cited them as early dwellers in Ireland. He held that they settled across the whole of the British Isles.

Rome divided the Picts into two groups. The Dicalydones, or Double Caledonians, lived north and south of the Atholl forest barrier. The Verturiones held lands farther south. The Picts allied with other tribes against Rome, and Hadrian's Wall rose against their raids. Custom set them apart from their neighbours. Descent ran through the female line, and only sons of the royal house could take the kingship. They left a strong artistic record in carved memorial stones and stylized crosses. The stones bear bulls, horses, serpents, ravens, and hammers. The signs may encode religious beliefs or tribal marks. Irish chronicles still name them in the 8th century. Their lasting presence lay in Scotland.

Migrants of Q-Celtic speech, the Scotti, crossed from Ireland to Dal Riada in the 6th century and earlier. That movement caused little conflict. The Irish saint Colum Cille brought the faith to the Pictish king Bridei, also called Brudei. The Northumbrians proved the more constant enemy. The Picts crushed them at Nechtansmere in AD 685. The cross-slab at Aberlemno may mark that victory. Within two hundred more years Cinad mac Ailpn, Kenneth MacAlpin, joined Picts and Gaelic invaders under one crown. That kingdom, Alba, grew into Scotland.

The union ended the Picts as a separate people. Their language died out in the ninth century. The surviving Gaelic account casts the victory as fated and God-given. Historians read that claim as the winners' story. The loss of Pictish writing points to wide suppression, not slaughter. The king-list survived because Gaelic genealogists prized it. The church kept scriptural and Easter books, now lost. Ogam answered Latin writing in the carved record.
```
