``` tab [group1:Info]
::: magazinestyle
P-Celts is the name MacKillop 2008 gives to the speakers of the p- branch of the Celtic languages, defined against the Q- branch by how each family treated the Indo-European group qu-. Speakers of the p- languages substituted p- for that sound, a shift the dictionary dates probably to the first millennium BC in central Europe, spreading westward from there. The change never reached Ireland or the Celtic districts of the Iberian peninsula, which is why the Gaelic languages kept the older pronunciation. Gaulish was largely a p- language with traces of the other branch. The split is clearest in shared roots, where Welsh pen, head, answers to Irish ceann. The modern representatives of the p- family are Welsh, Cornish, and Breton, which the dictionary also collects under the name Brythonic. When Rome invaded, the dominant speech of ancient Britain and Scotland already belonged to this branch. The entry directs readers onward to the Belgae, Builg, Caledonii, Érainn, and Picts for the peoples involved.

:::
```
