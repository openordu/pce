``` tab [group1:Info]
::: magazinestyle
Partholón, son of Sera son of Srú, led the second settlement of Ireland. It came, per Lebar Gabála, about three centuries after the Flood. Earlier texts differ. Historia Brittonum casts his people as the island's first settlers. Scél Tuáin meic Cairill names him a Greek. No native Irish name begins with the letter P, so scribes borrowed his name. MacKillop traces it probably to Bartholomaeus. Jerome and Isidore explain it as meaning one who stays the waters, a Flood survivor. The ancient name Parthia, for northern Iran, is another candidate. His biblical pedigree descends from Magog, a figure of Abraham's own day. Tradition also records a Greek prince who killed his own father, Sera, and his mother, hoping to seize their kingdom. That crime blinded him in the left eye and cursed his fortune. Writers still styled him chief of every craft, aligned with the all-skilled Lug. After seven years of wandering, he reached Ireland. He brought Dealgnaid his wife, three sons, and a wife for each son. The fuller list of sons names Er, Ferann, Feigna, Laiglinni, Orba, Rudraige, and Sláine. Three brother druids served him: Eólas, Fios, and Fochmarc. One day, during a hunt, Dealgnaid lay with the servant Todga, Irish literature's first adultery. Some texts show a furious outburst. Others keep her verse plea, that nobody should have left her beside such temptation. He fought the Fomorians in a magical battle. Plague wiped out his Partholonians. Partholón himself died near present-day Tallaght after thirty years in the island. One hundred and twenty years after his death, plague killed the last of his people there. Their chief seat occupied Mag nElta, where Dublin later rose. Later genealogies never cite his name. A shard of his persona survives as Parthanán, an agricultural demon who threshes ripe grain at harvest. He rests at Tallaght.
```
