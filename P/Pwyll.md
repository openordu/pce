``` tab [group1:Info]
::: magazinestyle
Pwyll, Prince of Dyfed, is a Welsh hero. He heads the first of the four branches, and that tale usually bears his name. The story opens with a discourtesy to Arawn, ruler of Annwfn. To pay for that slight, Pwyll agrees to take the form of Arawn for a year. He must also face the enemy Hafgan in that year. Through that year he may lie beside the wife of Arawn, yet no love may pass between them. He honours every part of the pact. He kills Hafgan in single combat, avoids the wife, and wins the title Pen Annwfn, which means head of Annwfn. Back in his own world he sees Rhiannon ride past on a white horse. When he calls to her she stops, and the two wed. Gwawl, a rival suitor, tricks him first, so the wedding waits one year. Three years into the marriage she bears a son who is stolen. A false charge of infanticide falls on her. Her sentence is seven years at the horse-block. There she must carry each visitor on her back. The child is returned and named Pryderi. Pwyll rules on from his palace at Arberth, and in time Pryderi succeeds him.
Modern critics judge the character of Pwyll harshly. They name him foolish and spineless, chiefly over his treatment of Rhiannon. His title Pen Annwfn points to origins in the supernatural, as with many Celtic heroes. The more likely root meaning of his name is wisdom. That sense links him with the Irish figures Midir of Brí Léith and Conn. In 1927 Roger Sherman Loomis traced a thread from this refusal. It fed the temptress-wife scene in the Sir Gawain poem.

:::
```
